Isomorphism theorems for groups (aa-27)

Third pass, three categories deep — rings, modules, groups — one lattice that always folds the same way.

You have proved these theorems twice already, in aa-10 for rings and aa-17 for modules. This is the third and last time, and the point of doing it again is not that the proofs are new. It is that the hypotheses are: "submodule" has become "normal subgroup", and every statement below has to earn its quotients by checking normality where the earlier versions had nothing to check. Aluffi's own summary of the section is that the other isomorphism theorems are applications of the first one.

Predict before we start. Given a surjective group homomorphism φ : G → H, what should H be isomorphic to?

Exactly as for rings and modules: collapse everything φ cannot distinguish, and what remains is H. The reason this is now a theorem with content rather than a triviality is that G/ker φ has to be a group first, which needed all of aa-26 — and it is available precisely because kernels are normal, by Proposition 11.58. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Theorem 11.68, p. 260

Why this matters for the mission: The version used silently in every homology and fundamental-group computation you will read.

25 min Segment 1 — The first theorem, and what it is good for

aa-26 built G/K for normal K and showed the projection π is surjective with kernel K. That was the last missing piece of the canonical decomposition begun at the start of §11.4, because the set G/∼ appearing there is exactly G/ker φ.

Theorem 11.67 (Canonical decomposition). Every group homomorphism φ : G → H factors as a surjective projection π, followed by an isomorphism φ̃, followed by an inclusion ι — and φ̃ is an isomorphism. — Aluffi Underground, Theorem 11.67, p. 260

The only thing left to check was that φ̃(a ker φ) := φ(a) is a homomorphism, and it is, in one line: φ̃((a ker φ)(b ker φ)) = φ̃((ab) ker φ) = φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b). — Aluffi Underground, p. 260 Note that the coset product is being used exactly as aa-26 defined it, which is why that well-definedness argument had to come first.

Theorem 11.68 (First Isomorphism Theorem). If φ : G → H is a surjective group homomorphism then H ≅ G/ker φ, via the isomorphism φ̃(gK) = φ(g). — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Theorem 11.68, p. 260

Proof. Since φ is surjective, the image in Theorem 11.67 is all of H. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 260

A two-word proof, and the entire remaining machinery of the section rests on it. Aluffi's first applications are the two quotients aa-25 and aa-26 have already made you compute.

Example 11.69. Sn/An ≅ C₂ for n ≥ 2, because An is the kernel of the sign homomorphism onto {+1, −1} ≅ C₂ and that map is surjective for n ≥ 2. Likewise D2n/Cn ≅ C₂ for n ≥ 3, with Cn the rotation subgroup — the flip-or-not homomorphism of aa-26's Example 11.61. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.69, p. 260 Aluffi adds a third: the map C2n → C₂ given by multiplication by [n]2n has kernel {[0], [2], …, [2n−2]} ≅ Cn, so C2n/Cn ≅ C₂ as well. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 261

Worked, then faded — Example 11.70. Claim: if K₁ is normal in G₁ and K₂ is normal in G₂, then K₁ × K₂ is normal in G₁ × G₂ and

(G₁ × G₂)/(K₁ × K₂) ≅ (G₁/K₁) × (G₂/K₂).

The strategy is the one you will use for every application of Theorem 11.68: do not construct the isomorphism, construct a surjection onto the right-hand side and identify its kernel.

Worked: the map G₁ × G₂ → (G₁/K₁) × (G₂/K₂) sending (g₁, g₂) to (g₁K₁, g₂K₂) is a homomorphism because the operation in a product is componentwise, and it is surjective because each projection is. Its kernel is {(g₁, g₂) : g₁K₁ = K₁ and g₂K₂ = K₂} = {(g₁, g₂) : g₁ ∈ K₁, g₂ ∈ K₂} = K₁ × K₂. Apply Theorem 11.68. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.70, p. 261

Now fade it and run the same three steps for a claim Aluffi does not spell out: show that G₁ × G₂ modulo the subgroup G₁ × {e} is isomorphic to G₂. The surjection is ⟨?⟩; it is a homomorphism because ⟨?⟩; its kernel is ⟨?⟩; conclude by ⟨?⟩.

CheckThe surjection is the second projection (g₁, g₂) ↦ g₂. It is a homomorphism because the product operation is componentwise, and it is surjective because every g₂ is hit by (e, g₂). Its kernel is {(g₁, g₂) : g₂ = e} = G₁ × {e}. Theorem 11.68 then gives (G₁ × G₂)/(G₁ × {e}) ≅ G₂. Notice that normality of G₁ × {e} never had to be checked separately — being a kernel delivers it free, by Proposition 11.58. That is the labour-saving move, and it is why "find a surjection" is almost always faster than "verify normality, build the quotient, construct a map both ways".

Example 11.71 — presentations. aa-25 ended with the free group F(A) and the observation that if A generates G there is a natural surjection φ : F(A) → G. Feed that surjection to Theorem 11.68 and you get G ≅ F(A)/ker φ: every group is isomorphic to a quotient of a free group. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.71, p. 261 Aluffi asks you to compare this with Cayley's theorem from aa-24, which says every group is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group. — Aluffi Underground §11.2, Theorem 11.29, p. 240 Two universal embeddings of every group whatsoever, one from below and one from above.

Concretely, D2n is generated by x and y subject to x² = e, yⁿ = e, xy = y⁻¹x, so D2n ≅ F({x, y})/K where K is the smallest normal subgroup containing x², yⁿ and xyxy. In general a presentation of G is a pair (R | A) with A a set and R a set of words, such that G ≅ F(A)/K for K the smallest normal subgroup containing R; a presentation of D2n is (x², yⁿ, xyxy | x, y). — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 261 Aluffi flags the cost: deciding whether two words represent the same element of G — the word problem — is undecidable in general, a result of Pyotr Novikov, though solvable for many classes of groups. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 261

Self-check 1. Why must the third relation in the presentation of D2n be written xyxy rather than xy = y⁻¹x?

R is a set of words in the alphabet, and K is the smallest normal subgroup containing them — so each relation must be phrased as "this word dies". Multiply xy = y⁻¹x on the left by x and on the right by y: x·xy·y = x·y⁻¹x·y gives... more directly, from xy = y⁻¹x we get xyxy = (y⁻¹x)(xy) = y⁻¹x²y = y⁻¹y = e. Inverses do exist in F({x, y}) — every reduced word has one — but a relation still has to be stated as an element of the kernel. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.71, p. 261

25 min Segment 2 — The second theorem, and the hypothesis it needs

The Second Isomorphism Theorem is about HK = {hk : h in H, k in K}, the set of products. For modules the analogous H + K was automatically a submodule and nobody remarked on it. For groups it is not automatic, and Aluffi stops to say so: if H and K are subgroups, HK is not necessarily a subgroup. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 262

Example 11.72 — the counterexample, again in six elements. In D6, take H = {e, x} and K = {e, xy}. Both are subgroups of order 2, since x² = e and (xy)² = xyxy = e. Their product set is

HK = {e, x, xy, y},

because x·xy = x²y = y. This is not a subgroup: it contains xy and y but not their product xy·y = xy². — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.72, p. 262

Count as a second check: a subgroup of a six-element group cannot have four elements, since 4 does not divide 6 — but that is Lagrange's theorem, which arrives in aa-28. Aluffi's argument does not need it, and deliberately exhibits the missing product instead.

The repair is exactly the hypothesis this whole chapter has been building.

Proposition 11.73. Let H, K be subgroups of G with K normal in G. Then HK is a subgroup of G, K is normal in HK, and H ∩ K is a normal subgroup of H. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Proposition 11.73, p. 262

Aluffi's proof of the first claim is the one computation worth memorising. Take a = h₁k₁ and b = h₂k₂ in HK; then

ab⁻¹ = h₁k₁k₂⁻¹h₂⁻¹ = (h₁h₂⁻¹)(h₂k₁k₂⁻¹h₂⁻¹),

and the first bracket is in H because H is a subgroup, while the second is in K because it is a conjugate of an element of K and K is normal. So ab⁻¹ is in HK, which by the subgroup criterion of aa-23 is enough. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 262 The inserted h₂⁻¹h₂ in the middle is the whole trick: it converts "k on the wrong side of h" into "a conjugate of k", and conjugates are only guaranteed to stay put when K is normal.

The remaining two claims are quick. K = eK is a subgroup of HK, normal there because aKa⁻¹ = K holds for all a in G and in particular for all a in HK. And H ∩ K is normal in H because for k in H ∩ K and a in H, the element aka⁻¹ is in H (a product of elements of H) and in K (K is normal), hence in both. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 262

Theorem 11.74 (Second Isomorphism Theorem). Let H, K be subgroups of G with K normal in G. Then H/(H ∩ K) ≅ HK/K, and the isomorphism is h(H ∩ K) ↦ hK. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Theorem 11.74, p. 262

Both quotients in that statement are legal because of Proposition 11.73 — H ∩ K is normal in H, and K is normal in HK. Aluffi is explicit that this is why the preliminary proposition had to come first, and that the argument itself is the same as the module version of aa-17, mutatis mutandis. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 263

Guided proof. Aluffi leaves one step to the reader: "Verify that φ is surjective, by adapting the proof of Theorem 8.48." Do that, then finish the theorem. The map is φ : H → HK/K with φ(h) = hK.

Three things to establish: φ is a homomorphism, φ is surjective, and ker φ = H ∩ K. Then quote Theorem 11.68.

Model answer

Homomorphism. φ(h₁h₂) = (h₁h₂)K = (h₁K)(h₂K) = φ(h₁)φ(h₂), directly from the coset product of Definition 11.65 — which is well defined because K is normal in HK.

Surjective. A general element of HK/K is the coset of some hk with h in H and k in K. But hkK = hK, since kK = K for k in K. So that coset is φ(h), and every element of HK/K is in the image.

Kernel. ker φ = {h in H : hK = K} = {h in H : h in K} = H ∩ K, which is exactly Aluffi's computation.

Conclude. Theorem 11.68 applied to the surjection φ gives H/ker φ ≅ HK/K, that is H/(H ∩ K) ≅ HK/K, with the induced map sending the coset of h to hK — the formula in the statement.

Every isomorphism theorem in this section has this shape: build one surjection, compute one kernel, quote Theorem 11.68. The creativity is entirely in choosing the surjection.

Self-check 2. Where does normality of K get used in Proposition 11.73's proof that HK is a subgroup?

HK contains e for any subgroups at all, and H being a subgroup is a hypothesis. The single place the hypothesis bites is the second bracket of ab⁻¹ = (h₁h₂⁻¹)(h₂k₁k₂⁻¹h₂⁻¹): without normality, conjugating an element of K by h₂ can land anywhere. Example 11.72 is what that failure looks like — neither {e, x} nor {e, xy} is normal in D6, and their product set has four elements and no closure. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Proposition 11.73, p. 262

25 min Segment 3 — The third theorem: one lattice, folded

Theorem 11.75 (Third Isomorphism Theorem). Let K be normal in G and let H be a subgroup of G containing K. Then H/K is a subgroup of G/K, and every subgroup of G/K arises this way. Further, H/K is normal in G/K if and only if H is normal in G, and in that case

(G/K)/(H/K) ≅ G/H. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Theorem 11.75, p. 263

Three separate statements, and the first is the one people forget. It says the subgroups of the quotient are in bijection with the subgroups of G lying above K — nothing is lost and nothing is invented. Aluffi's proof is the projection π : G → G/K in both directions: H/K = π(H) is a subgroup, and conversely for a subgroup H̄ of G/K the preimage H = π⁻¹(H̄) is a subgroup containing K with π(H) = H̄. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 263 Images and preimages of subgroups are subgroups — aa-23's Example 11.9 and Example 11.10, quoted rather than reproved.

The second and third statements are the First Isomorphism Theorem again. If H is normal and contains K, the projection G → G/H induces a surjection ρ : G/K → G/H whose kernel is H/K, so H/K is normal and Theorem 11.68 gives the displayed isomorphism. Conversely if H/K is normal, the surjection G → (G/K)/(H/K) sending g to the class of gK has kernel H, so H is normal and the same theorem applies. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 263

Below, the bijection drawn. The left column lists the subgroups of G that contain K, the right column the subgroups of G/K, joined by the correspondence H ↦ H/K; the orders on the right are the orders on the left divided by |K|. Everything is enumerated from the group itself.

You try. Before pressing anything, predict the two column heights for D8 with K its centre {e, (1 3)(2 4)}. You know |G/K| = 4, and a group of order 4 is commutative — Aluffi sets that as Exercise 11.10 — so which group of order 4 is it, and how many subgroups does that have?
What you should seeFive and five. D8 modulo its centre has order 4, and every one of its elements squares to the identity: the square of a rotation is either e or the central half-turn, and the square of a flip is e, so gZ times gZ is Z in every case. A group of order 4 with no element of order 4 is not cyclic, so it is C₂ × C₂, the Klein four-group of aa-26's Example 11.63 — which has five subgroups: the trivial one, the whole thing, and three of order 2. Correspondingly D8 has exactly five subgroups containing its centre, of orders 2, 4, 4, 4, 8. Dividing each by |K| = 2 gives 1, 2, 2, 2, 4 — the orders on the right. The two lattices are the same shape; that is the sense in which the hook's "one lattice folds the same way" is literal.

Contrast S4 with K = A4: only two subgroups above A4, matching the two subgroups of C₂. A large K collapses more, and the correspondence tracks the collapse exactly.

Self-check 3. Which subgroups of G correspond to the subgroups of G/K?

A subgroup not containing K cannot be a union of cosets of K, so it has no image-and-preimage round trip: π⁻¹(π(H)) is HK, not H. The correspondence is with the interval above K in the subgroup lattice. Normality is a separate matter handled by the theorem's second clause — H/K is normal in G/K exactly when H is normal in G — so both normal and non-normal subgroups above K appear in the bijection. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Theorem 11.75, p. 263

Self-check 4. Aluffi proves the second and third isomorphism theorems by which route?

He says it outright at the start of the section: the other isomorphism theorems are examples of applications of the first one. Theorem 11.74 uses φ : H → HK/K with kernel H ∩ K; Theorem 11.75 uses ρ : G/K → G/H with kernel H/K in one direction and G → (G/K)/(H/K) with kernel H in the other. Three theorems, one technique, and it works identically for rings in aa-10 and modules in aa-17. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 262 — Aluffi Underground §11.5, p. 263

Self-check 5. Example 11.71 says every group is a quotient of a free group. What was the earlier companion statement?

Aluffi asks for exactly that comparison. Cayley's theorem embeds G into something universal; Example 11.71 presents G as a quotient of something universal. Neither is practical — Cayley's target is astronomically large, and free groups are unwieldy with two or more generators — but between them they say that symmetric groups and free groups are each rich enough to reach everything. The third option is false: a group of odd order has no subgroup of index 2 at all. — Aluffi Underground §11.5, Example 11.71, p. 261 — Aluffi Underground §11.2, Theorem 11.29, p. 240

  1. First (Theorem 11.68), from Theorem 11.67. A surjective φ : G → H gives H ≅ G/ker φ by φ̃(gK) = φ(g). Applications: Sn/An ≅ C₂ and D2n/Cn ≅ C₂ (Example 11.69); quotients of products split componentwise (Example 11.70); every group is F(A)/ker φ, which is what a presentation records, with (x², yⁿ, xyxy | x, y) for D2n (Example 11.71).
  2. Second (Theorem 11.74). H/(H ∩ K) ≅ HK/K for K normal in G. The preliminary work is Proposition 11.73 — HK need not be a subgroup at all, as Example 11.72 shows in D6 with {e, x} and {e, xy} — and normality is used exactly at the conjugate h₂k₁k₂⁻¹h₂⁻¹. The surjection is h ↦ hK from H to HK/K, with kernel H ∩ K.
  3. Third (Theorem 11.75). Subgroups of G/K correspond exactly to subgroups of G containing K, via H ↦ H/K = π(H) and H̄ ↦ π⁻¹(H̄). Normality matches on both sides, and then (G/K)/(H/K) ≅ G/H. The surjection is ρ : G/K → G/H with kernel H/K.
The mission strip is the reason to have done this three times. Every computation of a homology group runs the First Isomorphism Theorem without announcing it — Hn is ker ∂n modulo im ∂n+1, and identifying that quotient with a known group means exhibiting a surjection and computing its kernel, which is the only technique this section contains. The Third does the same work for fundamental groups: a covering space corresponds to a subgroup of π₁, subgroups of a quotient correspond to subgroups above the kernel, and the lattice you just watched fold is the lattice of covering spaces.
