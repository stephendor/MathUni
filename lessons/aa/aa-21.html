Abelian groups as ℤ-modules; cyclic groups and orders (aa-21)

This unit has almost nothing new to prove. Abelian groups are ℤ-modules, so every theorem of the last six units already applies to them — subgroups, quotients, direct sums, all three isomorphism theorems, and the structure theorem.

What is left is to look closely at the bricks. Every finitely generated abelian group is a direct sum of cyclic groups by aa-20's Theorem 9.40, so understanding cyclic groups is understanding everything. We will count their generators, list their subgroups, define the order of an element, and find out exactly when a product of two cyclic groups is cyclic. Along the way, one genuinely new idea: the endomorphisms of an abelian group form a ring, and that observation explains where the ring axioms came from in the first place.

Predict before we start. How many elements of ℤ/12ℤ generate the whole group?

[a]₁₂ generates exactly when gcd(a, 12) = 1, and there are φ(12) = 4 such classes: 1, 5, 7, 11. The "only 1" answer confuses the group generator with the ring identity — and Aluffi's remark is that from the group's point of view 1 is not special at all: in ℤ itself both +1 and −1 generate, and n ↦ −n is a group isomorphism that is not even a ring homomorphism. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.9, pp. 207–208

Why this matters for the mission: H0 and torsion in homology are finitely generated abelian groups; this is how you read them.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Everything transfers — and two simplifications

Abelian groups face two ways. They generalise rings and modules, being defined by a subset of those axioms; and they are a special case of R-modules, namely R = ℤ. The second fact means the work is already done: the category of abelian groups, subgroups, direct sums, quotients, kernels, cokernels, the canonical decomposition and all three isomorphism theorems are aa-15 to aa-17 with R = ℤ. In particular kernel ⟺ subgroup, and you may quotient by any subgroup. And since ℤ is a Euclidean domain, aa-20's Theorem 9.40 already says every finitely generated abelian group is a direct sum of cyclic groups. — Aluffi §10.1, p. 202

Definition 10.1. The order of an abelian group A, written |A|, is the cardinality of its underlying set. — Aluffi §10.1, Definition 10.1, p. 202

A notational caveat that will bite in aa-23. With only one operation in play, it may be written + or · indifferently. In additive notation repeated application gives multiples, a + a + a + a + a = 5a; in multiplicative notation the same thing is a power, ggggg = g⁵, and the identity is written e (or 1). This text keeps + for now, and the choice carries information: + conventionally signals that the operation is commutative, while · does not. — Aluffi §10.1, p. 203

Example 10.2 — the multiplicative examples. For a commutative ring R, the units (R∗, ·) form an abelian group: multiplication is associative and commutative, 1 is the identity, and units have inverses by definition. So ℚ≠0 is an abelian group under multiplication, and for a prime p, ((ℤ/pℤ)∗, ·) is an abelian group of order p − 1. — Aluffi §10.1, Example 10.2, p. 203

Note what has been forgotten deliberately: (ℤ/5ℤ)∗ has four elements and a multiplication table, and no addition at all. The same set carried two operations as a ring; as a group it carries one.

Proposition 10.3. A function f : A → B between abelian groups is a homomorphism if and only if f(a + a′) = f(a) + f(a′) for all a, a′. The condition f(n·a) = n·f(a) is implied. — Aluffi §10.1, Proposition 10.3, p. 204

Proposition 10.4. A subset S ⊆ A is a subgroup if and only if it is nonempty and closed under subtraction. — Aluffi §10.1, Proposition 10.4, p. 204

Why both simplify. For 10.3, the ℤ-action is by multiples, so preserving addition already preserves them: f(3a) = f(a + a + a) = f(a) + f(a) + f(a) = 3f(a), and the general case is an induction — Aluffi's Exercise 10.1, and your guided proof.

For 10.4: S nonempty gives some b, then 0 = b − b ∈ S, then −b = 0 − b ∈ S, then a + b = a − (−b) ∈ S. So S is closed under addition and negation, hence under all integer multiples, hence a submodule by aa-16's Proposition 8.23. ∎ — Aluffi §10.1, p. 204

Count the shrinking checklists. Subrings needed four conditions (aa-07's Proposition 4.14); submodules needed three (Proposition 8.23); subgroups need two. Each time, a stronger ambient structure does more of the work — and here a single closure condition, subtraction, generates everything else.

Self-check 1. Which is the correct subgroup test for an abelian group?

Nonemptiness plus closure under subtraction produces 0, produces negatives, and then produces sums — so the two conditions generate the rest (Proposition 10.4). There is no 1 and no multiplication in an abelian group, so the first option is not even statable. And closure under addition alone is not enough: the positive integers are closed under + inside ℤ and are not a subgroup. — Aluffi §10.1, Proposition 10.4, p. 204

End(A) is a ring — and why the ring axioms are not arbitrary

Definition 10.5. For abelian groups A, B, Hom(A, B) is the set of homomorphisms A → B. — Aluffi §10.1, Definition 10.5, p. 205

The point is that Hom(A, B) is itself an abelian group, under (f + g)(a) := f(a) + g(a). That the sum of two homomorphisms is a homomorphism needs one line, and it uses commutativity of B:

(f + g)(a + a′) = f(a) + f(a′) + g(a) + g(a′) = f(a) + g(a) + f(a′) + g(a′),

where the middle step reorders the four terms. So the Hom-sets of this category are themselves objects of the category — a property Aluffi notes not every category has. — Aluffi §10.1, p. 205

Now take End(A) := Hom(A, A). It carries a second operation, composition, and (End(A), +, ∘) is a ring: its 0 is the trivial homomorphism and its 1 is idA. — Aluffi §10.1, p. 205

Proposition 10.6. Let M be an abelian group and R a ring. Ring homomorphisms f : R → End(M) correspond exactly to R-module structures on M, by

r·a := f(r)(a),

and the two correspondences are mutually inverse. — Aluffi §10.1, Proposition 10.6, pp. 205–206
Proof — each module axiom is one property of f. Axioms (i)–(iv) hold because (M, +) is an abelian group. Then:

(v) 1R·a = f(1R)(a) = idM(a) = a, because ring homomorphisms send 1 to 1;

(vi) (rs)·a = f(rs)(a) = (f(r) ∘ f(s))(a) = r·(s·a), because f preserves multiplication;

(vii) (r + s)·a = f(r + s)(a) = f(r)(a) + f(s)(a), because f preserves addition;

(viii) r·(a + b) = f(r)(a + b) = f(r)(a) + f(r)(b), because f(r) is itself a homomorphism.

The converse runs the same formula backwards. ∎ — Aluffi §10.1, p. 206

Line the four up: the module axioms are not a list someone chose. They are exactly the statement that scalar multiplication is a ring homomorphism into End(M), clause by clause.

Remark 10.7 — three things this explains.

What an action is. If the endomorphisms of an object form a ring, then "R acts on X" means "there is a ring homomorphism R → End(X)". An R-module is an abelian group with a ring acting on it. aa-24 will do the same with groups in place of rings, and this is the template. — Aluffi §10.1, Remark 10.7, p. 206

Why abelian group and ℤ-module are the same thing. There is exactly one ring homomorphism ℤ → End(M), because ℤ is initial among rings. So there is exactly one way to make an abelian group a ℤ-module — viewing it as one adds nothing. aa-15's Proposition 8.10 said this; here is the reason. — Aluffi §10.1, pp. 206–207

Where the ring axioms come from. End(A) is a ring for every abelian group A. So the ring axioms are the abstraction of what End(A) always satisfies — not an arbitrary invention, but something you stumble on while studying a simpler structure. Aluffi adds a corollary of the observation: End(A) has a multiplicative identity, idA, which is another reason the "right" definition of ring should demand one. — Aluffi §10.1, p. 207

Self-check 2. Why is there only one way to regard an abelian group as a ℤ-module?

By Proposition 10.6, module structures on M correspond to ring homomorphisms R → End(M). For R = ℤ there is exactly one such homomorphism — a ring homomorphism out of ℤ is forced by 1 ↦ 1 — so exactly one module structure. Being Euclidean is what gives ℤ the classification theorem, a different service; and End(M) is generally not commutative. — Aluffi §10.1, Remark 10.7, pp. 206–207

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Cyclic groups, generators, subgroups, orders

Definition 10.8. An abelian group G is cyclic if G = ⟨g⟩ for some g ∈ G. — Aluffi §10.2, Definition 10.8, p. 207

By aa-17's Example 8.45 every cyclic R-module is R/I, and ℤ is a PID, so every cyclic group is ℤ/nℤ for some n ≥ 0: one infinite cyclic group, ℤ, and exactly one of each finite order n, written Cn. In multiplicative notation Cn = ⟨g⟩ and every element is gk. — Aluffi §10.2, p. 207

Proposition 10.9. ℤ has exactly two generators, +1 and −1. And ℤ/nℤ has φ(n) generators: [a]n generates if and only if gcd(a, n) = 1, if and only if [a]n is a unit of the ring ℤ/nℤ. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.9, p. 207

Proof: if [a]n generates then k[a]n = [1]n for some k, i.e. [k][a] = [1], so [a] is a unit; conversely a unit generates, since [b] = b[1] = bk[a]. Then apply aa-06's Proposition 3.28. — Aluffi §10.2, p. 208

Proposition 10.10. (i) Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic; Cn = ⟨g⟩ has exactly one subgroup of order m for each positive m ∣ n, generated by gn/m. (ii) Every homomorphic image of a cyclic group is cyclic, and there is a surjection Cn → Cm if and only if m ∣ n. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.10, p. 208
Proof of (i), and where you have seen it before. Work in ℤ/nℤ. By the Third Isomorphism Theorem the subgroups of ℤ/nℤ correspond to the subgroups of ℤ containing nℤ; every subgroup of ℤ is dℤ, and nℤ ⊆ dℤ forces d ∣ n. So the subgroups are dℤ/nℤ, one per positive divisor. And dℤ/nℤ ≅ ℤ/(n/d)ℤ by the First Isomorphism Theorem applied to ℤ → dℤ → dℤ/nℤ, whose kernel is (n/d)ℤ. ∎

Aluffi's own note: this is Example 5.59 again — the ideals of the ring ℤ/12ℤ — arrived at from the group side. The lattice of subgroups of Cn is the divisibility lattice of n. — Aluffi §10.2, pp. 208–209; Example 10.11, p. 209

For prime powers the lattice is a single chain: C₁ ⊆ Cp ⊆ Cp² ⊆ ⋯. So g ∈ Cpk is a generator exactly when it is not in the unique largest proper subgroup Cpk−1 — from which φ(pk) = pk − pk−1. — Aluffi §10.2, p. 210

Definition 10.12. For g in an abelian group G, the order |g| is the order of the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩. — Aluffi §10.2, Definition 10.12, p. 210

Equivalently — and this is the working definition — |g| is the smallest positive n with ng = 0, or ∞ if there is none. — Aluffi §10.2, p. 210 That ∞ is the convention used throughout this module, in the problem sets as well as here. It makes Proposition 10.16 below read correctly in the infinite case too, provided "|g| divides N" is understood as "N = 0" when |g| = ∞ — which is the only N that annihilates an element of infinite order.

Below: the elements of Cn around a circle, coloured by order. Choose an element and the subgroup it generates lights up. Generators are the elements whose subgroup is everything — and there are φ(n) of them.

Proposition 10.13. For m, n > 0:   Cm × Cn ≅ Cmn if and only if gcd(m, n) = 1. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.13, p. 210
Proof. If gcd(m, n) = 1 this is the Chinese Remainder Theorem, in the form aa-20 used it. If gcd(m, n) = d ≠ 1, then for every (a, b),

(mn/d)(a, b) = ((n/d)·ma, (m/d)·nb) = (0, 0),

so every element has order at most mn/d < mn. A cyclic group of order mn needs an element of order mn, and there is none. ∎ — Aluffi §10.2, pp. 210–211

Concretely, C₄ × C₆ has 24 elements and its largest element order is 12, so it is not C₂₄ — the largest orders present are 12, and indeed C₄ × C₆ ≅ C₂ × C₁₂. This is aa-07's Example 4.2 and Example 4.3, finally with a criterion attached.

Faded example — counting elements by order in C₁₂. Use |[a]₁₂| = 12/gcd(a, 12).

Order 12: the generators, gcd(a, 12) = 1, so there are φ(12) = ⟨your step⟩ of them.

Order 1: only a = 0. Order 2: need gcd(a, 12) = 6, so a = 6 — one element.

Order 3: need gcd(a, 12) = 4, so a ∈ {4, 8} — that is φ(3) = ⟨your step⟩ elements, the generators of the unique subgroup of order 3.

Order 4: gcd = 3, a ∈ {3, 9}, that is φ(4) = 2. Order 6: gcd = 2, a ∈ {2, 10}, that is φ(6) = 2.

Total: 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 4 = ⟨your step⟩, which must equal 12 — and does, since each element has exactly one order and lies in exactly one subgroup as a generator. The general pattern is Example 10.14: Cn has φ(m) elements of order m for each m ∣ n, and none of any other order. — Aluffi §10.2, Example 10.14, p. 211

Proposition 10.16. For a ∈ A and N ∈ ℤ:   Na = 0 if and only if |a| divides N. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.16, p. 211

Proposition 10.17. Let Cn = ⟨g⟩ and let A be an abelian group. If f : Cn → A is a homomorphism then |f(g)| divides n; and conversely, for every a ∈ A whose order divides n there is a unique homomorphism f : Cn → A with f(g) = a. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.17, pp. 211–212

Proof of 10.17, second part. ℤ is free, so aa-18's Proposition 9.8 gives f̄ : ℤ → A with f̄(1) = a. Then f̄(n) = n·a = 0, because |a| ∣ n and Proposition 10.16. So nℤ ⊆ ker f̄, and the universal property of quotients — aa-10's Proposition 5.39, transplanted — induces a unique f : ℤ/nℤ → A with f([1]n) = a. ∎ — Aluffi §10.2, p. 212

Read it as a count. Homomorphisms Cn → A correspond exactly to elements of A of order dividing n. Compare aa-15's Example 8.18 (homomorphisms out of R are elements of M, no condition) and aa-18's Example 9.10 (out of ℤ/2ℤ, elements killed by 2). This is the general statement those two were instances of.

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi states Proposition 10.3 and says "You should try to give a formal proof (by induction)". Do it. Let A, B be abelian groups and f : A → B a function with f(a + a′) = f(a) + f(a′) for all a, a′. Prove that f(n·a) = n·f(a) for every n ∈ ℤ. Handle n > 0 by induction, then n = 0, then n < 0 — and say which earlier result you need for the n = 0 case.

Reveal model answer

n > 0, by induction. Base n = 1: f(1·a) = f(a) = 1·f(a). Step: assume f(n·a) = n·f(a). Then

f((n+1)·a) = f(n·a + a) = f(n·a) + f(a) = n·f(a) + f(a) = (n+1)·f(a),

using the hypothesis on f for the second equality and the induction hypothesis for the third.

n = 0. f(0·a) = f(0A), and a map preserving addition sends 0 to 0: f(0) = f(0 + 0) = f(0) + f(0), so f(0) = 0 by additive cancellation — aa-05's Proposition 3.17, valid in any abelian group since its proof used only axioms (i)–(iii). Hence f(0·a) = 0 = 0·f(a).

n < 0. Write n = −k with k > 0. From f(k·a) + f((−k)·a) = f(k·a + (−k)·a) = f(0) = 0 we get f((−k)·a) = −f(k·a) = −(k·f(a)) = (−k)·f(a).

So f(n·a) = n·f(a) for all n ∈ ℤ, and f is a homomorphism of ℤ-modules. ∎

Grade yourself on the n = 0 case. It is the one people skip, and it is not free from the inductive step — it needs f(0) = 0, which needs cancellation. Note also what the proposition depends on: that the ℤ-action is by repeated addition. Over any other ring the second condition is genuinely independent, which is why Definition 8.14 states it.

You try (solo). (a) Count the elements of each order in C₂₀, and check the counts sum to 20. (b) Use Proposition 10.10 to list all subgroups of C₂₀ and say which is contained in which. (c) Decide whether C₄ × C₅ ≅ C₂₀ and whether C₂ × C₁₀ ≅ C₂₀, giving the criterion in each case; for the one that fails, name the largest order actually attained. (d) Finally, count the homomorphisms C₆ → C₄ using Proposition 10.17 — that is, count the elements of C₄ whose order divides 6.

Self-check 3. How many subgroups does C₂₀ have, and of what orders?

Proposition 10.10(i): exactly one subgroup of order m for each positive m ∣ n. The divisors of 20 are 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20 — six subgroups. Distinct elements often generate the same subgroup: the four elements of order 5 all generate the single subgroup of order 5. Only groups of prime order have just the two trivial subgroups. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.10, p. 208

Self-check 4. Is C₄ × C₆ isomorphic to C₂₄?

Both have 24 elements, so cardinality decides nothing. Proposition 10.13 requires gcd(m, n) = 1, and here it is 2; the proof shows every element is killed by mn/d = 12, so the largest order present is 12 and no generator exists. Compare the coprime case: C₄ × C₅ ≅ C₂₀, because 4 and 5 share nothing. This is aa-07's Examples 4.2 and 4.3 with the criterion supplied. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.13, pp. 210–211

Self-check 5. How many homomorphisms are there from Cn to an abelian group A?

Proposition 10.17: f is determined by f(g), that element must have order dividing n, and every such element occurs. So Hom(Cn, A) is in bijection with { a ∈ A : |a| ∣ n }. For instance Hom(C₆, C₄) has 2 elements, since the elements of C₄ of order dividing 6 are those of order 1 or 2. There is always at least one — the trivial homomorphism, corresponding to a = 0 — matching aa-15's observation that Hom is never empty for modules. — Aluffi §10.2, Proposition 10.17, pp. 211–212

Then check yourself
  1. Nothing needs reproving. Abelian groups are ℤ-modules, so subgroups, quotients, direct sums, the three isomorphism theorems and the structure theorem all transfer. Two things simplify: preserving addition already forces preserving multiples (Proposition 10.3), and a subgroup test needs only "nonempty and closed under subtraction" (Proposition 10.4) — two conditions, where subrings needed four.
  2. End(A) is a ring, and an R-module structure on M is exactly a ring homomorphism R → End(M) (Proposition 10.6), clause for clause. Three consequences: an action is a homomorphism into an endomorphism ring; there is exactly one ℤ-module structure on an abelian group, since ℤ is initial; and the ring axioms are the abstraction of what End(A) always satisfies, so they were never arbitrary.
  3. Cyclic groups are ℤ or Cn. Cn has φ(n) generators, namely the units of the ring ℤ/nℤ (Proposition 10.9); exactly one subgroup of each order dividing n, so its subgroup lattice is the divisor lattice of n (Proposition 10.10); and φ(m) elements of order m for each m ∣ n. Cm × Cn ≅ Cmn exactly when gcd(m, n) = 1 (Proposition 10.13). Homomorphisms out of Cn are the elements of order dividing n (Proposition 10.17).
