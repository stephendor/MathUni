Groups and their category: subgroups and homomorphisms (aa-23)

There are 42 abelian groups of order 1024. There are 49,487,365,402 groups of order 1024 — or, by the count usually cited today, 49,487,365,422.

One axiom separates those two numbers. Drop commutativity from Definition 8.11 and what remains — associativity, an identity, inverses — is a group, and the theory becomes incomparably wilder. Aluffi's comparison is blunt: the classification of finite abelian groups took us one lesson; the classification of finite simple groups fills many thousands of pages by hundreds of authors over fifty years, and "is not stuff for mere mortals". Today we set up the category of groups and watch, at four separate points, exactly what stops working when gh ≠ hg.

Predict before we start. A ring homomorphism has three conditions and a module homomorphism has two. How many does a group homomorphism have?

Definition 11.2 asks only that φ(g₁g₂) = φ(g₁)φ(g₂). That φ(e) = e and φ(g−1) = φ(g)−1 then follow — the guided proof at the end. Contrast rings, where f(1) = 1 had to be imposed because aa-08 could not cancel f(1) from f(1) = f(1)f(1). In a group every element is invertible, so that cancellation is always available, and the condition comes free. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.2, p. 230

Why this matters for the mission: Fundamental groups and covering-space deck transformations are non-abelian; this is their grammar.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Three axioms, and what order now costs

Definition 11.1 (group). A group (G, ·) is a set with a binary operation such that
  1. (i) (gh)k = g(hk) for all g, h, k;
  2. (ii) there is eG ∈ G with geG = eGg = g for all g;
  3. (iii) for every g there is h with gh = hg = eG.
The order |G| is its cardinality. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.1, p. 229

This is aa-15's Definition 8.11 with axiom (iv), commutativity, deleted — and Definition 8.11 was itself axioms (i)–(iv) of aa-05's Definition 3.1, the list that opened the module. The tower now reads ring, module, abelian group, group, each step discarding requirements — and written multiplicatively, as aa-21 warned it would be. Two elements commute if gh = hg; a group in which all do is commutative, which is precisely an abelian group. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 230

What survives unchanged. The identity is unique, each inverse is unique (so g−1 is well defined), and (g−1)−1 = g — by the proofs of aa-05's Propositions 3.14 and 3.15 verbatim, since those used only associativity, an identity, and inverses. And cancellation holds:

g₁h = g₂h ⟹ g₁ = g₁(hh−1) = (g₁h)h−1 = (g₂h)h−1 = g₂,

and likewise on the left. In particular, if g ≠ e then gh ≠ h for every h. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, pp. 229–230

Note how cheap cancellation has become. In a ring it needed a non-zero-divisor (aa-06's Proposition 3.26) and often failed. In a group every element is invertible, so it always holds — and that single fact is why several later proofs are shorter here than their ring counterparts.

What order costs. Powers behave as expected: g⁵ = ggggg, g−3 = g−1g−1g−1, and gm+n = gmgn. But

(gh)² = ghgh  cannot be simplified,   and   (gh)−1 = h−1g−1,

where the switch is necessary: check (gh)(h−1g−1) = g(hh−1)g−1 = e, whereas (gh)(g−1h−1) does not collapse. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 230

And the size of the change. Aluffi's figures: 42 abelian groups of order 1024, all writable from aa-22's classification; 49,487,365,402 groups of order 1024. One axiom. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 230

A note on that second figure, since it is quoted here verbatim and Aluffi himself hedges it with "I am told". The number produced by the Besche–Eick–O'Brien enumeration of groups of order 1024, and the one usually quoted, is 49,487,365,422 — twenty more than the printed figure. Nothing in the argument turns on the last two digits; the gap between 42 and roughly 4.9 × 10¹⁰ is the point. But if you meet the number elsewhere and it disagrees with this page, the other source is probably the right one.

A notation warning you will meet in aa-25 and in Carter. Aluffi's Definition 11.32 writes D2n for the symmetry group of the regular n-gon — so the symmetries of the square are D₈, a group of order 8. Carter, and many other books, write D₄ for the same group, indexing by the number of sides. Both conventions are standard and they conflict; this module follows Aluffi, so D₈ here is the square. When you read Carter, halve the subscript. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.32, p. 242

Self-check 1. In a group, what is (gh)−1?

(gh)(h−1g−1) = g(hh−1)g−1 = gg−1 = e, and similarly on the other side; so h−1g−1 is the inverse, by uniqueness. The order reversal is not a convention — g−1h−1 genuinely fails when g and h do not commute. In an abelian group all three answers coincide, which is exactly why the reversal is easy to forget. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 230

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Homomorphisms, and the failure of Hom(G, H)

Definition 11.2. A function φ : G → H between groups is a group homomorphism if φ(g₁g₂) = φ(g₁)φ(g₂) for all g₁, g₂. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.2, p. 230

Proposition 11.3. Identities are homomorphisms, composites of homomorphisms are homomorphisms, and composition is associative — so groups and group homomorphisms form a category. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Proposition 11.3, p. 230

One family is already classified: by aa-19's Theorem 9.35 a finitely generated vector space is free, so its additive group is a direct sum of copies of (k, +) — an abelian group, hence a group, and every linear map is in particular a group homomorphism. Other examples arrive free: the map sending everything to eH is the trivial homomorphism, and every homomorphism of abelian groups is a group homomorphism — indeed for abelian A, B the set Hom(A, B) of aa-21 is exactly the set of group homomorphisms, which makes abelian groups a full subcategory of groups. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 231

Remark 11.4 — the first casualty of non-commutativity. aa-21 found that Hom(A, B) is itself an abelian group, under (f + g)(a) = f(a) + g(a). Try the same for groups: define (φψ)(g) := φ(g)ψ(g). Is it a homomorphism? Compute both sides:

(φψ)(g₁g₂) = φ(g₁)φ(g₂)ψ(g₁)ψ(g₂),

(φψ)(g₁)(φψ)(g₂) = φ(g₁)ψ(g₁)φ(g₂)ψ(g₂).

The two differ in the middle: they agree exactly when φ(g₂)ψ(g₁) = ψ(g₁)φ(g₂), and nothing supplies that. In the abelian case, axiom (iv) did. So Hom(G, H) is in general not a group — it is "nothing more than a set" (with one distinguished element, the trivial homomorphism, making it a pointed set). — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Remark 11.4, p. 231

This is a real loss. aa-21's Proposition 10.6 built the whole notion of a module out of End(A) being a ring; that construction has no group-theoretic analogue here.

Definition 11.5. φ : G → H is an isomorphism if there is a group homomorphism ψ : H → G with ψ ∘ φ = idG and φ ∘ ψ = idH — equivalently, if φ is a bijection. G ≅ H if such a φ exists, and ≅ is an equivalence relation. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.5, p. 231

The equivalence of the two clauses is the same argument as aa-08's Proposition 4.30 and aa-15's guided proof — Aluffi sets it as a review exercise, and the point is that you have now proved it three times in three categories from the same template. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 231

Self-check 2. Is Hom(G, H) a group under (φψ)(g) = φ(g)ψ(g)?

Expanding (φψ)(g₁g₂) and (φψ)(g₁)(φψ)(g₂) gives two products differing by the swap of φ(g₂) and ψ(g₁), and nothing forces them to commute. For abelian targets axiom (iv) did, which is why Hom(A, B) is an abelian group. Finiteness is irrelevant — the obstruction is one transposition inside a product of four terms. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Remark 11.4, p. 231

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Subgroups, generation, and products

Definition 11.6. A subset H ⊆ G that is itself a group is a subgroup if the inclusion H → G is a group homomorphism — i.e. if H's operation is the restriction of G's. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.6, p. 232
Proposition 11.7. A nonempty subset H ⊆ G is a subgroup if and only if gh−1 ∈ H for all g, h ∈ H. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Proposition 11.7, p. 232
Proof. Take h ∈ H. Setting g = h gives e = hh−1 ∈ H. Setting g = e gives h−1 = eh−1 ∈ H. Then for g, h ∈ H we have h−1 ∈ H, so gh = g(h−1)−1 ∈ H. So H contains e, is closed under inverses and under the operation — hence a group under the restricted operation, associativity being inherited. ∎ — Aluffi Underground §11.1, p. 232

One condition. Subrings needed four checks, submodules three, subgroups of abelian groups two (aa-21's Proposition 10.4: nonempty, closed under subtraction), and here one — gh−1 ∈ H, which is the multiplicative spelling of the same thing. The checklist has been shrinking all module long, and this is the floor.

The standard constructions, all by Proposition 11.7.

11.8. Any intersection of subgroups — finite or not — is a subgroup. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Example 11.8, p. 232

11.9. Images of subgroups are subgroups; in particular im φ ≤ G′. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Example 11.9, p. 232

11.10. Preimages of subgroups are subgroups; in particular ker φ = φ−1(e) is a subgroup of G. Kernels return in aa-26. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Example 11.10, p. 232

Definition 11.11. For S ⊆ G, ⟨S⟩ is the smallest subgroup containing S — which exists, being the intersection of all subgroups that contain S. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.11, p. 232

Remark 11.12 — the second casualty, and it is severe. For an abelian group, ⟨a₁, …, an⟩ is the set of combinations m₁a₁ + ⋯ + mnan — a clean normal form, which is what made aa-18's matrices possible. The multiplicative analogue would be the products g₁m₁⋯gnmn, and these do not form a group: unless g₁ and g₂ commute there is no reason g₁g₂g₁g₂ should equal g₁m₁g₂m₂ for any exponents.

What ⟨g₁, …, gn⟩ actually is: all products of the generators and their inverses, in any order — words such as

g₃g₂−1g₁g₂²g₃−1g₁−3,

with in general no way to simplify further. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Remark 11.12, p. 233

Only the single-generator case escapes: ⟨g⟩ is the set of powers gi, exactly as in aa-21's Definition 10.8. That is why cyclic groups remain easy and everything else does not — and why aa-25's free groups, which are all words and no relations, are the wild end of the subject.

Definition 11.13 — products, and the third casualty. G × H with componentwise operation is a group, and the projections πG, πH make it satisfy the universal property of products, exactly as for modules. But it is not a coproduct: aa-16's injections and their universal property fail, and if you trace the attempted verification you find, again, a step needing commutativity. A coproduct of groups does exist — the free product G ∗ H — and is much harder to handle. Aluffi's notation records the difference: ⊕ for modules, × for groups. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.13, p. 233

Example 11.16 — the example to hold on to. The invertible elements of a possibly noncommutative ring form a group. For 2 × 2 real matrices this is the general linear group

GL₂(ℝ) = { (a b / c d) : ad − bc ≠ 0 },

and GLn(ℝ) in general. Every ring, ideal and module you have met is a group, but they are all abelian and so unrepresentative; GLn is what a group actually looks like. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Examples 11.15 and 11.16, p. 234

Carter's way of seeing the same thing. Carter draws a group as a Cayley diagram — one node per element, one coloured arrow per generator — and defines a subgroup as one group "completely contained in another", written H < G, with the orbit of an element as the basic example: in S₃ the orbit {e, r, r²} is a copy of C₃. — Carter §6.2, Definition 6.2, p. 99

Some subgroups are visually obvious and some are not, so Carter's §6.3 gives a method: any subgroup can be revealed by regenerating the diagram using generators that include the subgroup's generators, then relaying out the nodes. His worked case is C₆ = ⟨2, 3⟩, where neither 2 nor 3 generates alone; drawing the diagram with those two arrow colours makes the subgroups ⟨2⟩ and ⟨3⟩ visible at once. — Carter §6.3, p. 101; §6.2, pp. 99–100

And in §8.1 he identifies what that containment is: none of the elements 0, 1, 2 of C₃ are literally in S₃, so "C₃ < S₃" means a structure-preserving correspondence 0 ↦ e, 1 ↦ r, 2 ↦ r² — an injective homomorphism, or embedding. Aluffi Underground's Definition 11.6 and Carter's picture are the same statement. — Carter §8.1, p. 157

Faded example — using Proposition 11.7 in S₃. Let G = S₃ = {e, r, r², f, rf, r²f} with r³ = e, f² = e. Is H = {e, r, r²} a subgroup?

H is nonempty, so by Proposition 11.7 it is enough that gh−1 ∈ H for all g, h ∈ H. The inverses inside H are e−1 = e, r−1 = ⟨your step: which power of r?⟩, and (r²)−1 = r.

So every product gh−1 is a product of powers of r, hence a power of r, hence in H — because r³ = ⟨your step⟩ makes the powers close up after three steps. So H ≤ G. ✓

Now try K = {e, f, r}. Take g = f and h = r: then gh−1 = fr², and fr² is ⟨your step: in K or not?⟩ — the six elements of S₃ are e, r, r², f, rf, r²f, and fr² is a reflection different from f. So K is not a subgroup.

The moral matches Carter's: subgroups are not arbitrary subsets of the right size. {e, f, r} has three elements, and 3 divides 6, and it is still not a subgroup — a point aa-28's Lagrange theorem will sharpen from the other direction.

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi asserts that a group homomorphism sends the identity to the identity and inverses to inverses, and defers it to Exercise 11.2. Prove it. Let φ : G → H satisfy φ(g₁g₂) = φ(g₁)φ(g₂) for all g₁, g₂. Show that (a) φ(eG) = eH and (b) φ(g−1) = φ(g)−1 for every g. Then answer the question that makes this worth doing: why did the analogous condition have to be an axiom for rings?

Reveal model answer

(a) φ(eG) = φ(eGeG) = φ(eG)φ(eG). Now cancel φ(eG) — legal because every element of H is invertible, so cancellation holds in H — leaving eH = φ(eG).

(b) φ(g)φ(g−1) = φ(gg−1) = φ(eG) = eH, and symmetrically φ(g−1)φ(g) = eH. So φ(g−1) satisfies the defining property of the inverse of φ(g), and inverses are unique, so φ(g−1) = φ(g)−1. ∎

Why rings were different. For a ring homomorphism the same first move gives f(1) = f(1)f(1) — but cancelling f(1) requires it to be a non-zero-divisor, and in a general ring it need not be. aa-08 flagged exactly this, with ι(a) = (a, 0) as the witness: a map respecting both operations that sends 1 to a non-identity. So f(1) = 1 must be imposed. In a group, invertibility is universal, cancellation is free, and the condition is a theorem.

Grade yourself on naming cancellation as the step. If your proof of (a) said "clearly φ(e) = e", it has not identified what makes groups different from rings — which is the whole content of the exercise.

You try (solo). (a) Prove Proposition 11.3: identities and composites of group homomorphisms are group homomorphisms. It is one line each, and the contrast worth noticing is how much shorter it is than aa-08's Proposition 4.27 for rings. (b) Show that the six elements of S₃ have orders 1, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, and use Proposition 11.7 to list all subgroups of S₃. (There are six.) (c) Which of them are images ⟨g⟩ of a single element, and which is not? (d) Finally, check by hand that S₃ is not abelian by exhibiting two elements with rf ≠ fr, and confirm your answer is consistent with the Cayley diagram above.

Self-check 3. What is the subgroup of G generated by two non-commuting elements g₁, g₂?

The products g₁mg₂n are not closed under multiplication when the generators fail to commute — g₁g₂g₁g₂ generally cannot be rewritten in that shape. So ⟨g₁, g₂⟩ is all words in the generators and their inverses, usually with no simplification available (Remark 11.12). This is precisely the normal form that abelian groups had and groups do not, and it is why the matrix methods of aa-18 stop here. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Remark 11.12, p. 233

Self-check 4. Carter says "C₃ < S₃". What does that mean precisely?

Carter is explicit that none of the elements 0, 1, 2 are literally in S₃ — the structure of C₃ shows up even though the elements are different. The precise content is a structure-preserving correspondence, an embedding, which is what §8.1 introduces homomorphisms in order to say. The orders are 3 and 6, not equal. This is the same statement as Definition 11.6. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Definition 11.6, p. 232 — Carter §8.1, p. 157

Self-check 5. How many conditions does the subgroup test of Proposition 11.7 require?

Nonemptiness gives some h; then gh−1 with g = h gives e, with g = e gives h−1, and then gh = g(h−1)−1 gives closure. Subrings needed 0, 1, closure and negatives (Proposition 4.14); this needs one closure condition. And closure under the operation alone is not enough for infinite groups — the positive integers under addition inside ℤ are the standard witness. — Aluffi Underground §11.1, Proposition 11.7, p. 232

Then check yourself
  1. A group is Definition 8.11 minus commutativity (Definition 11.1). Uniqueness of e and of inverses, and cancellation, survive verbatim — cancellation now being free, since every element is invertible. What changes is order: (gh)−1 = h−1g−1, and (gh)² does not simplify. The scale of the change: 42 abelian groups of order 1024 against about 4.9 × 10¹⁰ groups — 49,487,365,402 as Aluffi prints it, 49,487,365,422 by the standard enumeration.
  2. A group homomorphism has one condition (Definition 11.2); φ(e) = e and φ(g−1) = φ(g)−1 follow by cancellation, where the ring analogue had to be imposed. Groups and homomorphisms form a category (Proposition 11.3), and isomorphism is defined and behaves as in every previous chapter.
  3. Four things break. Hom(G, H) is no longer a group (Remark 11.4) — the abelian construction needs one transposition it cannot make. ⟨g₁, …, gn⟩ has no normal form, only words (Remark 11.12). G × H is a product but not a coproduct (Definition 11.13). And, most bluntly, the classification is out of reach. What survives: the subgroup test, now down to one condition (Proposition 11.7), images and preimages of subgroups, and kernels.
Carter's picture and Aluffi's definition are the same object: a subgroup is a sub-diagram of a Cayley diagram, and "C₃ < S₃" means an injective homomorphism, not a containment of elements. That reading — structure, not elements — is exactly what the fundamental group of a space will require.
