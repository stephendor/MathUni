Prime and maximal ideals; primes vs irreducibles (aa-11)

When you drew a parabola in a calculus class, you were drawing a set of maximal ideals of a polynomial ring. You just did not know it.

aa-10 ended on a dictionary: properties of the quotient R/I are properties of the ideal I. Today we name the two entries that matter. An ideal is prime when the quotient is an integral domain and maximal when the quotient is a field — and then everything ℤ taught you gets re-read. "Prime integer" becomes "prime ideal". "Irreducible" and "prime" stop being the same word, and the ring where they come apart turns out to be the coordinate ring of a curve with a cusp at the origin.

Predict before we start. Every maximal ideal is prime. Is every prime ideal maximal?

ℤ/(0) ≅ ℤ is an integral domain but not a field, so (0) is prime and not maximal. In ℤ[x], the quotient by (x) is ℤ — same verdict — and here the failure is visible as room to grow: (x) ⊊ (2, x) ⊊ ℤ[x]. Aluffi's point in §6.2 is that ℤ is unusually well behaved: there, and not in general, maximal and nonzero prime coincide. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.12, p. 109

Why this matters for the mission: Primes and maximals are the points of the spectrum — algebra's first bridge back to space.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Two definitions, and what they say about elements

The motivating observations are already on the table. ℤ/nℤ is a field exactly when n is irreducible. And ℝ[x]/(x² − 1) ≅ ℝ × ℝ has zero-divisors precisely because x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1) factors — that is what let the Chinese Remainder Theorem apply. In both cases a feature of the quotient reflects a feature of the ideal. — Aluffi §6.1, p. 106

Definition 6.1 (prime ideal). Let R be a commutative ring and I an ideal. Then I is prime if R/I is an integral domain. — Aluffi §6.1, Definition 6.1, p. 106

Definition 6.2 (maximal ideal). I is maximal if R/I is a field. — Aluffi §6.1, Definition 6.2, p. 106

Both are stated only for commutative rings, because that is where they are used — commutative algebra, number theory, algebraic geometry. — Aluffi §6.1, p. 106

Proposition 6.3. Maximal ideals are prime. — Aluffi §6.1, Proposition 6.3, p. 106

Proof: fields are integral domains, which is aa-06's Proposition 3.31. One line, and it inherits everything — including the fact that the converse fails, since ℤ is a domain and not a field.

Theorem 6.4. An ideal I of a commutative ring R is prime if and only if I ≠ (1) and

for all a, b ∈ R:   ab ∈ I ⟹ a ∈ I or b ∈ I.   (6.1)

— Aluffi §6.1, Theorem 6.4, p. 107
Proof. (⟹) If R/I is a domain then 0 ≠ 1 there, so R/I is nontrivial and I ≠ (1). If ab ∈ I then (a + I)(b + I) = ab + I = 0 in R/I, so by Definition 3.23 one factor is 0, i.e. a ∈ I or b ∈ I.

(⟸) R/I is commutative because R is, and nontrivial because I ≠ (1). If (a + I)(b + I) = 0 then ab ∈ I, so a ∈ I or b ∈ I by (6.1), so a + I = 0 or b + I = 0. That is Definition 3.23. ∎ — Aluffi §6.1, p. 107

The clause I ≠ (1) is not decoration. Take I = R: then ab ∈ I always, and a ∈ I always, so condition (6.1) holds vacuously — yet R/R is the trivial ring, which is not a domain. Without the clause the whole ring would count as prime. This is aa-06's "0 ≠ 1" in Definition 3.23, appearing in its ideal-theoretic costume.

What the definition recovers.

Example 6.5. A commutative ring R is an integral domain if and only if (0) is prime — because R/(0) ≅ R. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.5, p. 107

Example 6.6 — the point of the whole definition. In ℤ, Theorem 6.4 says (p) is prime exactly when p ≠ ±1 and

for all b, c ∈ ℤ:   p ∣ bc ⟹ p ∣ b or p ∣ c.

That is aa-02's Definition 1.19, word for word. So "prime ideal" is a direct generalisation of "prime integer", and aa-04's Theorem 2.15 — proved by hand, at some cost — is now a very particular case of Theorem 6.4. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.6, p. 107; §1.4, Definition 1.19, p. 14

Aluffi also settles the convention question here: everyone agrees (0) is a prime ideal of ℤ, so declining to call 0 a prime integer would clash. That is why Definition 1.19 was written the way it was, three chapters ago.

Example 6.7. (x) is prime in ℤ[x] because ℤ[x]/(x) ≅ ℤ is a domain; and in ℤ[x, y] both (y) and (x, y) are prime, since the quotients are ℤ[x] and ℤ. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.7, p. 107

Self-check 1. Why does Theorem 6.4 need the clause I ≠ (1)?

If I = R, then ab ∈ I for all a, b, and a ∈ I for all a — so "ab ∈ I ⟹ a ∈ I or b ∈ I" holds trivially. But R/R is the one-element ring, where 0 = 1, and Definition 3.23 excludes it from being a domain. The clause is the ideal-side spelling of the nontriviality requirement, exactly as aa-06 needed 0 ≠ 1. — Aluffi §6.1, Theorem 6.4, p. 107; §3.3, Definition 3.23, p. 53

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Maximal means no room in between — and Spec

Theorem 6.9. An ideal I of a commutative ring R is maximal if and only if I ≠ (1) and there is no ideal J with I ⊊ J ⊊ R. — Aluffi §6.1, Theorem 6.9, p. 108
Proof — an application of the Third Isomorphism Theorem. Recall a commutative ring is a field exactly when its only ideals are (0) and (1).

(⟹, contrapositive) Suppose some J has I ⊊ J ⊊ R. By aa-10's Theorem 5.57, J/I is an ideal of R/I with (0) ⊊ J/I ⊊ (1). So R/I has an ideal other than (0) and (1) and is not a field: I is not maximal.

(⟸, contrapositive) If R/I is not a field it has an ideal J̄ with (0) ⊊ J̄ ⊊ (1). By Theorem 5.57 that J̄ is J/I for an ideal J containing I, and I ⊊ J ⊊ R. So the stated condition fails. ∎ — Aluffi §6.1, p. 108

So the name is honest: maximal ideals are exactly the ideals that cannot be enlarged without becoming the whole ring. And note the proof is entirely the ideal correspondence — no element is ever chosen.

The verdicts.

Example 6.10. R is a field ⟺ (0) is maximal ⟺ R has exactly two ideals. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.10, p. 108

Example 6.11 — in ℤ, maximal ⟺ nonzero prime. If I is maximal it is nonzero, since ℤ/(0) ≅ ℤ is not a field, and prime by Proposition 6.3. Conversely if I is a nonzero prime, then I = nℤ with n ≠ 0, so ℤ/I is finite and is a domain; and a finite integral domain is a field by aa-06's Proposition 3.33. So I is maximal. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.11, p. 108

Where the specialness of ℤ enters: at finiteness. Proposition 3.33 is doing the work, and it is exactly the step that has no analogue in ℤ[x], where ℤ[x]/(x) ≅ ℤ is an infinite domain. Combined with Example 6.6, this recovers "ℤ/pℤ is a field ⟺ p is irreducible", i.e. Theorem 2.15 again. — Aluffi §6.1, p. 109

Example 6.13. If k is a field, (x) is maximal in k[x] but not in k[x, y]; there (x, y) is maximal, and (x) ⊊ (x, y) ⊊ k[x, y]. Adding an indeterminate adds room. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.13, p. 109

Faded example — Example 6.12: (2, x) is maximal in ℤ[x].

By Definition 6.2 we must show the quotient is a field. aa-10's Example 5.58 computed ℤ[x]/(2, x) ≅ ⟨your step: which ring?⟩, and that ring is a field because ⟨your step: which theorem, about ℤ/nℤ?⟩. So (2, x) is maximal.

By contrast (x) is prime but not maximal: ℤ[x]/(x) ≅ ℤ, an integral domain that is ⟨your step: what is it not?⟩. Theorem 6.9 makes the failure visible — there is an ideal in between, namely ⟨your step⟩, and

(x) ⊊ (2, x) ⊊ ℤ[x].

Both inclusions are strict: 2 ∈ (2, x) has no factor of x, and 1 ∉ (2, x) because every element of (2, x) has even constant term. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.12, p. 109

You try (solo). Verify Example 6.13 in full for k a field. (a) Show k[x]/(x) ≅ k, so (x) is maximal in k[x]. (b) Show k[x, y]/(x) ≅ k[y], which is a domain but not a field — so (x) is prime and not maximal there. (c) Show k[x, y]/(x, y) ≅ k, so (x, y) is maximal, and exhibit the chain (x) ⊊ (x, y) ⊊ k[x, y] with both inclusions strict. Every quotient here is an application of aa-10's First Isomorphism Theorem to an evaluation map; you should not need to manipulate a single coset.

The bridge back to space. For a commutative ring R, the spectrum Spec(R) is the set of prime ideals of R, and it is regarded as a space whose ordinary points are the maximal ideals. In algebraic geometry the parabola y = x² in ℂ² corresponds to the prime ideal (y − x²) of ℂ[x, y] — prime because ℂ[x, y]/(y − x²) ≅ ℂ[x] is a domain — and points of the parabola correspond to maximal ideals containing it. The origin lies on the parabola, and correspondingly (y − x²) ⊊ (x, y). Taking V(I) to be the primes containing I gives the closed sets of a topology on Spec(R), the Zariski topology. — Aluffi §6.1, Examples 6.8 and 6.14 and the discussion of Spec, pp. 108–109

Self-check 2. In ℤ, why is every nonzero prime ideal maximal?

A nonzero prime ideal is nℤ with n ≠ 0, so the quotient has n elements and is a domain; Proposition 3.33 upgrades that to a field. Finiteness is the whole of it. Being principal is true but irrelevant — ℤ[x]'s ideal (x) is principal, prime, and not maximal, because ℤ[x]/(x) ≅ ℤ is infinite. — Aluffi §6.1, Example 6.11, p. 108; §3.3, Proposition 3.33, p. 56

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Prime elements, irreducible elements, and where they part

ℤ is privileged, and Aluffi's aim for the chapter is to measure how far its good behaviour extends. First, generalise the vocabulary of Chapter 1 to any commutative ring. — Aluffi §6.2, p. 110

Definition 6.15 (divides). For a, b in a commutative ring R, b ∣ a means a ∈ (b), i.e. a = bc for some c ∈ R. — Aluffi §6.2, Definition 6.15, p. 110

Definition 6.16 (gcd). For a, b in an integral domain R, d is a greatest common divisor of a and b if (d) is the smallest principal ideal containing both a and b. — Aluffi §6.2, Definition 6.16, p. 110

Unwound, that is exactly aa-02's Definition 1.5: (d) ∋ a, b says d is a common divisor, and minimality says every common divisor divides d. Note the article: if d is a gcd then so is du for any unit u, so there is no "the" gcd. — Aluffi §6.2, p. 110

Remark 6.17 — two things that fail. Aluffi raises two questions about Definition 6.16 and answers both in the negative.

Does a gcd exist? Not necessarily.

If 1 is a gcd of a and b, is 1 a linear combination of a and b? Not necessarily. In ℤ[x], 1 is a gcd of 2 and x — the only principal ideal containing both is (1), since a common divisor must divide 2 in ℤ and divide x — and yet 1 is not of the form 2α(x) + xβ(x): every such combination has even constant term. — Aluffi §6.2, Remark 6.17, p. 110

So Bézout is not a consequence of the definition of gcd. It was a theorem about ℤ (Theorem 1.8), proved from well-ordering, and it does not travel. A useful corollary drops out: (2, x) is not principal, so ℤ[x] is not a principal ideal domain — which is Problem 6(c), and aa-12's subject.

Definition 6.18 (prime element). Let R be an integral domain. An element p ∈ R is prime if the principal ideal (p) is a prime ideal. — Aluffi §6.2, Definition 6.18, p. 111

By Theorem 6.4 this says exactly: p is not a unit, and whenever p ∣ ab we have p ∣ a or p ∣ b — Definition 1.19, generalised word for word. Remark 6.19: under this definition 0 is a prime element, consistently with §6.1. — Aluffi §6.2, Remark 6.19, p. 111

Definition 6.20 (irreducible element). Let R be an integral domain. An element q ∈ R is irreducible if q is not a unit and

for all a, b ∈ R:   q = ab ⟹ a is a unit or b is a unit.   (6.2)

— Aluffi §6.2, Definition 6.20, p. 111

This forces q ≠ 0, and it specialises to aa-02's Definition 1.17 in ℤ, where the units are ±1. Note the shift in phrasing: in a general ring "the only divisors are ±1 and ±q" must become "any factorisation has a unit factor", because ±1 is not a meaningful phrase. — Aluffi §6.2, p. 111

Lemma 6.22. Let R be an integral domain and q ≠ 0 a non-unit. Then q is irreducible if and only if (q) is maximal among proper principal ideals: if (q) ⊆ (a) then (a) = (1) or (a) = (q). — Aluffi §6.2, Lemma 6.22, p. 112

Theorem 6.23. In any integral domain, a nonzero prime element is irreducible. — Aluffi §6.2, Theorem 6.23, p. 112

Proof of Lemma 6.22. (⟹) Suppose q is irreducible and (q) ⊆ (a). Then q = ab for some b. Irreducibility makes a or b a unit: if a is a unit, (a) = (1); if b is a unit, (a) = (ab) = (q).

(⟸) Suppose the maximality condition holds and q = ab. Then (q) ⊆ (a), so (a) = (1) — making a a unit — or (a) = (q), in which case a = qu for a unit u, whence q = qub, and cancelling q (legal: R is a domain and q ≠ 0) gives ub = 1, so b is a unit. ∎ — Aluffi §6.2, p. 112

Cancellation is aa-06's Proposition 3.26, and it is the only place the domain hypothesis is spent.

Why the other direction cannot follow. It is worth tracing aa-02's proof that an irreducible integer is prime, because every step of it is now visibly local to ℤ. Given irreducible p with p ∤ b, that argument ran:

Lemma 1.18: gcd(p, b) = 1  ⟶  Corollary 1.11: p ∣ bc and gcd(p, b) = 1 give p ∣ c.

Both steps break in a general integral domain. Lemma 1.18 concludes that a certain gcd equals 1, but Definition 6.16 only determines a gcd up to units, and Remark 6.17 warns that one need not exist at all. And Corollary 1.11 was proved by writing 1 = mp + nb and multiplying by c — that is Bézout, Theorem 1.8, which Remark 6.17 has just shown does not survive: 1 is a gcd of 2 and x in ℤ[x] and is not a combination of them. — Aluffi §1.3, Corollary 1.11, p. 10; §1.4, Lemma 1.18, p. 13

So the failure of "irreducible ⟹ prime" is not a surprise to be absorbed; it is the predictable consequence of removing the one theorem the old proof rested on. aa-12 puts Bézout back, under a hypothesis, and gets the implication back with it.

Example 6.21 — irreducible but not prime. Let R = ℂ[x, y]/(y² − x³), writing x̲, y̲ for the cosets of x, y. This R is an integral domain, x̲ is irreducible, and y̲ ∉ (x̲). But in R

y̲² = x̲³,

because y² − x³ is 0 in the quotient. So y̲·y̲ ∈ (x̲) while y̲ ∉ (x̲): by Theorem 6.4, (x̲) is not a prime ideal, i.e. x̲ is not a prime element. It is irreducible and not prime. — Aluffi §6.2, Example 6.21, p. 111

And there is a geometric reason. The real curve y² = x³ is singular — it has a cusp at the origin — whereas the parabola y = x² of Example 6.8 is smooth, and in its coordinate ring ℂ[x, y]/(y − x²) ≅ ℂ[t] every irreducible is prime. Draw both and the difference is visible. — Aluffi §6.2, p. 111

A second witness, built by hand — ℤ[√−5]. Inside ℂ, in the manner of aa-07's Example 4.12, take

ℤ[√−5] = { a + b√−5 : a, b ∈ ℤ }.

It is a subring of ℂ (closed under + visibly, and under · because (a₁ + b₁√−5)(a₂ + b₂√−5) = (a₁a₂ − 5b₁b₂) + (a₁b₂ + a₂b₁)√−5), hence an integral domain — it sits inside a field. Define N(a + b√−5) = a² + 5b², which is just the squared modulus of the complex number, so N(αβ) = N(α)N(β).

The units are ±1. If αβ = 1 then N(α)N(β) = 1 with both factors positive integers, so N(α) = 1, i.e. a² + 5b² = 1, forcing b = 0 and a = ±1.

2 is irreducible. N(2) = 4. If 2 = αβ then N(α)N(β) = 4. Neither factor may have norm 1 (that would make it a unit), so both would have norm 2 — but a² + 5b² = 2 has no integer solution (b = 0 needs a² = 2; b ≠ 0 makes the value at least 5). So one factor is a unit.

2 is not prime. Observe 6 = 2·3 and also

6 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5),

since (1)² − (√−5)² = 1 + 5 = 6. So 2 ∣ (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5). But 2 divides neither factor: if 2 ∣ (1 ± √−5) then N(2) = 4 would divide N(1 ± √−5) = 6, and it does not.

So 2 is irreducible and not prime — a second, purely arithmetic instance of the failure that Example 6.21 exhibits geometrically. This is Problem 5(c), and the same ring reappears in aa-12 as the standard example of a domain without unique factorisation.

Guided proof (write it yourself)

One direction of "prime ⟺ irreducible" survives into every integral domain. Prove Theorem 6.23: if p is a nonzero prime element of an integral domain R, then p is irreducible. Use Lemma 6.22 — that is, assume (p) ⊆ (a) and show (a) = (p) or (a) = (1). Say explicitly where you use that R is a domain, and where you use that p ≠ 0.

Reveal model answer

Assume (p) ⊆ (a). Then p ∈ (a), so p = ab for some b ∈ R. Since p is a prime element, (p) is a prime ideal, and ab = p ∈ (p), so by Theorem 6.4 either a ∈ (p) or b ∈ (p).

Case a ∈ (p). Then (a) ⊆ (p), and combined with the assumption (p) ⊆ (a) we get (a) = (p).

Case b ∈ (p). Write b = cp. Then p = ab = a(cp) = (ac)p, so p·1 = p·(ac). Since R is an integral domain and p ≠ 0, cancellation (Proposition 3.26) gives 1 = ac, so a is a unit and (a) = (1).

In both cases (a) = (p) or (a) = (1), so by Lemma 6.22 p is irreducible. ∎

Grade yourself on the second case. Both hypotheses are consumed there and nowhere else: without a domain you cannot cancel, and without p ≠ 0 there is nothing to cancel. Notice also what the proof does not do — it never tries the converse, and the converse is exactly what fails in ℂ[x, y]/(y² − x³) and in ℤ[√−5].

Self-check 3. In ℤ[x], 1 is a gcd of 2 and x. Is 1 a linear combination 2α(x) + xβ(x)?

The constant term of 2α(x) + xβ(x) is 2α(0), which is even, so it is never 1. Bézout's theorem is a fact about ℤ proved from well-ordering (Theorem 1.8), not a consequence of the definition of gcd — and Aluffi raises this in Remark 6.17 precisely to stop the transfer. The consequence is that (2, x) is not principal, so ℤ[x] is not a PID. — Aluffi §6.2, Remark 6.17, p. 110

Self-check 4. Which implication holds in every integral domain?

Theorem 6.23 gives prime ⟹ irreducible everywhere, by cancellation. The converse is exactly what fails: x̲ in ℂ[x, y]/(y² − x³) and 2 in ℤ[√−5] are both irreducible and not prime. Theorem 1.21 proved the equivalence in ℤ, and aa-02 already flagged that its second half ran through Bézout — which Remark 6.17 has just shown does not generalise. — Aluffi §6.2, Theorem 6.23 and Example 6.21, pp. 111–112

Self-check 5. Why is 2 not a prime element of ℤ[√−5]?

Primality is about products, not factorisations — Definition 6.18 via Theorem 6.4. And 2 does not factor into non-units: the norm argument shows it is irreducible, which is the whole point. The units are ±1, so 2 is not one. What fails is that the product (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) = 6 is divisible by 2 while neither factor is, since N(2) = 4 does not divide N(1 ± √−5) = 6. — Aluffi §6.2, Definition 6.18 and Definition 6.20, p. 111
