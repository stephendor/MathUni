Classification of finitely generated abelian groups (aa-22)

There are exactly six abelian groups of order 360. Not "at least six", not "six that anyone has found" — six, and here they are.

A classification theorem is a rare and expensive thing, and this is the one this module has been building towards since aa-15. It turns every question about an arbitrary finite abelian group into a question about direct sums of cyclic groups, which you can simply write down. Today we complete the uniqueness half that aa-20 left open, list all groups of a given order, and spend the results: Cauchy's theorem, the fact that the nonzero classes mod p form a cyclic group, and — as a detour that would be very hard without any of this — Fermat's theorem on which primes are sums of two squares.

Predict before we start. How many abelian groups of order 360 are there, up to isomorphism?

Six. They are C₃₆₀, C₂ ⊕ C₁₈₀, C₂ ⊕ C₂ ⊕ C₉₀, C₃ ⊕ C₁₂₀, C₆ ⊕ C₆₀ and C₂ ⊕ C₆ ⊕ C₃₀ — one for each chain 1 < d₁ ∣ d₂ ∣ ⋯ with product 360. C₃₆₀ is one of them, not all of them; and the count is finite because the exponents of 2, 3 and 5 can only be split in finitely many ways. Aluffi's line: every abelian group of order 360 you will ever run across is isomorphic to one and only one on that list. — Aluffi §10.3, Example 10.20, p. 214

Why this matters for the mission: Free rank (Betti numbers) plus torsion coefficients — the full content of an integral homology group.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The theorem, its uniqueness, and how to list

Theorem 10.18 (Classification of finite abelian groups). Every finite abelian group A satisfies

A ≅ Cd₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Cds   with 1 < d₁ ∣ d₂ ∣ ⋯ ∣ ds,

uniquely; equivalently, A ≅ ⊕i,j ℤ/piαijℤ for primes pi and exponents αij > 0 uniquely determined by A. — Aluffi §10.3, Theorem 10.18, p. 212

The two forms are equivalent by the Chinese Remainder Theorem, in the shape of aa-21's Proposition 10.13. The di are the invariant factors; the piαij are the elementary divisors. Existence was proved in aa-20 (Theorem 9.40 with R = ℤ); uniqueness was only sketched there, and finishing it is the first order of business. — Aluffi §10.3, p. 213

Proposition 10.19. The elementary divisors are unique: if ⊕i,j ℤ/piαijℤ ≅ ⊕k,ℓ ℤ/qkβkℓℤ, then the primes and exponents agree up to reordering. — Aluffi §10.3, Proposition 10.19, p. 213
Proof — by counting, one prime at a time. Fix a prime p and extract from each side the summands whose prime is p; the two resulting groups are isomorphic, both being the subgroup of A of elements of p-power order. So it suffices to show that

ℤ/pα₁ℤ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ ℤ/pαmℤ ≅ ℤ/pβ₁ℤ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ ℤ/pβnℤ

forces m = n and matching exponents. Count, for each d, the elements of order at most pd: a tuple has order ≤ pd exactly when every coordinate does, and by aa-21's Example 10.15 the ith coordinate contributes pmin(αi, d) choices. So

∏i pmin(αi, d) = ∏k pmin(βk, d)   for every d ≥ 0,

and the lists must agree — which is your guided proof. ∎ — Aluffi §10.3, p. 213

Notice the strategy. Two groups are compared not by constructing a map but by counting something both must have the same amount of: elements of order at most pd. That is the same move as aa-08's "find a ring-theoretic property that separates them", used constructively.

Faded example — Example 10.20: listing the abelian groups of order 360. Factor: 360 = 2³·3²·5. An invariant-factor list is a chain 1 < d₁ ∣ d₂ ∣ ⋯ with product 360, and Aluffi's method builds one from a table.

Write columns for the primes 2, 3, 5, each column a decreasing list of powers whose product is the full prime power — 2³ = 8, 3² = 9, 5¹ = 5. The rows of the table, multiplied out, are the di.

For instance the table with rows (4, 3, 5) and (2, 3, —) gives d₂ = 4·3·5 = ⟨your step⟩ and d₁ = 2·3 = ⟨your step⟩, i.e. the group C₆ ⊕ C₆₀ — and indeed 6 ∣ 60 and 6·60 = 360. ✓

Running through every table gives six lists, hence six groups:

Check one: the third row has product 2·2·90 = ⟨your step⟩ ✓ and 2 ∣ 2 ∣ 90 ✓. — Aluffi §10.3, Example 10.20, p. 214

The count depends only on how the exponent of each prime can be split: 3 splits in three ways, 2 in two ways, 1 in one — and 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.

Self-check 1. The uniqueness proof compares two decompositions by counting what?

Both sides have the same total size by hypothesis, so that settles nothing; and the number of summands is what is being proved equal, so it cannot be assumed. The quantity that does the work is the count of elements killed by pd, which is ∏ pmin(αi, d) and depends on the exponents in a way that recovers them. — Aluffi §10.3, Proposition 10.19, p. 213

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Three consequences, each one paragraph

Aluffi's remark is that it is hard to overemphasise how useful a classification is: questions about arbitrary finite abelian groups reduce to questions about direct sums of cyclic groups, disposing of them "without the need for clever constructions, induction arguments, and whatnot". Three examples. — Aluffi §10.3, p. 214

Proposition 10.21. For a finite abelian group A and a ∈ A, the order |a| divides |A|. — Aluffi §10.3, Proposition 10.21, p. 214

Proof: write A ≅ ℤ/d₁ℤ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ ℤ/dsℤ with d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ ds. Then ds·a = 0 for every a, since each di divides ds; and |A| = d₁⋯ds is a multiple of ds, so |A|·a = 0, so |a| ∣ |A| by aa-21's Proposition 10.16. ∎ (aa-28 recovers this from Lagrange's theorem, which is much cheaper — but it applies to all groups, not only abelian ones.) — Aluffi §10.3, p. 215

Theorem 10.22 (Cauchy's theorem, abelian version). If a prime p divides |A| then A contains an element of order p. — Aluffi §10.3, Theorem 10.22, p. 215

Proof: A ≅ Cd₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Cds. Since p ∣ d₁⋯ds and d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ ds, p divides ds; by aa-21's Proposition 10.10(i), Cds has a subgroup of order p, and Cds sits inside A. A generator of that subgroup has order p. ∎ Aluffi notes an induction proof exists and invites you to compare; the classification makes it three lines. This becomes an ingredient of the first Sylow theorem in aa-29. — Aluffi §10.3, p. 215

Lemma 10.23. If a finite abelian group A has, for every n > 0, at most n elements with na = 0, then A is cyclic. — Aluffi §10.3, Lemma 10.23, p. 215

Theorem 10.24. Every finite subgroup of the multiplicative group (k∗, ·) of a field is cyclic. — Aluffi §10.3, Theorem 10.24, p. 216

Proofs. For Lemma 10.23: with A ≅ ℤ/d₁ℤ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ ℤ/dsℤ, every a satisfies dsa = 0, so the number of elements killed by ds is |A|. The hypothesis forces |A| ≤ ds. But |A| = d₁⋯ds ≥ ds, so |A| = ds and s = 1: A is cyclic. ∎

For Theorem 10.24: in k[x] the polynomial xn − 1 has at most n roots, because unique factorisation holds in k[x] (aa-13's Corollary 7.8). So at most n elements g of G satisfy gn = 1, and Lemma 10.23 applies. ∎ — Aluffi §10.3, pp. 215–216

Two facts, from two different chapters, meeting. The bound "at most n roots" is polynomial algebra; the conclusion "therefore cyclic" is group classification. Neither alone gives the theorem.

What Theorem 10.24 buys, at no cost.

Example 10.26. ((ℤ/pℤ)∗, ·) is cyclic of order p − 1 for every prime p. Aluffi's example: 87178291199 is prime, so that multiplicative group is cyclic of order 87178291198 — meaning some integer a in that range has every other one congruent to a power of it. We know this "without any further work whatsoever", while producing such an a is real labour. — Aluffi §10.3, Example 10.26, p. 216

Example 10.27. x³ + x + 1 is irreducible over ℤ/5ℤ (no root — aa-14's Proposition 7.15), so (ℤ/5ℤ)[x]/(x³ + x + 1) is a field with 5³ = 125 elements (irreducibles generate maximal ideals in a PID, aa-12's Proposition 6.33). Its multiplicative group is therefore cyclic of order 124 — known without any acquaintance with the field at all. — Aluffi §10.3, Example 10.27, p. 216

Example 10.25. And aa-04's Exercise 2.9, which asked for a class [a]₇ whose powers give every nonzero class, was simply asking for a generator of a group we now know to be cyclic.

Self-check 2. Why is ((ℤ/pℤ)∗, ·) cyclic?

Its order is p − 1, which is usually not prime — for p = 7 it is 6. Being cyclic under addition is a different statement about a different operation. What applies is Theorem 10.24: ℤ/pℤ is a field (aa-04's Theorem 2.15), its nonzero elements form a finite multiplicative group, and every such group is cyclic — because xn − 1 has at most n roots and Lemma 10.23 then bites. — Aluffi §10.3, Theorem 10.24, p. 216

Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares

Theorem 10.28. Let p > 2 be prime. Then p is a sum of two squares if and only if p ≡ 1 mod 4. — Aluffi §10.4, Theorem 10.28, p. 217

Fermat stated it around 1640 without proof. It is a statement about integers with no visible algebra in it; the proof runs entirely through the Gaussian integers ℤ[i] of aa-07. — Aluffi §10.4, p. 217

The chain of results.

Lemma 10.29. The units of ℤ[i] are ±1 and ±i. — Aluffi §10.4, Lemma 10.29, p. 219

Proposition 10.30. ℤ[i] is a Euclidean domain — so by aa-12 it is a PID and a UFD, with the valuation given by the norm N(a + bi) = a² + b². — Aluffi §10.4, Proposition 10.30, p. 219

Corollary 10.32. If a positive prime p is not prime in ℤ[i], then p is a sum of two perfect squares. The reason is short: a factorisation p = zw with neither factor a unit gives N(z)N(w) = N(p) = p², and neither norm is 1, so N(z) = p — that is, p = a² + b² where z = a + bi. — Aluffi §10.4, Corollary 10.32, p. 220

Lemma 10.34. p is prime in ℤ[i] if and only if x² + 1 is irreducible in (ℤ/pℤ)[x]. — Aluffi §10.4, Lemma 10.34, p. 222

Lemma 10.33. For p > 2, x² + 1 is reducible over ℤ/pℤ if and only if p ≡ 1 mod 4. — Aluffi §10.4, Lemma 10.33, p. 221

Assembling. For p > 2:   p ≡ 1 mod 4 ⟺ x² + 1 reducible mod p (10.33) ⟺ p not prime in ℤ[i] (10.34) ⟹ p is a sum of two squares (10.32). The converse direction is the easy one — a square is 0 or 1 mod 4, so a sum of two squares is 0, 1 or 2 mod 4 and an odd such number must be ≡ 1.

And where the abelian-group theory enters. Lemma 10.33 is a statement about whether −1 is a square mod p — that is, about the structure of the group ((ℤ/pℤ)∗, ·), which Theorem 10.24 has just told us is cyclic of order p − 1. In a cyclic group of order p − 1 there is an element of order 4 exactly when 4 ∣ p − 1, i.e. exactly when p ≡ 1 mod 4 — and an element of order 4 squares to the unique element of order 2, which is −1. The classification theorem is doing the work.

The picture: a prime p is a sum of two squares exactly when some Gaussian integer has norm p — that is, when a lattice point lies on the circle of radius √p. Try the primes below.

Aluffi's headline example. The 10,000th positive prime is 104729. It is ≡ 1 mod 4, so Theorem 10.28 guarantees it is a sum of two squares — before any search is performed. And indeed

104729 = 20² + 323² = 400 + 104329.

The theorem does not find the representation; it certifies that one exists. That distinction — an existence theorem is not an algorithm — has recurred all through this module, from Bézout in aa-02 to the barcode in aa-20. — Aluffi §10.4, p. 217

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Proposition 10.19 reduces to a combinatorial step that Aluffi leaves as Exercise 10.15. Supply it. Let α₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ αm and β₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ βn be positive integers with

Σi min(αi, d) = Σk min(βk, d)   for every d ≥ 0.

Prove that m = n and αi = βi for all i. (The exponent equation ∏ pmin(αi,d) = ∏ pmin(βk,d) becomes this by comparing exponents.) The whole proof is one difference.

Reveal model answer

Write f(d) = Σi min(αi, d) and g(d) = Σk min(βk, d), so f = g by hypothesis.

The key difference. For d ≥ 1, min(α, d) − min(α, d−1) equals 1 if α ≥ d, and 0 otherwise. Summing,

f(d) − f(d−1) = #{ i : αi ≥ d }.

Since f = g, we get #{i : αi ≥ d} = #{k : βk ≥ d} for every d ≥ 1.

Conclusion. Taking d = 1 and using that all exponents are ≥ 1 gives m = n. And the function d ↦ #{i : αi ≥ d} determines the multiset {αi} completely: the largest exponent is the largest d for which the count is nonzero, the number of αi equal to a given value v is (count at v) − (count at v+1), and so on. Since the two counting functions agree for every d, the multisets agree; and both lists being sorted, αi = βi for every i. ∎

Grade yourself on taking the difference. Working with f(d) directly is hard because min is not additive in α; the first difference converts it into a plain count, and after that the argument is bookkeeping. This is the same device as reading a partition off its conjugate — and it is why the elementary divisors, rather than the invariant factors, are the convenient form for the uniqueness proof.

You try (solo). (a) List all abelian groups of order 200 = 2³·5² in invariant-factor form, and check your count against the rule "multiply the number of ways to split each prime exponent". (b) Do the same for order 72, and observe you get the same count as for 360 and 200 — explain why, in terms of the exponents rather than the numbers. (c) Convert one of your order-200 groups into elementary-divisor form and back, checking that both give the same total order. (d) Finally: is C₄ ⊕ C₅₀ on your list? If not, find which group on the list it is isomorphic to.

Self-check 3. Cauchy's theorem for abelian groups says what?

Theorem 10.22 is a converse in spirit to Proposition 10.21: the latter says every element order divides |A|, the former says every prime divisor of |A| is realised as an element order. It does not claim that every divisor is realised — that is true for cyclic groups but not in general — and certainly not that all element orders are prime. It becomes an ingredient of Sylow's first theorem in aa-29. — Aluffi §10.3, Theorem 10.22, p. 215

Self-check 4. Why is 7 not a sum of two squares, while 13 is?

Theorem 10.28: an odd prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is ≡ 1 mod 4. Here 13 = 4 + 9 = 2² + 3², while no pair of squares sums to 7 — as the easy direction shows, since squares are 0 or 1 mod 4 and so a sum of two is never ≡ 3. Both numbers are prime, and size is irrelevant: 104729 is ≡ 1 mod 4 and is 20² + 323². — Aluffi §10.4, Theorem 10.28, p. 217

Self-check 5. Which property of ℤ[i] makes the two-squares proof work?

Proposition 10.30 makes ℤ[i] Euclidean, so aa-12's chain gives PID and UFD, and the multiplicative norm turns the factorisation p = zw into the numerical statement N(z)N(w) = p², from which N(z) = p, i.e. p = a² + b². It is not a field — 2 has no inverse — and it is infinite. The comparison with aa-11's ℤ[√−5], which is not a UFD, is the point: which quadratic ring you land in decides everything. — Aluffi §10.4, Proposition 10.30 and Corollary 10.32, pp. 219–220

Then check yourself
  1. Theorem 10.18 classifies finite abelian groups two equivalent ways — invariant factors 1 < d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ ds, or elementary divisors pα — with the Chinese Remainder Theorem converting between them. Existence came from aa-20; uniqueness (Proposition 10.19) is proved by counting elements of order at most pd and reading the exponents off the first difference of that count. Listing all groups of a given order is then combinatorics on the prime exponents: six of order 360.
  2. The theorem is spent immediately. Every element order divides the group order (Proposition 10.21); every prime divisor of the order is realised as an element order (Cauchy, Theorem 10.22); and a finite abelian group with at most n solutions of na = 0 is cyclic (Lemma 10.23) — hence every finite subgroup of a field's multiplicative group is cyclic (Theorem 10.24), because xn − 1 has at most n roots. So (ℤ/pℤ)∗ is cyclic, for every prime, known without exhibiting a generator.
  3. Fermat's two-squares theorem — an odd prime is a sum of two squares exactly when p ≡ 1 mod 4 — is proved by moving into ℤ[i], which is Euclidean (Proposition 10.30) hence a UFD, where the multiplicative norm converts a factorisation of p into p = a² + b². The mod-4 condition enters through whether −1 is a square mod p, which is a question about the cyclic group (ℤ/pℤ)∗.
The mission strip, unpacked: an integral homology group is a finitely generated abelian group, so by Theorem 10.18 it is ℤb ⊕ (torsion). The b is the Betti number; the invariant factors are the torsion coefficients; and by uniqueness, those numbers are the group. Nothing else about it is left to know.
