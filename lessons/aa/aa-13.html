Polynomial rings over a field (aa-13)

Fermat's Last Theorem took 358 years. The same statement for polynomials takes about a page, and Aluffi sets it as homework.

The reason is one theorem: if k is a field then k[x] admits long division, so it is Euclidean, so it is a principal ideal domain, so unique factorisation holds in it. Everything aa-12 built for PIDs lands on k[x] at once — gcds, Bézout, irreducible ⟺ prime, a Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic for polynomials. Aluffi puts the Fermat application before the proof, on purpose, to show what the abstraction buys. We will prove the machinery first and cash the cheque at the end.

Predict before we start. k is a field. Is k[x] a field?

A polynomial ring R[x] is never a field: x ≠ 0, and xα(x) = 1 is impossible because multiplying by x can never produce a nonzero constant term. Over a domain the units of k[x] are exactly the nonzero constants — the units of k, viewed as constant polynomials. So k[x] is a PID that is not a field, which by aa-12's Remark 6.31 makes it a "curve" of Krull dimension 1, the ring algebraic geometry attaches to a line. — Aluffi §7.2, p. 132

Why this matters for the mission: A persistence module is formally a graded module over k[x] — this is that ring.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Degree, long division, and roots

Definition 7.1. For a nonzero f(x) = a₀ + a₁x + ⋯ + adxd ∈ R[x] with ad ≠ 0: d is the degree deg f, ad is the leading coefficient, a₀ the constant term. f is constant if it equals its constant term, and monic if its leading coefficient is 1. The polynomial 0 has no degree and no leading coefficient; it counts as constant. — Aluffi §7.2, Definition 7.1, p. 131

The constant polynomials form a subring of R[x] isomorphic to R — aa-07's Example 4.11. Degree behaves well when R is an integral domain, and then the units of R[x] are exactly the units of R sitting as constants; over a field k that means every nonzero constant. Both facts are your guided proof. — Aluffi §7.2, pp. 131–132

Proposition 7.3 (long division). Let R be a ring and f(x), g(x) ∈ R[x] with f ≠ 0 and the leading coefficient of f a unit. Then there are q(x), r(x) ∈ R[x] with

g(x) = q(x)f(x) + r(x),    r(x) = 0 or deg r(x) < deg f(x).

— Aluffi §7.2, Proposition 7.3, p. 132
Proof. If g = 0 or deg g < deg f, take q = 0 and r = g. Otherwise suppose the statement fails for some g with n = deg g ≥ m = deg f, and by well-ordering choose such a g with n least. Write f = a₀ + ⋯ + amxm and g = b₀ + ⋯ + bnxn, and let u = am−1, which exists by hypothesis. Put

g₁(x) := g(x) − bnu f(x) xn−m.

Its xn coefficient is bn − bnuam = 0, so g₁ = 0 or deg g₁ < n. By minimality the statement holds for g₁: g₁ = q₁f + r₁ with r₁ = 0 or deg r₁ < deg f. Then

g(x) = (q₁(x) + bnu xn−m)·f(x) + r₁(x),

so the statement holds for g after all — contradiction. ∎ — Aluffi §7.2, p. 133

Read the hypothesis. Nothing is assumed about R except that f's leading coefficient is invertible — that is exactly the coefficient you must divide by at each step of the schoolbook algorithm. This is why long division works in ℤ[x] when you divide by a monic polynomial and fails when you divide by 2x + 1.

Two consequences worth naming.

Example 7.4. For any ring R and any α(x) ∈ R[x] there are β(x) ∈ R[x] and a, b ∈ R with α(x) = β(x)(x² + 1) + a + bx — because x² + 1 is monic and every polynomial of degree < 2 has that shape. This is the fact aa-10's Example 5.42 used to compute ker(α ↦ α(i)) and conclude ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) ≅ ℂ. — Aluffi §7.2, Example 7.4, p. 133

Example 7.5 — the remainder on dividing by x − a is g(a). Since x − a is monic, g(x) = q(x)(x − a) + r with r constant; evaluating at a gives g(a) = q(a)·0 + r = r. Hence

x − a is a factor of g(x) if and only if g(a) = 0.

— Aluffi §7.2, Example 7.5, p. 133

Definition 7.6. a ∈ R is a root of g(x) if g(a) = 0. So: the roots of g are exactly the a for which x − a divides g. — Aluffi §7.2, Definition 7.6, p. 134

Proposition 7.9 (uniqueness). If R is an integral domain and f ≠ 0 has unit leading coefficient, then the q and r of Proposition 7.3 are uniquely determined by f and g. — Aluffi §7.2, Proposition 7.9, p. 134
Proof. Suppose g = q₁f + r₁ = q₂f + r₂ with both remainders 0 or of degree < deg f. Subtracting, (q₁ − q₂)f = r₂ − r₁. If r₂ − r₁ ≠ 0 then, R being a domain, the left side has degree at least deg f while the right side has degree less than deg f — impossible. So r₁ = r₂, and then (q₁ − q₂)f = 0 with f ≠ 0 forces q₁ = q₂. ∎ — Aluffi §7.2, p. 135

The domain hypothesis is used twice, and both times through the degree formula deg(αβ) = deg α + deg β — which is the first half of your guided proof.

Self-check 1. In ℤ[x], can you always divide g(x) by f(x) = 2x + 1 with remainder of smaller degree?

Proposition 7.3 requires the leading coefficient of the divisor to be a unit, because the algorithm multiplies by its inverse at every step. In ℤ[x] that rules out 2x + 1: dividing x² by it would need a coefficient of ½. Over a field every nonzero constant is a unit, which is precisely why k[x] is Euclidean and ℤ[x] is not. Having no root is irrelevant — x² + 1 has no real root and divides fine in ℝ[x]. — Aluffi §7.2, Proposition 7.3, p. 132

Segment 2 · ~25 min

k[x] is Euclidean — and everything that follows

Theorem 7.2. If k is a field then k[x] is a Euclidean domain. — Aluffi §7.2, Theorem 7.2, p. 132
Proof. k is a field, hence a domain, hence k[x] is a domain. Take the valuation v(f) = deg f. Every nonzero element of k is a unit, so every nonzero f ∈ k[x] has unit leading coefficient, and Proposition 7.3 applies to every divisor: for all f ≠ 0 and all g there are q, r with g = qf + r and r = 0 or deg r < deg f. That is Definition 6.24. ∎ — Aluffi §7.2, p. 134

Corollary 7.7. k[x] is a PID — Euclidean domains are PIDs (aa-12's Theorem 6.26). — Aluffi §7.2, Corollary 7.7, p. 134

Corollary 7.8. Unique factorisation holds in k[x] — PIDs are UFDs (Theorem 6.35). — Aluffi §7.2, Corollary 7.8, p. 134

Two corollaries, each one line, and between them they transfer the entire content of aa-12. Concretely, in k[x] you now have: every ideal is (f) for a single f; gcds exist and are Bézout combinations; an irreducible polynomial is prime, and (q) is a maximal ideal for q irreducible — that is aa-12's Proposition 6.33 with aa-11's Lemma 6.22 and Definition 6.2; and every nonconstant polynomial factors into irreducibles uniquely up to units and order. Aluffi's summary: for gcds and irreducibility, k[x] behaves just like ℤ. — Aluffi §7.2, p. 134; §6.1, Definition 6.2, p. 106; §6.2, Lemma 6.22, p. 112

Corollary 7.10. Let R be an integral domain and f ≠ 0 with unit leading coefficient. Then every coset in R[x]/(f(x)) has a unique representative r(x) + (f(x)) with r = 0 or deg r < deg f. — Aluffi §7.2, Corollary 7.10, p. 135
Proof. Given g(x) + (f(x)), Propositions 7.3 and 7.9 supply unique q, r with g = qf + r and r = 0 or deg r < deg f. Then g − r = qf ∈ (f), so g + (f) = r + (f), and r is determined by g. ∎ — Aluffi §7.2, p. 135

This is the exact analogue of aa-03's Theorem 2.7, where every class of ℤ/nℤ had a unique representative in {0, …, n − 1}. There the window was "remainder smaller than n"; here it is "degree smaller than deg f". Same theorem, same proof shape, different valuation.

Theorem 7.11. Let k be a field and f ∈ k[x] nonzero of degree d. Then k[x]/(f(x)) is a d-dimensional k-vector space — its elements correspond to d-tuples (a₀, …, ad−1), compatibly with addition and scalar multiplication. — Aluffi §7.2, Theorem 7.11, p. 135

Because over a field every nonzero polynomial has unit leading coefficient, Corollary 7.10 applies to every f, and the unique representative a₀ + a₁x + ⋯ + ad−1xd−1 is the tuple. So the quotient is finite-dimensional and you can compute in it by reducing mod f after every multiplication. — Aluffi §7.2, p. 135

Faded example — computing in ℝ[x]/(x² + 1).

deg(x² + 1) = 2, so by Theorem 7.11 the quotient is a ⟨your step: how many⟩-dimensional real vector space, with cosets uniquely written a + bx + (x² + 1).

Multiply two of them: (a₁ + b₁x)(a₂ + b₂x) = a₁a₂ + (a₁b₂ + a₂b₁)x + b₁b₂x². The last term is not in reduced form, so divide by x² + 1: since x² = 1·(x² + 1) − 1, we may replace x² by ⟨your step⟩.

The product is therefore ⟨your step: collect the constant⟩ + (a₁b₂ + a₂b₁)x, which is the multiplication rule for ⟨your step: which famous ring?⟩ — recovering aa-09's Example 5.34 and aa-10's Example 5.42 by pure long division.

Now change the modulus: in ℝ[x]/(x² − 1), the same computation replaces x² by ⟨your step⟩ instead, and the resulting ring is not a field. Ask yourself which property of x² + 1 the other polynomial lacks.

You try (solo). Work in 𝔽 = ℤ/2ℤ, a field. (a) Divide x⁴ + x + 1 by x² + x + 1 in 𝔽[x], giving q and r explicitly; remember that in 𝔽 we have −1 = 1, so subtraction is addition. (b) By Theorem 7.11, how many elements does 𝔽[x]/(x² + x + 1) have? List them. (c) Check whether x² + x + 1 has a root in 𝔽, and use Example 7.5 to decide whether it is irreducible there. (d) If it is, aa-12's Proposition 6.33 says (x² + x + 1) is a maximal ideal — so the quotient is a field. You have just built a field with four elements, which is not ℤ/4ℤ.

Self-check 2. Why is k[x] a PID when k is a field, while ℤ[x] is not?

Proposition 7.3 needs the divisor's leading coefficient to be invertible. Over a field that is automatic for every nonzero divisor, so the degree is a Euclidean valuation (Theorem 7.2) and Theorem 6.26 gives a PID. Over ℤ it fails for divisors like 2x + 1, and indeed (2, x) is not principal. ℤ is a PID, which is exactly what makes the contrast instructive: the PID property does not pass to polynomial rings. — Aluffi §7.2, Theorem 7.2 and Corollary 7.7, pp. 132–134

Self-check 3. How many elements does the ring k[x]/(f) have when k = ℤ/3ℤ and deg f = 2?

By Theorem 7.11 the quotient is a 2-dimensional vector space over ℤ/3ℤ, so its elements are the pairs (a₀, a₁) — that is, the cosets a₀ + a₁x with a₀, a₁ ∈ ℤ/3ℤ. There are 3² = 9 of them. Note what this construction does: it manufactures finite rings, and finite fields whenever f is irreducible, of sizes that are prime powers rather than primes. — Aluffi §7.2, Theorem 7.11, p. 135

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Two facts were used above and left to the exercises. Let R be an integral domain. (a) Prove that deg(fg) = deg f + deg g for all nonzero f, g ∈ R[x]. (b) Deduce that the units of R[x] are exactly the units of R, as constant polynomials — and hence that k[x] is never a field. Say where the domain hypothesis is used, and give a ring where (a) is false.

Reveal model answer

(a) Let f have degree d with leading coefficient ad ≠ 0, and g degree e with leading coefficient be ≠ 0. In the product, the coefficient of xd+e is adbe, and no higher power occurs. Since R is an integral domain and both ad, be are nonzero, adbe ≠ 0. So fg ≠ 0 and deg(fg) = d + e.

(b) Suppose fg = 1 in R[x]. Both are nonzero, so by (a) deg f + deg g = deg 1 = 0, and since degrees are nonnegative, deg f = deg g = 0. So f and g are constants, say f = a and g = b with ab = 1 — that is, a is a unit of R. Conversely a unit of R is clearly a unit of R[x]. Hence R[x]∗ = R∗.

In particular, for a field k the units of k[x] are the nonzero constants, so x — which is nonzero and non-constant — is not a unit, and k[x] is not a field.

Where the hypothesis is needed. Only at adbe ≠ 0. Without it (a) is false: in (ℤ/4ℤ)[x], both 2x + 1 and 2x + 1 have degree 1, yet (2x + 1)² = 4x² + 4x + 1 = 1, of degree 0. That same ring also breaks (b) — 2x + 1 is a non-constant unit. ∎

Grade yourself on producing a counterexample for (a). If you only proved the positive statement, you have not established that the hypothesis is doing work, and every claim in this lesson about degrees — including the uniqueness in Proposition 7.9 — rests on it.

Fermat's Last Theorem, for polynomials

Fermat's Last Theorem says an + bn = cn has no solution in positive integers for n ≥ 3. Fermat wrote it in a margin in 1637; Wiles proved it in 1995, 358 years later. We are not going to prove that. But the same question over a different ring is tractable: are there f(t), g(t), h(t) ∈ ℂ[t], not all constant, with — Aluffi §7.1, p. 129

f(t)n + g(t)n = h(t)n,    n ≥ 3?   (7.1)

Three tools: ℂ[t] is a UFD (Corollary 7.8), every complex number has n-th roots, and 1 − tn factors completely: — Aluffi §7.1, pp. 129–130

1 − tn = (1 − t)(1 − ζt)(1 − ζ²t)⋯(1 − ζn−1t),    ζ = e2πi/n.   (7.2)

For n = 4, ζ = i and the identity reads 1 − t⁴ = (1 − t)(1 − it)(1 + t)(1 + it), which you can check in seconds. In general the powers ζj are the vertices of a regular n-gon in the complex plane. — Aluffi §7.1, p. 130

The argument. Suppose (7.1) has a solution with f, g, h not all constant. Clearing common factors we may assume g and h are relatively prime, and by well-ordering we may choose the solution with deg f + deg g + deg h as small as possible.

Step 1 — factor. Substitute t ↦ g(t)/h(t) in (7.2) and clear denominators:

hn − gn = (h − g)(h − ζg)(h − ζ²g)⋯(h − ζn−1g).   (7.3)

By (7.1) the left side is fn.

Step 2 — the factors are pairwise coprime. Take two distinct factors α = h − ζig and β = h − ζjg. Then

(α − β)/(ζj − ζi) = g   and   (ζjα − ζiβ)/(ζj − ζi) = h,

so a common irreducible factor of α and β would be a common irreducible factor of g and h — which we excluded. — Aluffi §7.1, p. 130

Step 3 — unique factorisation. fn is an n-th power and the factors on the right of (7.3) are pairwise coprime, so each factor is itself an n-th power up to a unit — and units in ℂ[t] are nonzero complex numbers, which are themselves n-th powers because ℂ is algebraically closed. Since n ≥ 3 there are at least three factors, so there exist a(t), b(t), c(t) with

h − g = an,   h − ζg = bn,   h − ζ²g = cn.

Step 4 — descend. A short computation gives (−ζ)(h − g) + (1 + ζ)(h − ζg) = h − ζ²g, that is (−ζ)an + (1 + ζ)bn = cn. Choosing λ, μ ∈ ℂ with λn = −ζ and μn = 1 + ζ,

(λa)n + (μb)n = cn,

another solution of the same shape — with strictly smaller total degree, since f, g, h were not all constant. That contradicts minimality. So no solution exists. ∎ — Aluffi §7.1, pp. 130–131

Why this does not settle the integer case. ℤ is also a PID, so the temptation is obvious. But the argument does not run in ℤ — it runs in a ring containing ℤ and ζ, and possibly n-th roots of other elements. The relevant ring ℤ[ζ], the smallest subring of ℂ containing ℤ and ζ = e2πi/n, is not a UFD for most n. Step 3 is exactly where it collapses. — Aluffi §7.1, p. 131

This is worth sitting with. The proof above is not clever; it is three routine moves standing on unique factorisation. The 358 years were spent because that one hypothesis is false in the ring where the integer argument would have to live. aa-11's ℤ[√−5] was the small version of the same disaster.

One more use of the machinery — interpolation is the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Let k be a field and a₁, …, an distinct elements. The ideals (x − ai) are pairwise comaximal: for i ≠ j,

1 = ((x − aj) − (x − ai))/(ai − aj) ∈ (x − ai) + (x − aj),

using that ai − aj ≠ 0 is invertible in k. So aa-10's Theorem 5.52 applies and

k[x]/((x − a₁)⋯(x − an)) ≅ k[x]/(x − a₁) × ⋯ × k[x]/(x − an) ≅ k × ⋯ × k,

each factor by aa-09's Example 5.33. Read the isomorphism from left to right and it says: a polynomial of degree < n is determined by, and may be prescribed arbitrarily at, n distinct points. That is Lagrange interpolation, and it is the Chinese Remainder Theorem wearing polynomial clothes. This is Problem 6 of your set. — Aluffi §5.6, Theorem 5.52, p. 97

Self-check 4. In Step 3 of the Fermat argument, which property of ℂ[t] is used?

Unique factorisation is the load-bearing hypothesis: if a product of pairwise coprime elements is an n-th power, then comparing exponents in the factorisations forces each factor to be an n-th power up to a unit. ℂ[t] is a UFD by Corollary 7.8, via Euclidean ⟹ PID ⟹ UFD. It is not a field (the prediction gate), and it is infinite-dimensional over ℂ. And this is exactly the step that fails over ℤ[ζ]. — Aluffi §7.1, p. 130

Self-check 5. Why does the polynomial argument not prove Fermat's Last Theorem over ℤ?

ℤ is a PID, and a size function to descend on is available. What is missing is the ring: the factorisation (7.3) requires ζ = e2πi/n, so the argument lives in ℤ[ζ], and unique factorisation fails there for most n. Aluffi says so plainly, and it is the historically decisive point — several nineteenth-century "proofs" of Fermat's Last Theorem assumed exactly this and were wrong. — Aluffi §7.1, p. 131

Then check yourself
  1. Long division needs one thing (Proposition 7.3): the divisor's leading coefficient must be a unit. Over any ring that gives division by monic polynomials; over a field it gives division by everything. Uniqueness of q and r needs a domain (Proposition 7.9), through deg(fg) = deg f + deg g. Dividing by x − a leaves the constant g(a), so x − a ∣ g ⟺ a is a root.
  2. k a field ⟹ k[x] Euclidean with v = deg (Theorem 7.2), hence a PID (Corollary 7.7), hence a UFD (Corollary 7.8). Every ideal is (f); gcds and Bézout return; irreducible ⟺ prime, and (q) is maximal for q irreducible. Cosets have unique representatives of degree < deg f (Corollary 7.10), so k[x]/(f) is a d-dimensional k-vector space (Theorem 7.11) — the exact analogue of ℤ/nℤ having n elements.
  3. Fermat for polynomials is a page. Factor hn − gn using the n-th roots of unity, note the factors are pairwise coprime, use unique factorisation to make each an n-th power, and produce a smaller solution — contradiction with a minimal choice. It does not transfer to ℤ because the argument needs ℤ[ζ], which is usually not a UFD.
The one line to keep: k[x] is the ring that behaves like ℤ, and it does so because the degree is a Euclidean valuation. Persistence modules live over k[x] for exactly this reason — aa-20 needs a PID, and this is the PID that graded intervals come from.
