Kernels, ideals, and quotient rings (aa-09)

Take ℝ[x], the real polynomials, and declare x² + 1 to be zero. Not "approximately zero" — build a new ring in which it genuinely is. What you get is ℂ.

That is the whole of today in one sentence. aa-03 built ℤ/nℤ by gluing along congruence mod n; now we do it for an arbitrary ring, gluing along an arbitrary ideal. And the ideals are not chosen for convenience: they are forced. The set of things a homomorphism sends to 0 turns out to have exactly one strange extra property — it absorbs multiplication by everything — and that property is precisely what is needed to make the quotient's multiplication well defined. Kernel modulo image is the shape of a homology group; this is where the shape is first assembled.

Predict before we start. Let f : R → S be a nontrivial ring homomorphism. Is ker f = {r : f(r) = 0} a subring of R?

It is closed under both operations and contains 0 and all its negatives. What it lacks is 1: f(1) = 1 ≠ 0 whenever S is nontrivial, so 1 ∉ ker f. Aluffi's word for it is the one from aa-05: the kernel is a rng. It is not a subring, and it is not supposed to be — it is a different kind of substructure, and naming that kind is the business of Segment 1. — Aluffi §5.2, p. 80

Why this matters for the mission: Kernel-mod-image, made concrete for rings — the exact shape of a homology group.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

What a kernel is, and why "ideal" is the right word

Aluffi's plan for the chapter, stated up front: given any ring homomorphism f : R → S, factor it as

R → R′ → S′ → S

with the first map a surjection, the last an injection, and the middle one an isomorphism. Half of that is already in hand. — Aluffi §5.1, p. 78

Proposition 5.1. If f : R → S is a ring homomorphism, then im f = f(R) is a subring of S. — Aluffi §5.1, Proposition 5.1, p. 78
Proof. f(R) contains 1S = f(1R) by Definition 4.15 and 0S = f(0R) by Proposition 4.16. If s₁ = f(r₁) and s₂ = f(r₂), then s₁ + s₂ = f(r₁ + r₂) and s₁s₂ = f(r₁r₂) are again in f(R); and −f(r) = f(−r). Now apply aa-07's Proposition 4.14. ∎

So S′ = im f and the last arrow is the inclusion, which aa-08 already knows is an injective homomorphism. The hard half is the first arrow, and it starts by asking which elements f fails to distinguish: f(a₁) = f(a₂) ⟺ f(a₂ − a₁) = 0. — Aluffi §5.2, p. 79

Definition 5.3 (kernel). For a ring homomorphism f : R → S, the kernel is ker f = { r ∈ R : f(r) = 0 }. — Aluffi §5.2, Definition 5.3, p. 79

With it, the relation "f cannot tell a₁ from a₂" becomes   a₁ ∼ a₂ ⟺ a₂ − a₁ ∈ ker f. — Aluffi §5.2, p. 79

Two kernels you already have.

Example 5.4. For φ : ℤ → ℤ/nℤ, φ(a) = [a]n:   [a]n = 0 ⟺ a ∈ nℤ, so ker φ = nℤ. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.4, p. 79

Example 5.5. For e₀ : R[x] → R, e₀(f) = f(0): since f(0) = a₀, the kernel is the set of polynomials with no constant term. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.5, p. 79

Proposition 5.6. For a ring homomorphism f, the pair (ker f, +) satisfies axioms (i)–(iv) of Definition 3.1. — Aluffi §5.2, Proposition 5.6, p. 80

Proposition 5.7 (absorption). For all a ∈ ker f and all r ∈ R:   ra ∈ ker f and ar ∈ ker f. — Aluffi §5.2, Proposition 5.7, p. 80

Proofs. For 5.6: f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b) = 0 + 0 = 0 gives closure; f(0) = 0 gives 0 ∈ ker f; f(−a) = −f(a) = 0 gives negatives. Associativity and commutativity of + are universally quantified in R, so they hold in any closed subset — aa-07's argument again. ∎

For 5.7: f(ar) = f(a)f(r) = 0·f(r) = 0, by aa-05's Corollary 3.18; and f(ra) = f(r)·0 = 0 likewise. ∎ — Aluffi §5.2, p. 80

Read absorption carefully — it is stronger than closure. Closure under · would say: the product of two kernel elements is in the kernel. Absorption says: the product of a kernel element with anything at all in R is in the kernel. The kernel does not merely fail to escape; it swallows. That asymmetry is the whole reason a kernel is not a subring, and it is exactly what Segment 3 will consume.

Definition 5.8 (ideal). A subset I of a ring R is an ideal if it is closed under + and satisfies axioms (i)–(iv) of Definition 3.1 together with the absorption property: for all a ∈ I and all r ∈ R, ar ∈ I and ra ∈ I. — Aluffi §5.2, Definition 5.8, p. 80

Strictly these are two-sided ideals. Absorbing only on the left gives a left-ideal, only on the right a right-ideal; this text uses two-sided throughout, and for commutative rings the distinction is immaterial. — Aluffi §5.2, pp. 80–81

Proposition 5.9. A nonempty subset I of a ring R is an ideal if and only if it is closed under addition and satisfies absorption. — Aluffi §5.2, Proposition 5.9, p. 81
Proof of the substantive direction. Assume I is nonempty, closed under +, and absorbing. Axioms (i) and (iv) are universal in R, hence inherited. For (ii): I contains some element a, so by absorption it contains 0·a = 0. For (iii): given a ∈ I, absorption gives (−1)·a = −a ∈ I. ∎ — Aluffi §5.2, p. 81

Compare Proposition 4.14 for subrings: there you had to check the existential axioms by hand, because closure under · gave you nothing about 0 or negatives. Here absorption is strong enough to produce both. Two clauses replace four.

Self-check 1. What does absorption say that closure under multiplication does not?

Closure asks only that I·I ⊆ I. Absorption asks that R·I ⊆ I and I·R ⊆ I — a much larger demand, and one that no subring of a nontrivial ring can meet unless it is the whole ring (if 1 ∈ I then r = r·1 ∈ I for every r). Ideals and subrings are genuinely different kinds of substructure, and only one of them is what a kernel is. — Aluffi §5.2, Definition 5.8, p. 80

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Where ideals come from, and the principal ones

The standing examples.

5.10. Propositions 5.6 and 5.7 together say: every kernel is an ideal. Aluffi previews the converse — every ideal is a kernel — for §5.4. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.10, p. 81

5.11 and 5.12. R itself is an ideal of R (the kernel of the map to a trivial ring), and {0R} is an ideal of R (the kernel of idR). Every ring has at least these two. — Aluffi §5.2, Examples 5.11 and 5.12, p. 81

5.13 — a non-example. ℤ is not an ideal of ℚ. It is closed under + and even under ·, but absorption fails: ½ ∈ ℚ and 3 ∈ ℤ, while ½·3 = 3/2 ∉ ℤ. This is the cleanest illustration that a subring need not be an ideal. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.13, p. 81

5.14. nℤ is an ideal of ℤ — it is a kernel by Example 5.4, and directly: it is nonempty, sums of multiples of n are multiples of n, and any integer times a multiple of n is a multiple of n. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.14, p. 81

5.15. The polynomials in ℂ[x, y] with no constant term form an ideal. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.15, pp. 81–82

Proposition 5.16. Let R be a commutative ring and a ∈ R. Then (a) = { ra : r ∈ R }, the set of multiples of a, is an ideal of R. — Aluffi §5.2, Proposition 5.16, p. 82

Definition 5.17. (a) is the principal ideal generated by a. More generally, for a₁, …, an ∈ R the set (a₁, …, an) = { r₁a₁ + ⋯ + rnan } is the ideal generated by a₁, …, an. — Aluffi §5.2, Definition 5.17, p. 82

Proof of Proposition 5.16. By Proposition 5.9 only two things need checking. Nonempty: a = 1·a ∈ (a). Closed under +: r₁a + r₂a = (r₁ + r₂)a. Absorbing: if b = ca then rb = r(ca) = (rc)a ∈ (a) by associativity, and br ∈ (a) because R is commutative. ∎

Aluffi flags that commutativity is necessary here, not decorative: in a noncommutative ring the left-multiples of a need not absorb on the right. — Aluffi §5.2, p. 82

Remark 5.18. If a₁, …, an lie in an ideal I, then so does every combination r₁a₁ + ⋯ + rnan, by absorption followed by closure. So (a₁, …, an) is the smallest ideal containing them. — Aluffi §5.2, Remark 5.18, p. 82

Most of Segment 2's examples are principal: R = (1), {0} = (0), and nℤ = (n). Example 5.15 is not — it is (x, y), and needs two generators.

Worked example — the ideals of ℤ are exactly the nℤ. Let I ⊆ ℤ be an ideal. If I = {0} then I = 0ℤ. Otherwise I contains a nonzero element and, being closed under negation, a positive one; let n be the least positive element of I, which exists by well-ordering.

Certainly nℤ ⊆ I, by absorption. Conversely take any m ∈ I and divide with remainder — aa-01's Theorem 1.3 — writing m = nq + r with 0 ≤ r < n. Then r = m − nq is a difference of two elements of I, so r ∈ I. But 0 ≤ r < n and n was the least positive element, so r = 0. Hence m ∈ nℤ, and I = nℤ = (n). ∎ — Aluffi §1.2, Theorem 1.3, p. 6

Every ideal of ℤ is principal. That is a special property of ℤ, not a general fact — aa-12 gives it a name (principal ideal domain) and aa-11 exhibits rings without it. Note also the shape of the argument: least positive element, then divide, exactly as in aa-02's Bézout and aa-07's characteristic.

Self-check 2. ℤ is a subring of ℚ. Is it an ideal of ℚ?

ℤ passes every closure test and still fails absorption, which needs q·a ∈ ℤ for every rational q. In fact no proper subring containing 1 can be an ideal: 1 ∈ I forces r = r·1 ∈ I for all r, so I = R. Subring and ideal are near-opposite conditions — one asks you to contain 1, the other effectively forbids it. — Aluffi §5.2, Example 5.13, p. 81

Segment 3 · ~25 min

The quotient ring R/I

Definition 5.19. Let I be an ideal of R. Define a ∼I b ⟺ b − a ∈ I; we say a is congruent to b modulo the ideal I. — Aluffi §5.3, Definition 5.19, p. 83
Proposition 5.20. ∼I is an equivalence relation. — Aluffi §5.3, Proposition 5.20, p. 83
Proof. Reflexive: a − a = 0 ∈ I. Symmetric: if b − a ∈ I then a − b = −(b − a) ∈ I. Transitive: if b − a ∈ I and c − b ∈ I then c − a = (c − b) + (b − a) ∈ I. ∎

Aluffi's own footnote to this, and it matters: the proof used only axioms (i)–(iv) — absorption was never needed. So the equivalence relation, the classes, and the addition on classes all work for any additively-closed subgroup. Absorption is being held in reserve, and Lemma 5.25 is where it gets spent. — Aluffi §5.3, p. 83

Example 5.21. With R = ℤ and I = nℤ, a ∼I b is exactly a ≡ b mod n. Everything in aa-03 was this construction in one instance. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.21, p. 84

Definition 5.22. The class of a is written a + I and called the coset of a modulo I; the set of cosets is R/I, the quotient of R modulo I. — Aluffi §5.3, Definition 5.22, p. 84

Remark 5.23. The notation is literal: a + I = { a + r : r ∈ I } as a subset of R. In particular I = 0 + I is itself a coset, so I is an element of R/I. And the old warning returns unchanged: a + I is both a subset of R and a single element of R/I, exactly as [a]n was. In the new notation [a]n is a + nℤ. — Aluffi §5.3, Remark 5.23, p. 84

Definition 5.24. For cosets a + I and b + I,

(a + I) + (b + I) := (a + b) + I,    (a + I)·(b + I) := (a·b) + I.

— Aluffi §5.3, Definition 5.24, p. 84

This is Definition 2.10 with I in place of nℤ, and it raises the same question in the same place: the recipe names representatives, so is it well defined? — Aluffi §2.3, Definition 2.10, p. 27

Lemma 5.25. Let I be an ideal of R and a, a′, b, b′ ∈ R with a′ − a ∈ I and b′ − b ∈ I. Then (i) (a′ + b′) − (a + b) ∈ I, and (ii) a′b′ − ab ∈ I. — Aluffi §5.3, Lemma 5.25, p. 85
Proof of (ii) — where absorption is spent. Write a′ − a = i and b′ − b = j with i, j ∈ I. Then

a′b′ − ab = (a + i)(b + j) − ab = aj + ib + ij.

Now look at the three terms. aj is an element of I multiplied by an arbitrary a ∈ R — in I by absorption. ib likewise, on the other side. ij is in I by absorption too (or by closure). Their sum is in I because I is closed under +. So a′b′ − ab ∈ I. ∎ — Aluffi §5.3, p. 85

This is the answer to "why ideals?". Nothing weaker works: aj and ib are products of an ideal element with an element of R that is under no control whatsoever, and only absorption can place them back inside I. Mere closure under multiplication would leave both terms loose, and the product of cosets would depend on the representatives — precisely the collapse of aa-04's Example 2.11.

Theorem 5.26. For an ideal I of a ring R, (R/I, +, ·) is a ring, with 0R/I = 0 + I = I and 1R/I = 1 + I. — Aluffi §5.3, Theorem 5.26, p. 85
Proof. All the difficulty was in the definition; the axioms are routine transfers from R. For instance axiom (iii): given a + I, the coset (−a) + I satisfies (a + I) + ((−a) + I) = (a − a) + I = 0 + I. Every other axiom goes the same way, by picking representatives, using the axiom in R, and pushing back down. ∎ — Aluffi §5.3, p. 85

Example 5.27. ℤ/nℤ is R/I with R = ℤ, I = nℤ — and now n may be any integer, since (−n)ℤ = nℤ. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.27, pp. 85–86

Examples 5.28, 5.29. At the extremes: R/R is the trivial ring (one coset), and R/(0) ≅ R. — Aluffi §5.3, Examples 5.28 and 5.29, p. 86

Example 5.31 — the quotient can be worse than the ring. ℤ is an integral domain, but ℤ/4ℤ is not. Passing to a quotient does not preserve being a domain. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.31, p. 86

Below: cosets of a principal ideal drawn inside a ring. The grid is the Gaussian integers ℤ[i] from aa-07; the ideal (z) = { wz : w ∈ ℤ[i] } is one colour, and each translate a + (z) is another. The cosets tile the plane without overlapping — Proposition 5.20 made visible — and the number of colours is the size of the quotient ring ℤ[i]/(z).

Faded example — Example 5.32: identify ℤ[x]/(x).

Take f(x) = a₀ + a₁x + ⋯ + anxn. Then f(x) − a₀ = (a₁ + a₂x + ⋯ + anxn−1)·x, which lies in (x). So as cosets, f(x) + (x) = ⟨your step: which constant?⟩ + (x). Every coset therefore has an integer representative.

Define φ : ℤ → ℤ[x]/(x) by φ(a) = a + (x). The previous paragraph says φ is ⟨your step: injective or surjective?⟩.

For the other half, suppose φ(a) = φ(b). Then b − a is a multiple of x. But b − a is an integer, and the only integer that is a multiple of x is ⟨your step⟩. So a = b, and φ is injective.

Finally φ is a homomorphism: φ(1) = 1 + (x) = 1ℤ[x]/(x), and φ(a + b) = (a + b) + (x) = (a + (x)) + (b + (x)), with multiplication identical. Conclusion: ℤ[x]/(x) ≅ ℤ, and the same argument gives R[x]/(x) ≅ R for every ring R. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.32, pp. 86–87

Example 5.34 — ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) ≅ ℂ. Every coset can be written uniquely as a + bx + (x² + 1), so cosets correspond to pairs (a, b). Addition is componentwise. Multiplication is where the ideal does something:

(a₁ + b₁x)(a₂ + b₂x) = a₁a₂ + (a₁b₂ + a₂b₁)x + b₁b₂x²,

and the leftover x² term can be reduced, because b₁b₂x² − (−b₁b₂) = b₁b₂(x² + 1) lies in the ideal — so b₁b₂x² + (x² + 1) = −b₁b₂ + (x² + 1). Hence

(a₁, b₁)·(a₂, b₂) = (a₁a₂ − b₁b₂,   a₁b₂ + a₂b₁),

which is exactly the multiplication rule for complex numbers. So the quotient is ℂ. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.34, pp. 87–88

What just happened. Quotienting by (x² + 1) is declaring x² + 1 = 0, i.e. adjoining a square root of −1 — and the coset x + (x² + 1) is that root. Note the contrast with Example 5.31: there the quotient of a domain failed to be a domain; here the quotient of a domain is a field in the sense of Definition 3.29. Which of the two happens is decided entirely by the ideal, and aa-11 is about telling them apart. — Aluffi §3.3, Definition 3.29, p. 55

(b) Compute ℝ[x]/(x² − 1) by the method of Example 5.34: cosets are a + bx again, but now x² reduces to +1 rather than −1. Multiply out, and then check whether your answer has zero-divisors. (It does; find two. Compare Example 5.31 and ask what is different about x² + 1.)

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Example 5.33 generalises the faded example and Aluffi ends it with "Please do this." Let R be a commutative ring and r ∈ R. Prove that R[x]/(x − r) ≅ R. Three steps: show every coset has a constant representative, define φ : R → R[x]/(x − r) by φ(a) = a + (x − r), and prove φ is a bijective homomorphism. For the first step, Aluffi's hint is a change of variable x = t + r.

Reveal model answer

Every coset has a constant representative. Put g(t) = f(t + r), a polynomial in t. As in Example 5.32, g(t) − g(0) is a multiple of t. Substituting back t = x − r gives f(x) − f(r) ∈ (x − r), hence the equality of cosets

f(x) + (x − r) = f(r) + (x − r),

and f(r) ∈ R. So every element of R[x]/(x − r) is of the form a + (x − r) with a ∈ R.

φ is surjective. That is exactly the previous paragraph.

φ is injective. Suppose a + (x − r) = b + (x − r) with a, b ∈ R. Then b − a is a multiple of x − r, say b − a = (x − r)h(x). Comparing degrees: the left side is a constant, and if h ≠ 0 the right side has degree at least 1. So h = 0 and b − a = 0. (Equivalently: evaluate at x = r, which sends the right side to 0.)

φ is a homomorphism. φ(1) = 1 + (x − r) = 1R[x]/(x−r); and φ(a + b) = (a + b) + (x − r) = (a + (x − r)) + (b + (x − r)) = φ(a) + φ(b), with the multiplicative line identical by Definition 5.24.

So φ is a bijective ring homomorphism: R[x]/(x − r) ≅ R. ∎

Grade yourself on injectivity — it is the only step with content, and "b − a is a multiple of x − r and also a constant" needs an actual reason, either a degree count or an evaluation. Aluffi says this fact is used frequently in concrete examples and is worth memorising: killing x − r is the same as substituting r. aa-10 will explain why, by identifying the map as evaluation and the ideal as its kernel.

Self-check 3. Which part of Lemma 5.25 requires the absorption property?

Part (i) uses only that I is closed under + and negation. Part (ii) expands a′b′ − ab = aj + ib + ij, where a and b are arbitrary elements of R and i, j ∈ I — and only absorption puts aj and ib back into I. Aluffi flags this deliberately, having already noted that Proposition 5.20 needs no absorption at all. Absorption exists to make multiplication descend to the quotient, and nothing else. — Aluffi §5.3, Lemma 5.25, p. 85

Self-check 4. ℤ is an integral domain. Must ℤ/I be one for every ideal I?

ℤ/4ℤ is a quotient of a domain by a principal ideal and has zero-divisors. So neither "domain" nor "principal" is enough — what decides the matter is a property of the ideal itself. ℤ/pℤ is a domain for prime p, and ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) is even a field; ℝ[x]/(x² − 1) is not a domain. aa-11 names the two conditions on I that make the quotient a domain or a field. — Aluffi §5.3, Example 5.31, p. 86

Self-check 5. In R/I, what is the zero element?

0R/I = 0 + I, and as a subset of R that coset is exactly I. So the ideal you divided by becomes the single element 0 of the quotient — which is what "declaring the elements of I to be zero" means precisely. This is the double vision of Remark 5.23: I is simultaneously a subset of R and one point of R/I. — Aluffi §5.3, Theorem 5.26 and Remark 5.23, pp. 84–85

Then check yourself
  1. A kernel is not a subring — it is an ideal. ker f is closed under + with 0 and negatives (Proposition 5.6) and satisfies absorption: r·(kernel element) is in the kernel for every r ∈ R (Proposition 5.7). It misses 1. Definition 5.8 abstracts exactly those two properties, and Proposition 5.9 reduces the check to nonempty + closed under + + absorbing.
  2. Ideals are plentiful and often principal. {0} and R always; nℤ = (n) in ℤ, and by well-ordering plus division with remainder every ideal of ℤ is of this form; (x, y) ⊆ ℂ[x, y] needs two generators. ℤ ⊆ ℚ is a subring that is not an ideal — absorption fails.
  3. R/I is a ring (Theorem 5.26). The relation b − a ∈ I is an equivalence relation using only the additive axioms (Proposition 5.20); addition of cosets needs no more; but multiplication of cosets is well defined only because of absorption (Lemma 5.25(ii), where the terms aj and ib appear). That single line is the reason ideals are the right objects to quotient by.
And the two computations to keep: R[x]/(x − r) ≅ R — killing x − r is substituting r — and ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) ≅ ℂ, where quotienting creates a square root of −1. A quotient is not only a collapse; it is a construction.
