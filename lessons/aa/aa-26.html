Normality and quotient groups (aa-26)

Which subgroups can you divide by? The quotient move in its non-abelian final form. (Compare aa-09, aa-16.)

Three times now you have built a quotient. aa-09 divided a ring by an ideal, aa-16 divided a module by a submodule, and both times the recipe was the same and the answer was "any submodule will do, any ideal will do". Run the recipe on a group and it can break. Not for every group — but the moment commutativity goes, the guarantee goes with it. This unit is about exactly what breaks, exactly which subgroups escape, and why the escaping ones are precisely the kernels. (Non-commutativity is necessary for the failure, not sufficient: the quaternion group Q₈ is non-abelian and every one of its six subgroups is normal, so its quotients all exist. What non-commutativity removes is the guarantee.)

Predict before we start. In aa-16 you could form M/N for any submodule N. For a group G and any subgroup K, can you always make the set of cosets into a group?

The obstruction is that a group has two ways of forming cosets, aK and Ka, and they need not agree. When they disagree, the coset multiplication (aK)(bK) = (ab)K is not even well defined, so there is nothing to check associativity of. Finiteness has nothing to do with it: the smallest counterexample has six elements, and every abelian group of every size is fine. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 255

Why this matters for the mission: Hn is a quotient group; the third and deepest rehearsal of homology's defining construction.

25 min Segment 1 — Two relations where there used to be one

Aluffi sets up the canonical decomposition of a group homomorphism exactly as §5.1 did for rings and §8.4 for modules — a surjection, then an isomorphism, then an injection — and then warns that the discussion "will be quite a bit more involved than the analogous discussion for modules". — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 254 Here is where the extra involvement comes from.

Given φ : G → H, the relation to quotient by is a ∼ b when φ(a) = φ(b). Rewrite it. Since φ(a) = φ(b) exactly when φ(a⁻¹b) = e, and that happens exactly when a⁻¹b lies in the kernel:

Definition 11.49. The kernel of a group homomorphism φ : G → H is ker φ = {g in G : φ(g) = eH}. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Definition 11.49, p. 254

aa-23 already showed ker φ is a subgroup, and φ is injective exactly when the kernel is trivial. So far this is aa-16 word for word. Now generalise: for any subgroup K, define a ∼′K b to mean a⁻¹b lies in K. It is an equivalence relation, and its classes are easy to identify — the class of a is aK = {ak : k in K}, the left coset. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 254

Definition 11.50. For a subgroup K of G, the notation G/K denotes the set of left cosets of K in G. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Definition 11.50, p. 254

Note what that definition carefully does not say: it makes G/K a set, not a group. And now rewind. The very same relation ∼ can be rewritten a second way, because φ(a) = φ(b) also happens exactly when φ(ba⁻¹) = e, that is when ba⁻¹ lies in the kernel. Generalise that to an arbitrary subgroup and you get a different relation, a ∼″K b meaning ba⁻¹ is in K, whose classes are the right cosets Ka. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 255

In a ring or a module the operation was written +, and a + I is a + I whichever side you write it on. The two rewritings collapse into one, and the whole difficulty of this unit disappears — which is why aa-09 and aa-16 never mentioned it. For a group they do not collapse.

Example 11.51 — the obstruction, in six elements. Take G = D6, the symmetries of the equilateral triangle, in the notation of aa-25: x the flip, y the rotation, with xy = y⁻¹x. Let K = ⟨x⟩ = {e, x} and a = y. Then

aK = {y, yx}, Ka = {y, xy}.

By Proposition 11.35, xy = y⁻¹x, which is not yx. So aK ≠ Ka. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.51, p. 255

In cycle notation on the three vertices this is concrete: with x = (2 3) and y = (1 2 3), the products are yx = (1 2) and xy = (1 3), genuinely different transpositions. The left cosets partition D6 as {e, (2 3)}, {(1 2), (1 2 3)}, {(1 3 2), (1 3)}; the right cosets partition it as {e, (2 3)}, {(1 2), (1 3 2)}, {(1 2 3), (1 3)}. Two partitions of the same six elements, agreeing on one block and disagreeing on the other two.

Now the argument that turns this from a curiosity into a prohibition. Suppose we did manage to make G/K a group in such a way that the projection π : G → G/K is a homomorphism. Then K would be ker π. But for a kernel the two rewritings above are both descriptions of the same relation ∼, so left and right cosets of a kernel must coincide. Since they do not coincide for ⟨x⟩ in D6, no such group structure exists. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 255

So the conclusion is not that the construction is harder for groups. It is that for general groups it is impossible for some subgroups, and the subgroups it works for need a name.

Definition 11.52. A subgroup K of G is normal if gK = Kg for all g in G, or equivalently gKg⁻¹ = K for all g in G. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Definition 11.52, p. 255

Two immediate riders, both of which people get wrong.

Remark 11.53. To verify normality it suffices to show gKg⁻¹ is contained in K for every g. If that holds for all g it holds for g⁻¹ too, and g⁻¹Kg ⊆ K is equivalent to K ⊆ gKg⁻¹; both inclusions give equality. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.53, p. 255

Remark 11.54 — the caveat. gK = Kg does not mean gk = kg for every k in K. It means that for every k there is some k′ in K with gk = k′g. Normality is the statement that for all k in K and all g in G there exists k′ in K with gkg⁻¹ = k′, and in general k′ ≠ k. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.54, p. 255 Normality is a condition on the subgroup as a set, not on its elements one at a time. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 256

Self-check 1. Why does this difficulty not arise for modules in aa-16?

The underlying abelian group of a module is commutative, so a + N = N + a automatically and the two rewritings of the relation coincide. Aluffi makes the same point about groups directly: if G is commutative then gkg⁻¹ = gg⁻¹k = k, so every subgroup of a commutative group is normal, "and this is why we did not have to face this issue when we dealt with abelian groups". — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 256

25 min Segment 2 — Recognising normality

Definition 11.52 is a condition to be checked for every g, which is impractical. Two better tests follow, one conceptual and one that is almost always the fastest in practice.

Proposition 11.57. A subgroup K of G is normal if and only if it is a union of conjugacy classes. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Proposition 11.57, p. 256

Conjugacy classes are aa-24's Definition 11.31: the orbits of G acting on itself by conjugation. Aluffi's proof sets K̂ to be the union of the conjugacy classes of the elements of K, notes K ⊆ K̂ because every element is conjugate to itself, and shows K is normal exactly when K̂ ⊆ K — the forward direction because h = gkg⁻¹ lies in gKg⁻¹ ⊆ K, the reverse because gKg⁻¹ ⊆ K̂ ⊆ K for every g, which suffices by Remark 11.53. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 256

This is a genuinely useful constraint rather than a restatement. Conjugacy classes partition G, so a normal subgroup's size must be a sum of conjugacy-class sizes, one of which is 1 for the identity. Knowing the class sizes of a group therefore restricts which orders a normal subgroup can have before you look at a single subgroup.

Proposition 11.58. If φ : G → H is a group homomorphism then ker φ is a normal subgroup of G. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Proposition 11.58, p. 256

Proof. For g in G and k in ker φ, φ(gkg⁻¹) = φ(g)φ(k)φ(g)⁻¹ = φ(g)eHφ(g)⁻¹ = eH, so gkg⁻¹ is in ker φ. That gives g(ker φ)g⁻¹ ⊆ ker φ for all g, which suffices by Remark 11.53. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 257

Aluffi states the practical consequence plainly: often the simplest way to verify that K is normal is to realise it as the kernel of a homomorphism out of G. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 257 Three of the examples below do exactly that.

Example 11.59 — index two. Suppose there are exactly two left cosets, G/K = {K, aK}. Then K is automatically normal. The proof uses Remark 11.56, which says that whether or not K is normal, G acts on the set of left cosets by g·aK := (ga)K — an action defined for any subgroup, and one that will reappear in the proof of the Sylow theorems. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.56, p. 256 With only two cosets that action is a homomorphism φ : G → S2, and Aluffi checks both inclusions to show ker φ = K exactly, so K is a kernel and hence normal. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.59, p. 257

Example 11.60 — the centre. Z(G) is the set of elements commuting with everything. It is a subgroup, and it is normal for a one-line reason: gzg⁻¹ = zgg⁻¹ = z for every z in Z(G), so gZ(G)g⁻¹ ⊆ Z(G). — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.60, p. 257 Notice this is the rare case where the naive reading of Remark 11.54 is correct — but only because the elements of Z(G) were chosen to commute.

Example 11.61 — inside the dihedral group. D2n for n ≥ 3 contains one subgroup isomorphic to Cn — the rotations — and n subgroups isomorphic to C2, generated by the individual flips. The flips are not normal: with F = {e, x}, the computation yxy⁻¹ = xy⁻¹y⁻¹ = xy⁻² lands outside F. The rotation subgroup K = {e, y, …, yn−1} is normal. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.61, p. 257

Aluffi checks the rotations directly. Write g = xⁱyʲ by Proposition 11.35. If i = 0 then g is in K and gKg⁻¹ = K. If i = 1 then gykg⁻¹ = xyʲykyn−jx = xykx = y⁻kxx = y⁻k, which is in K. He then gives the shortcut: define φ : D2n → C2 by sending g to e if it does not flip the n-gon and to f if it does; the rotations are the kernel, so they are normal by Proposition 11.58. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 258

Example 11.62 — the alternating group. An is normal in Sn, by definition the kernel of the sign homomorphism of aa-25's Definition 11.39. For n = 3 it is the copy of C3 inside D6 ≅ S3; A4 is the rotation group of the regular tetrahedron and A5 the rotation group of the icosahedron. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.62, p. 258

Example 11.63 — the Klein four-group. Inside A4 sits V = {e, (1 2)(3 4), (1 3)(2 4), (1 4)(2 3)}, isomorphic to C2 × C2. It is normal in A4; Aluffi's sample conjugation is (1 2 3)(1 2)(3 4)(3 2 1) = (1 4)(2 3), which is back in V. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Example 11.63, p. 258 Proposition 11.57 explains why: V is exactly the identity together with the whole conjugacy class of double transpositions, so it is a union of conjugacy classes on the nose.

The five reasons
  1. ⟨x⟩ in D6: not normal — this is Example 11.51 itself, and yxy⁻¹ = xy⁻² escapes, as in Example 11.61.
  2. ⟨y⟩ in D6: normal — the rotations are the kernel of the flip-or-not homomorphism to C2, Example 11.61. Two cosets, so Example 11.59 also settles it.
  3. A4 in S4: normal — the kernel of the sign homomorphism, Example 11.62. Again exactly two cosets, even and odd.
  4. V in A4: normal — a union of conjugacy classes by Proposition 11.57, namely the identity's class and the class of double transpositions. Not an index-two subgroup: 4 into 12 is index 3, so Example 11.59 does not apply and Proposition 11.57 is doing real work.
  5. ⟨(1 2)⟩ in S4: not normal — a single transposition is not a whole conjugacy class, since (1 3)(1 2)(1 3) = (2 3) is a conjugate lying outside. Proposition 11.57 rules it out immediately.

Self-check 2. Which statement does normality of K actually assert?

This is Remark 11.54, the caveat Aluffi writes out precisely because the first option is the standard misreading. The rotation subgroup of D2n is normal, yet x does not commute with y: conjugating y by x gives y⁻¹, a different element of K. Normality says the set K is preserved by conjugation, not that its elements are fixed. Subgroups whose elements are individually fixed are subgroups of the centre — Example 11.60 — which is a far stronger condition. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.54, p. 255

Self-check 3. What is the quickest way to prove a given subgroup is normal, in practice?

Aluffi says so outright: often the simplest way to verify K is normal is to realise it as the kernel of a homomorphism from G to some other group, which forces normality by Proposition 11.58. The rotations in D2n and An in Sn are both settled this way in a line. Being abelian is irrelevant — ⟨x⟩ in D6 is abelian and not normal. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 257

25 min Segment 3 — Normality is the only obstruction

Everything so far has been the bad news. Proposition 11.58 said that if a quotient exists then K is normal. The theorem says the converse: normality is not merely necessary, it is sufficient, and nothing else is required.

Theorem 11.64. Let K be a normal subgroup of G. Then aK = Ka for all a; the relations ∼′K and ∼″K coincide; and the set G/K of left cosets is a group under (aK)·(bK) := (ab)K. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Theorem 11.64, p. 258

The first point restates normality. The second follows, because left cosets are the classes of ∼′K and right cosets the classes of ∼″K, and an equivalence relation is determined by its classes — aa-03's fact, used here to identify two relations rather than to build one. The third is the only point with content, and it is entirely about well-definedness. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 259

Worked, then faded — the well-definedness computation. The rule (aK)(bK) := (ab)K names the product using representatives a and b. To be a definition at all, it must not depend on which representatives you picked. So suppose aK = a′K and bK = b′K, which means a⁻¹a′ is in K and b⁻¹b′ is in K, and we must show (ab)K = (a′b′)K, that is, (ab)⁻¹(a′b′) is in K.

Aluffi's manipulation, in full: (ab)⁻¹(a′b′) = b⁻¹a⁻¹a′b′ = b⁻¹a⁻¹a′(bb⁻¹)b′ = (b⁻¹a⁻¹a′b)(b⁻¹b′). — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 259

Now fade it. The second factor b⁻¹b′ lies in K by assumption. The first factor is b⁻¹ times ⟨?⟩ times b, where the middle element lies in K — so the first factor lies in the set ⟨?⟩, which equals K precisely because ⟨?⟩. Hence the product lies in K.

CheckThe middle element is a⁻¹a′, which lies in K by assumption. So the first factor lies in b⁻¹Kb, and b⁻¹Kb = K precisely because K is normal — this is Definition 11.52 applied with g = b⁻¹. Both factors are in K, K is closed under multiplication, so the product is in K and (ab)K = (a′b′)K.

That single step is where the whole unit lands. Every other line of the proof would go through for an arbitrary subgroup; normality is used exactly once, and exactly there.

The rest is routine: associativity is inherited from G, eK = K is the identity, and (aK)(a⁻¹K) = (aa⁻¹)K = K gives inverses. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 259

Definition 11.65. For K normal in G, the quotient G/K is the group of cosets of K with the operation (aK)(bK) := (ab)K. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Definition 11.65, p. 259

The projection π : G → G/K is then a surjective homomorphism straight from the definition of the product, since π(ab) = (ab)K = (aK)(bK) = π(a)π(b), and its kernel is {a : aK = K} = K. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 259 Which closes the circle:

Remark 11.66. The notions of kernel and of normal subgroup coincide. Kernels are normal by Proposition 11.58, and every normal subgroup is the kernel of its own projection. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.66, p. 259

That is the third instance of a pattern you have now seen three times. In rings, kernels and ideals coincide; in modules, kernels and submodules coincide; in groups, kernels and normal subgroups coincide. Each time, "the subobjects you can quotient by" and "the subobjects that arise as kernels" turn out to be the same class, and each time the quotient construction is what proves it. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, p. 259

Guided proof. Prove that a subgroup K of index 2 in G is normal — Example 11.59 — but without using the action on cosets. Argue directly from the partition instead. Two hints: the two left cosets are K and G minus K, and the same is true of the two right cosets.
Model answer

Case 1: g in K. Then gK = K, since gK ⊆ K by closure and k = g(g⁻¹k) shows K ⊆ gK. Symmetrically Kg = K. So gK = Kg.

Case 2: g not in K. The left cosets partition G — that is what makes them the classes of an equivalence relation — and there are exactly two of them, one of which is K. So the other is G \ K, and since g is not in K its coset gK must be that one: gK = G \ K. Now run the identical argument for right cosets: they also partition G, there are also exactly two, one is K, so the other is G \ K, and Kg = G \ K. Hence gK = Kg.

Both cases give gK = Kg, so K is normal by Definition 11.52. Note what the argument used: only that a partition into two blocks with one block known determines the other. It never touched the group operation beyond closure, which is why the result feels almost too cheap — and why Aluffi says Lagrange's theorem will make it simpler still, since index two is a statement about orders.

Self-check 4. Where exactly is normality used in the proof of Theorem 11.64?

Associativity is inherited from G, the identity and inverses are immediate, and all three would work for any subgroup. The entire weight falls on showing (ab)K depends only on aK and bK, and there the factorisation (ab)⁻¹(a′b′) = (b⁻¹a⁻¹a′b)(b⁻¹b′) produces a conjugate of an element of K, which is required to land back in K. That is Definition 11.52 and nothing else. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Theorem 11.64, p. 258

Self-check 5. Aluffi's Remark 11.66 concludes something about kernels. What?

Both directions are now available: Proposition 11.58 makes every kernel normal, and Definition 11.65 with its projection makes every normal subgroup a kernel. Uniqueness is not claimed and is false — K is the kernel of its projection and also of anything obtained by composing that projection with an injective homomorphism. The point is the coincidence of the two classes of subgroup, matching ideals in rings and submodules in modules. — Aluffi Underground §11.4, Remark 11.66, p. 259

Then check yourself
  1. The obstruction. φ(a) = φ(b) can be rewritten with a⁻¹b or with ba⁻¹ in the kernel (Definition 11.49). Generalised to an arbitrary subgroup these give different relations, whose classes are left cosets aK (Definition 11.50) and right cosets Ka. In D6 with K = ⟨x⟩ and a = y they genuinely differ, since xy = y⁻¹x ≠ yx — Example 11.51. A quotient group would make K a kernel, and for a kernel the two agree, so no quotient by ⟨x⟩ exists.
  2. The definition. K is normal when gK = Kg for all g, equivalently gKg⁻¹ = K (Definition 11.52). One inclusion suffices (Remark 11.53), and it does not mean elements of K commute with elements of G (Remark 11.54).
  3. The tests. Normal exactly when K is a union of conjugacy classes (Proposition 11.57), which constrains its possible size; and every kernel is normal (Proposition 11.58), which is usually the fast route. Worked cases: index two via the action on cosets (Remark 11.56 and Example 11.59), the centre (Example 11.60), the rotations but not the flips in D2n (Example 11.61), An in Sn (Example 11.62), the Klein four-group in A4 (Example 11.63).
  4. The theorem. Normality is sufficient: G/K is a group under (aK)(bK) = (ab)K (Theorem 11.64, Definition 11.65), with normality used at exactly one step, b⁻¹Kb = K. The projection is surjective with kernel K, so kernels and normal subgroups are the same thing (Remark 11.66).
The mission strip is item 4 read topologically. A homology group Hn is ker ∂n modulo im ∂n+1 — a quotient, and the third and deepest rehearsal of the move you have now made for rings, for modules, and for groups. The reason that quotient is always legal is that chain groups are abelian, so Self-check 1 applies and every subgroup is normal; the non-abelian difficulty of this unit is precisely what does not arise there. But it arises immediately in homotopy, where π₁ is not abelian, normal subgroups are exactly what covering spaces classify, and aa-25's free groups are the objects being quotiented.
