Isomorphism theorems for modules (aa-17)

aa-10 gave you a First Isomorphism Theorem and a Third. It never gave you a Second. There is a reason, and it is not that the Second is hard.

It is that the Second Isomorphism Theorem was never a statement about rings at all — it is a statement about modules, and could not be said until modules existed. Today all three theorems appear together in their proper home, and each one is easier here than its ring counterpart. The First identifies every quotient; the Third organises the submodules of a quotient; and the Second relates two submodules that need not contain one another. Those three identities are what every Betti-number computation actually runs on.

Predict before we start. Why did aa-10 state a First and a Third Isomorphism Theorem for rings, but no Second?

Aluffi is explicit: for ideals I, J of a ring R, Theorem 8.48 does give J/(I ∩ J) ≅ (I + J)/I — and that is a useful statement — but it is an isomorphism of R-modules, not of rings. Neither side is a ring: J/(I ∩ J) is a quotient of one non-unital object by another. So the theorem is true and could not have been stated in the ring chapter without saying something it was not yet able to say. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 171

Why this matters for the mission: Second pass at the iso theorems; these are the identities behind every Betti computation.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

First: every quotient, and every finitely generated module

aa-16 assembled the canonical decomposition; the last thing to check was that the middle map is R-linear, and it is one line in each clause: — Aluffi §8.4, p. 168

f̃(r·(m + ker f)) = f̃(r·m + ker f) = f(r·m) = r·f(m) = r·f̃(m + ker f),

with the additive clause identical. So Theorem 8.43 holds: every R-module homomorphism factors as surjection, isomorphism, injection.

Theorem 8.44 (First Isomorphism Theorem). If f : M → N is a surjective R-module homomorphism, then N ≅ M/ker f, via f̃(m + ker f) = f(m). — Aluffi §8.5, Theorem 8.44, p. 168

Proof: the canonical decomposition gives M/ker f ≅ im f, and surjectivity makes im f = N. What the theorem says, in Aluffi's framing, is that the prototype of a surjective module homomorphism is the projection M → M/K: every homomorphic image of M is a quotient of M by a submodule, up to isomorphism. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 168

Example 8.45 — every cyclic module is R/I. Let M = ⟨m⟩ over a commutative R. The map R → M, r ↦ r·m, is an R-module homomorphism (aa-15's Example 8.18), and it is surjective precisely because m generates M. Its kernel I is a submodule of R — which, for commutative R, is the same thing as an ideal (aa-16's Example 8.24). So by Theorem 8.44,

M ≅ R/I.

— Aluffi §8.5, Example 8.45, pp. 168–169

Over R = ℤ this says: every cyclic abelian group is ℤ/nℤ for some n ≥ 0, with n = 0 giving ℤ itself. That single sentence is what makes aa-22's classification statable.

Example 8.46 — the same idea with n generators. For any ring R, M = ⟨m₁, …, mn⟩ if and only if the map

R⊕n → M,   (r₁, …, rn) ↦ r₁m₁ + ⋯ + rnmn

is surjective — which is just Definition 8.28 rewritten. By Theorem 8.44, M ≅ R⊕n/K for some submodule K ⊆ R⊕n. — Aluffi §8.5, Example 8.46, p. 169

So "generated by n elements" and "a quotient of the free module R⊕n" are the same condition. That reformulation is the entire basis of the computational side of module theory: to describe M you record n generators and the submodule K of relations among them. aa-18 makes K a matrix, and aa-19 asks when K is itself finitely generated.

Self-check 1. M is generated by 3 elements. What does Example 8.46 let you say about M?

The map R⊕3 → M sending the standard triple to the generators is surjective, so the First Isomorphism Theorem gives M ≅ R⊕3/K with K its kernel. M itself need not be free: taking R = ℤ, M = ℤ/2ℤ is generated by one element and is ℤ/2ℤ = ℤ⊕1/2ℤ, not ℤ. The submodule K is exactly the relations the generators satisfy, and it is where all the information beyond "three generators" lives. — Aluffi §8.5, Example 8.46, p. 169

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Third: the submodules of M/K

If K ⊆ L ⊆ M are submodules, then K is a submodule of L, so L/K makes sense — and as a subset of M/K it is the set of cosets ℓ + K with ℓ ∈ L, i.e. π(L). — Aluffi §8.5, p. 169

Theorem 8.47 (Third Isomorphism Theorem). Let K ⊆ L ⊆ M be submodules. Then L/K is a submodule of M/K, every submodule of M/K is of this form, and

(M/K)/(L/K) ≅ M/L.

— Aluffi §8.5, Theorem 8.47, p. 169
Proof. Let π : M → M/K be the projection.

L/K is a submodule: it is π(L), and images of submodules are submodules — Proposition 8.32(i).

Every submodule arises this way: given a submodule L̄ ⊆ M/K, put L = π−1(L̄), a submodule by Proposition 8.32(ii), containing K = π−1(0) since 0 ∈ L̄; and π(L) = L̄.

The isomorphism: compose the two projections to get a surjection ρ : M → (M/K)/(L/K), ρ(m) = (m + K) + L/K. Then

ker ρ = { m : m + K ∈ L/K } = π−1(L/K) = L,

so Theorem 8.44 gives (M/K)/(L/K) ≅ M/L. ∎ — Aluffi §8.5, p. 169

Compare aa-10's Theorem 5.57 and notice what vanished. There, showing J/I was an ideal of R/I required a hand computation with absorption — the footnote in Aluffi's ring proof. Here nothing of the kind is needed: L/K is a module because it is the quotient of a module by a module, and Proposition 8.32 delivers it as an image. Aluffi's own comment is that the module version "clarifies a little" the ring statement. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Self-check 2. The submodules of M/K are in bijection with which submodules of M?

L ↦ L/K and L̄ ↦ π−1(L̄) are mutually inverse between {submodules L with K ⊆ L ⊆ M} and {submodules of M/K}, because π−1 of anything containing 0 must contain π−1(0) = K. Quotienting by K discards precisely the submodules that do not contain K — which is aa-10's dictionary, and is how ℤ/12ℤ was found to have six ideals, one for each divisor of 12. — Aluffi §8.5, Theorem 8.47, p. 169

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Second: two submodules, neither inside the other

Take submodules K, L of M with no containment assumed. aa-16's Examples 8.26 and 8.27 give two more submodules for free: K + L, the smallest containing both, and K ∩ L, the largest inside both. Four modules, arranged in a diamond. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Theorem 8.48 (Second Isomorphism Theorem). For submodules K, L of an R-module M,

L/(K ∩ L) ≅ (K + L)/K,

via the well-defined isomorphism ℓ + (K ∩ L) ↦ ℓ + K. — Aluffi §8.5, Theorem 8.48, p. 170

Aluffi's mnemonic: there are as many Ks as Ls in the formula. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Proof. Define φ : L → (K + L)/K by φ(ℓ) = ℓ + K.

φ is surjective — the step Aluffi calls "maybe a little surprisingly". A general element of (K + L)/K is (k + ℓ) + K with k ∈ K, ℓ ∈ L. But (k + ℓ) − ℓ = k ∈ K, so ℓ + K = (k + ℓ) + K, and that element is φ(ℓ). The K-part of the numerator is invisible in the quotient.

ker φ = K ∩ L:   ker φ = { ℓ ∈ L : ℓ + K = K } = { ℓ ∈ L : ℓ ∈ K } = K ∩ L.

Now apply Theorem 8.44 to the surjection φ: L/ker φ ≅ (K + L)/K, i.e. L/(K ∩ L) ≅ (K + L)/K, and the induced map sends ℓ + (K ∩ L) to φ(ℓ) = ℓ + K. ∎ — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Note the pattern, for the third time today. Build one surjection, compute its kernel, quote the First Isomorphism Theorem. Theorems 8.47 and 8.48 are both that, with a different map. Aluffi even notes in a footnote that the well-definedness of the isomorphism is hidden inside the argument — the First Isomorphism Theorem supplied it, so it never had to be checked by hand.

Faded example — checking Theorem 8.48 in ℤ. Take M = ℤ, K = 4ℤ, L = 6ℤ, both submodules of ℤ (aa-16's Example 8.24: submodules of ℤ are its ideals).

By aa-10's Example 5.45, K + L = (4) + (6) = (gcd(4, 6)) = ⟨your step⟩ℤ. And by Example 5.46, K ∩ L = (lcm(4, 6)) = ⟨your step⟩ℤ.

Left-hand side: L/(K ∩ L) = 6ℤ/12ℤ. Its elements are the cosets of 12ℤ inside 6ℤ, namely 0 + 12ℤ and 6 + 12ℤ — so it has ⟨your step: how many?⟩ elements.

Right-hand side: (K + L)/K = 2ℤ/4ℤ, with cosets 0 + 4ℤ and 2 + 4ℤ — the same count.

Both are isomorphic to ℤ/2ℤ, as Theorem 8.48 promises. In ℤ the theorem is exactly the identity gcd·lcm relation from aa-02 wearing quotient notation: |L/(K ∩ L)| = lcm/L-index and |(K + L)/K| = K-index/gcd, and these agree because ⟨your step: which classical identity?⟩.

What the theorem gives back to rings. If I, J are ideals of a ring R then

J/(I ∩ J) ≅ (I + J)/I

as R-modules — useful, and unavailable in the ring chapter because neither side is a ring. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 171

Remark 8.49. Everything here applies to abelian groups without a word of extra argument: that is the case R = ℤ. So the three isomorphism theorems for abelian groups are already proved. — Aluffi §8.5, Remark 8.49, p. 171

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi's Exercise 8.19 defines the notion the mission strip is really about. Call m ∈ M a torsion element if r·m = 0 for some nonzero r ∈ R. Prove that if R is an integral domain, the torsion elements of M form a submodule T of M. Then show by explicit example what goes wrong when R has zero-divisors. Use Proposition 8.23, so you need only nonemptiness and two closures.

Reveal model answer

Nonempty. 0 ∈ T, since 1·0 = 0 and 1 ≠ 0 in a nontrivial ring.

Closed under addition. Let r·m = 0 and s·n = 0 with r, s ≠ 0. Then

(rs)·(m + n) = (rs)·m + (rs)·n = s·(r·m) + r·(s·n) = 0 + 0 = 0,

using axioms (vi) and (viii) and commutativity where needed. And rs ≠ 0 because R is an integral domain — this is the only place the hypothesis is used, and it is decisive. So m + n ∈ T.

Closed under scalars. If r·m = 0 with r ≠ 0 and a ∈ R, then r·(a·m) = (ra)·m = (ar)·m = a·(r·m) = 0, so a·m ∈ T.

By Proposition 8.23, T is a submodule. ∎

What breaks with zero-divisors. Take R = ℤ/6ℤ acting on itself. Then [2] is torsion, since [3][2] = [0] with [3] ≠ 0; and [3] is torsion, since [2][3] = [0]. But [2] + [3] = [5], and [5] is a unit in ℤ/6ℤ, so r[5] = 0 forces r = 0: [5] is not torsion. The set of torsion elements is not closed under addition, and is not a submodule.

Grade yourself on the middle step. If your proof produced an annihilator for m + n without checking that it is nonzero, it proved nothing — and the ℤ/6ℤ example is what that gap looks like when it is real. This matters beyond the exercise: over an integral domain the torsion submodule is well defined, M/T is torsion-free (Exercise 8.21), and aa-20's structure theorem is the statement that a finitely generated module over a PID splits into a free part and that torsion part. Betti numbers count the first; torsion coefficients describe the second.

Self-check 3. In the proof of Theorem 8.48, why is φ : L → (K + L)/K surjective?

A general element of (K + L)/K is (k + ℓ) + K, and its difference from ℓ + K is k ∈ K, so the two cosets are equal — meaning every element is already φ(ℓ) for some ℓ ∈ L. No containment between K and L is assumed anywhere; that is the point of this theorem as against the Third. And injectivity plays no part: the kernel is K ∩ L, which is generally nonzero. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Self-check 4. All three isomorphism theorems today were proved the same way. What way?

Theorem 8.47 composed two projections and found ker ρ = L; Theorem 8.48 used φ(ℓ) = ℓ + K and found ker φ = K ∩ L. In neither case was an inverse constructed or a well-definedness check performed by hand — the First Isomorphism Theorem supplied both, which is exactly what Aluffi's footnote to Theorem 8.48 observes. Counting would not even apply, since the modules are usually infinite. — Aluffi §8.5, pp. 169–170

Self-check 5. Why is the Third Isomorphism Theorem easier for modules than for rings?

In aa-10's Theorem 5.57, showing J/I is an ideal of R/I took a separate absorption computation, which Aluffi relegated to a footnote. Here L/K is the quotient of a module by a module and is a module for that reason alone, and Proposition 8.32(i) hands it over as an image of a submodule. Commutativity is irrelevant — modules over noncommutative rings are covered — and nothing is finite. — Aluffi §8.5, p. 170

Then check yourself
  1. First (Theorem 8.44): a surjection f : M → N gives N ≅ M/ker f. Consequences: every cyclic module is R/I (Example 8.45), so every cyclic abelian group is ℤ/nℤ; and M is generated by n elements exactly when M ≅ R⊕n/K for some submodule K of relations (Example 8.46) — the reformulation that makes modules computable.
  2. Third (Theorem 8.47): for K ⊆ L ⊆ M, (M/K)/(L/K) ≅ M/L, and the submodules of M/K are exactly the L/K for K ⊆ L ⊆ M. Easier than the ring version, because L/K is a module for free rather than an ideal to be verified.
  3. Second (Theorem 8.48): for any two submodules, L/(K ∩ L) ≅ (K + L)/K — as many Ks as Ls. It has no ring counterpart because neither side is a ring, which is why aa-10 was silent about it. Proved, like the others, by one surjection and one kernel.
The method is the content: build a surjection, compute its kernel, quote the First Isomorphism Theorem. And the object to carry forward is the torsion submodule of the guided proof — well defined exactly when R is a domain, and exactly what aa-20 will separate from the free part.
