The category of rings I: products and subrings (aa-07)

Two rings with four elements each: ℤ/4ℤ, and ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ. Same size. Are they the same ring?

No — and the way you tell is worth more than the answer. In the product, every element added to itself gives 0; in ℤ/4ℤ, [1] + [1] = [2] ≠ [0]. That is a structural difference, one that survives any relabelling of elements. Today we stop studying rings one at a time and start building new ones out of old (products, subrings), and in doing so we discover — twice, from two unrelated constructions — the same short list of properties that the "right" kind of map between rings should have. aa-08 will make that list a definition.

Predict before we start. In R × R for a nontrivial ring R, is the subset S = {(a, 0) : a ∈ R} a subring?

S is closed under both operations, contains (0, 0), and has all its negatives — it is a ring in its own right, with identity (1, 0). But a subring must share the ambient ring's 1, and 1R×R = (1, 1) ∉ S. So S is not a subring of R × R under Definition 4.5, and the diagonal Δ = {(a, a)} is — that one does contain (1, 1). This is Aluffi's Example 4.10, and it is the sharpest illustration that "looks like a copy of R" and "is a subring" are different claims. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.10, p. 62

Why this matters for the mission: The categorical habit — objects plus structure-preserving maps — that persistence lives inside.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cartesian products, and the map they come with

Aluffi frames the whole chapter by analogy. Once you had the notion of set, you built new sets from old — unions, products, quotients — and you studied functions between them. Doing both at once is what it means to study the category of sets. We now run the same programme for rings: first some constructions, then let the constructions tell us what the right notion of "function between rings" is. — Aluffi §4.1, p. 59

Definition 4.1 (Cartesian product). Let R, S be rings. Their Cartesian product is the ring R × S = {(r, s) : r ∈ R, s ∈ S} with componentwise operations:

(r₁, s₁) + (r₂, s₂) := (r₁ + r₂, s₁ + s₂),    (r₁, s₁)·(r₂, s₂) := (r₁·r₂, s₁·s₂).

— Aluffi §4.1, Definition 4.1, p. 59

Watch the symbols: + has three meanings in that display — addition in R, addition in S, and the new addition being defined. Verifying Definition 3.1 for R × S is, in Aluffi's word, uninspiring: it is the pointwise argument from aa-05, run coordinate by coordinate. The identities are 0R×S = (0R, 0S) and 1R×S = (1R, 1S). — Aluffi §4.1, p. 60

Example 4.2 — two rings of size four. In ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ,

([1]₂, [1]₂) + ([1]₂, [1]₂) = ([0]₂, [0]₂),

and the same happens for every element: x + x = 0 throughout. In ℤ/4ℤ that is false, since [1]₄ + [1]₄ = [2]₄ ≠ [0]₄. So the two rings are not the same ring wearing different labels — in the vocabulary of aa-08, they are not isomorphic. — Aluffi §4.1, Example 4.2, p. 60

Example 4.3 — and two rings of size six. Compare ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/3ℤ with ℤ/6ℤ, and look for a distinguishing feature. Aluffi's hint is one word: No. There is none; these two really are the same ring in different clothes. The reason is the Chinese Remainder Theorem, which aa-10 will meet as Theorem 5.52. — Aluffi §4.1, Example 4.3, p. 60; §5.6, Theorem 5.52, p. 97

Put Examples 4.2 and 4.3 side by side: ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ ≇ ℤ/4ℤ but ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/3ℤ ≅ ℤ/6ℤ. The difference between the two situations is gcd(2, 2) = 2 against gcd(2, 3) = 1 — exactly the coprimality condition that has been doing this work since aa-02.

Example 4.4 — ℂ is not ℝ × ℝ. Both are "the plane" as a set. But in ℝ × ℝ,

(1, 0)·(0, 1) = (0, 0)

with neither factor zero: there are nonzero zero-divisors. ℂ is a field, hence an integral domain by Proposition 3.31, so it has none. The two rings cannot be isomorphic. — Aluffi §4.1, Example 4.4, p. 60

Generalise it. That computation used nothing about ℝ: in any product of two nonzero rings, (1, 0) and (0, 1) are nonzero and multiply to (0, 0). So a product of two nonzero rings is never an integral domain — Definition 3.23 fails no matter how well behaved the factors are. Products are a machine for manufacturing zero-divisors.

Now the map. Among all functions R × S → R (with two-element factors there are 2⁴ = 16 of them) one is writeable without knowing anything about R or S: the projection — Aluffi §4.1, p. 60

π : R × S → R,    π(r, s) := r.   (4.1)

Why π is special from the ring point of view. Add first, then project:

(r₁, s₁) + (r₂, s₂) = (r₁ + r₂, s₁ + s₂) ↦ r₁ + r₂.

Project first, then add: (r₁, s₁), (r₂, s₂) ↦ r₁, r₂ ↦ r₁ + r₂. Same answer. In symbols,

π(x + y) = π(x) + π(y),    π(x·y) = π(x)·π(y),    π(1R×S) = 1R.

Three properties. Hold on to them — the whole of Segment 3 is the observation that a completely different construction produces the very same three. — Aluffi §4.1, p. 61

Self-check 1. What structural feature separates ℤ/4ℤ from ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ?

Both have four elements and both are commutative. What differs is a statement quantified over all elements — x + x = 0 holds throughout the product and fails at [1]₄ — and such a statement cannot be changed by renaming elements. That is what makes it a legitimate way to tell two rings apart. Compare Example 4.3, where no such feature exists and the two rings are the same. — Aluffi §4.1, Example 4.2, p. 60

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Subrings, and how little you actually have to check

Definition 4.5 (subring). Let R be a ring and S ⊆ R a subset with 0R ∈ S, 1R ∈ S, and S closed under both operations of R. Then S is a subring of R if all the axioms of Definition 3.1 hold for S, with 0S = 0R and 1S = 1R. — Aluffi §4.2, Definition 4.5, p. 61

In short: S is a ring in its own right, under R's operations, with R's own 0 and 1. That last clause is where the interesting failures live.

The examples, and what each one tests.

4.6 — the familiar tower. ℤ is a subring of ℚ, which is a subring of ℝ, which is a subring of ℂ. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.6, p. 61

4.7 — ℤ/2ℤ is NOT a subring of ℤ. Tempting: read [0]₂ as 0 and [1]₂ as 1. But a subring must use R's operations, and in ℤ, 1 + 1 = 2, whereas [1]₂ + [1]₂ = [0]₂. The two demands are inconsistent. The same holds for every ℤ/nℤ with n > 0 — this is aa-03's warning, now with teeth. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.7, pp. 61–62

4.9 — the diagonal. For any ring R, Δ = {(a, a) : a ∈ R} ⊆ R × R is a subring: it contains (0, 0) and (1, 1), and (a, a) + (b, b) = (a+b, a+b), (a, a)(b, b) = (ab, ab) stay diagonal. Δ is a doppelgänger of R. — Aluffi §4.2, Examples 4.8 and 4.9, p. 62

4.10 — the axis is not. S = {(a, 0)} is closed and has negatives but misses (1, 1). Not a subring. The prediction gate. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.10, p. 62

4.11 — constants in R[x]. R sits inside R[x] as the constant polynomials. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.11, p. 62

Example 4.12 — the Gaussian integers. Inside ℂ, put

ℤ[i] := { a + bi : a, b ∈ ℤ }.

Closure under + is immediate. Closure under · is the content:

(a₁ + b₁i)(a₂ + b₂i) = (a₁a₂ − b₁b₂) + (a₁b₂ + a₂b₁)i,

and both of those coefficients are integers when all four inputs are. With 0 and 1 present and negatives available, ℤ[i] is a subring of ℂ — the ring of Gaussian integers, important in number theory, and used again in §10.4. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.12, p. 62

Aluffi asks you to compare ℤ[i] with ℤ × ℤ. They have the same underlying set of pairs; but ℤ × ℤ has the zero-divisors (1, 0) and (0, 1), and ℤ[i] sits inside a field, so it has none. Same set, different multiplication, genuinely different rings.

Below is ℤ[i] as a lattice in the plane. Multiplying every point by a fixed Gaussian integer rotates and scales the whole lattice — and the image lands back inside the lattice, which is closure under · made visible.

Verifying Definition 4.5 as written means checking every ring axiom for S, which is tedious. Almost all of it is unnecessary.

Proposition 4.14. Let R be a ring and S ⊆ R a subset containing 0R and 1R. Then S is a subring of R if and only if (i) S is closed under + and ·, and (ii) S contains the additive inverse of each of its elements. — Aluffi §4.2, Proposition 4.14, p. 63
Faded example — ℤ[i] by the short route. Redo Example 4.12 using Proposition 4.14 rather than Definition 4.5.

Membership of 0 and 1: take a = ⟨your step⟩, b = ⟨your step⟩ for 0, and a = 1, b = 0 for 1.

Closure under +: (a₁ + b₁i) + (a₂ + b₂i) = (a₁ + a₂) + (b₁ + b₂)i, and sums of integers are ⟨your step⟩.

Closure under ·: the display above, whose two coefficients are built from integers by ⟨your step: which two operations?⟩ only.

Additive inverses: −(a + bi) = ⟨your step⟩, again with integer coefficients.

Four checks, none of them an axiom. Associativity, commutativity and distributivity were never mentioned — and the guided proof explains why they never need to be.

You try (solo). Aluffi's Example 4.13: let R be the set of real matrices of the form (a −b / b a), i.e. rows (a, −b) and (b, a). Use Proposition 4.14 to show R is a subring of M2,2(ℝ). The one real computation is the product,

(a₁ −b₁ / b₁ a₁)·(a₂ −b₂ / b₂ a₂) = (a₁a₂ − b₁b₂   −(a₁b₂ + b₁a₂) / a₁b₂ + b₁a₂   a₁a₂ − b₁b₂).

Then answer the question the shape is asking: where have you seen those two coefficients before, today? — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.13, p. 63

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Prove Proposition 4.14. The forward direction is immediate from the definitions; the substance is the converse. Assume 0R, 1R ∈ S, that S is closed under + and ·, and that S contains additive inverses. Show every axiom of Definition 3.1 holds in S. The whole proof turns on one observation about the logical form of the axioms — find it and say it explicitly, then go through the list.

Reveal model answer

The observation. Sort the axioms by quantifier. Axioms (i) associativity of +, (iv) commutativity of +, (v) associativity of ·, and (vii) distributivity are purely universally quantified identities: each says "for all a, b, c, some equation holds." Axioms (ii), (iii), (vi) are existential: they assert that certain elements exist.

The universal axioms are free. If an identity holds for all elements of R, it holds in particular whenever the elements happen to lie in S. Closure guarantees that both sides of each identity are computed inside S, so nothing escapes. That disposes of (i), (iv), (v), (vii) with no work at all.

The existential axioms are exactly what we assumed. (ii) holds because 0R ∈ S by hypothesis and it is an additive identity for all of R, hence for S. (iii) holds because S was assumed to contain the additive inverse of each of its elements. (vi) holds because 1R ∈ S and it is a multiplicative identity throughout R.

So all seven axioms hold for S with 0S = 0R and 1S = 1R, which is Definition 4.5. ∎

Grade yourself on whether you stated the universal/existential split as the reason, rather than checking four identities by hand. It is the reason, and it is reusable: any substructure defined by "closed, contains the distinguished elements" inherits every universally quantified law of the parent for free. Aluffi notes the check can be trimmed further still — 1 ∈ S plus closure under subtraction and multiplication suffices (Exercise 4.5).

Segment 3 · ~25 min

The pattern: what both constructions hand you

A subring, like a product, comes with a distinguished map. If S ⊆ R there are in general many functions S → R — sixteen of them when |S| = 2 and |R| = 4 — and one is writeable knowing nothing: the inclusion — Aluffi §4.2, p. 64

i : S → R,    i(s) = s.

The inclusion satisfies the same three properties as the projection. For all s₁, s₂ ∈ S,

i(s₁ + s₂) = s₁ + s₂ = i(s₁) + i(s₂),    i(s₁·s₂) = s₁·s₂ = i(s₁)·i(s₂),

and i(1S) = 1R — this last one only because Definition 4.5 insisted that S share R's identity. — Aluffi §4.2, Definition 4.5, p. 61; the inclusion map, p. 64

Aluffi's comment is the point of the whole chapter: all of this may seem too obvious to be worth pointing out, and "in mathematics that is often how important notions manifest themselves." Two constructions with nothing in common — one builds up, one cuts down — and both hand you a map that respects +, respects ·, and preserves 1. That triple is about to be promoted to a definition in §4.3: the ring homomorphism, which is aa-08.

Why the third condition is not redundant. Compare π with the other obvious map between R and R × S, namely ι : R → R × S, ι(a) = (a, 0S). It respects + and · perfectly well:

ι(a + b) = (a + b, 0) = ι(a) + ι(b),    ι(ab) = (ab, 0) = ι(a)ι(b).

But ι(1R) = (1R, 0S), which is not 1R×S = (1R, 1S) when S is nonzero. Its image is Example 4.10's non-subring. So "respects both operations" is strictly weaker than the triple, and this is precisely why Definition 4.5 was written with the 1 clause in it. Problem 5 makes you separate these two maps carefully; the honest summary is that π deserves to be called structure-preserving and ι does not.

Application — the subring every ring contains. Fix a ring R. Using aa-05's multiple notation (Remark 3.16), write n·1R for 1R + ⋯ + 1R with n summands when n > 0, 0R when n = 0, and −((−n)·1R) when n < 0. Then

R₀ = { n·1R : n ∈ ℤ }

is a subring of R, by Proposition 4.14: it contains 0R = 0·1R and 1R = 1·1R, it is closed because (m·1)(n·1) = (mn)·1 and (m·1) + (n·1) = (m+n)·1, and −(n·1) = (−n)·1. And R₀ is contained in every subring of R, since any subring holds 1R and is closed under addition and negation.

Now let N = { n ∈ ℤ : n·1R = 0R }. It contains 0 and is closed under addition and negation. If N = {0} set c = 0; otherwise let c be its least positive element, which exists by well-ordering. For any n ∈ N divide with remainder — aa-01's Theorem 1.3 — as n = cq + r with 0 ≤ r < c; then r·1R = n·1R − q(c·1R) = 0, so r ∈ N, and minimality forces r = 0. Hence N = cℤ. That c is the characteristic of R. — Aluffi §1.2, Theorem 1.3, p. 6

ℤ has characteristic 0; ℤ/12ℤ has characteristic 12; ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/3ℤ has characteristic 6, since n(1,1) = (0,0) needs 2 ∣ n and 3 ∣ n — the same 6 as ℤ/6ℤ, exactly as Example 4.3 predicts. This is Problem 6, and it is the first place where products, subrings, and division with remainder are all doing work in one argument.

Self-check 2. Is ℤ/2ℤ a subring of ℤ?

Definition 4.5 requires S to be closed under R's operations and to be a ring under them. Reading [1]₂ as the integer 1 forces [1]₂ + [1]₂ to be the integer 2, but in ℤ/2ℤ that sum is [0]₂. No identification can satisfy both. This is aa-03's "ℤ/nℤ is not a subset of ℤ" restated in the language of subrings — and it now has a precise reason attached rather than an exhortation. — Aluffi §4.2, Example 4.7, pp. 61–62

Self-check 3. Proposition 4.14 lets you skip checking associativity and distributivity in S. Why?

Axioms (i), (iv), (v), (vii) each read "for all a, b, c ∈ R, [equation]". A statement true of all elements of R is true of the ones that lie in S; closure only guarantees the computation stays inside S. What cannot be inherited that way are the existential axioms (ii), (iii), (vi) — which is exactly the list Proposition 4.14 makes you check by hand. — Aluffi §4.2, Proposition 4.14, p. 63

Self-check 4. Let S be a nontrivial ring. Of π : R × S → R, π(a, b) = a, and ι : R → R × S, ι(a) = (a, 0), which preserves the multiplicative identity?

π(1, 1) = 1R ✓. But ι(1R) = (1R, 0S), and that is not (1R, 1S) — which is exactly why the question assumes S nontrivial. Drop that hypothesis and the answer changes: in the trivial ring 0S = 1S, so ι preserves the identity too and "both" becomes correct. Both maps respect + and ·, so the identity condition is the one doing the separating — which is the reason it is on the list at all. — Aluffi §4.1, p. 61; §4.2, Example 4.10, p. 62

Self-check 5. Can a product R × S of two nonzero rings be an integral domain?

The computation uses nothing about R and S beyond being nonzero, so it applies even to ℝ × ℝ or ℚ × ℚ — a product of two fields. Definition 3.23 fails immediately. Aluffi makes the point at ℂ versus ℝ × ℝ; the general statement is that products manufacture zero-divisors, which is one reason aa-20's structure theorem, which decomposes modules into products, will have to be careful about what it preserves. — Aluffi §4.1, Example 4.4, p. 60; §3.3, Definition 3.23, p. 53

Then check yourself
  1. Products (Definition 4.1) are componentwise, with 0 = (0, 0) and 1 = (1, 1). They always create zero-divisors: (1, 0)(0, 1) = (0, 0), so a product of nonzero rings is never a domain. Size does not determine a ring — ℤ/4ℤ ≇ ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ (x + x = 0 holds in one, not the other) while ℤ/6ℤ ≅ ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/3ℤ, the difference being coprimality.
  2. Subrings (Definition 4.5) must carry the ambient 0 and 1. The diagonal Δ ⊆ R × R qualifies; the axis {(a, 0)} does not; ℤ/2ℤ is not a subring of ℤ. Checking is cheap: Proposition 4.14 — contains 0 and 1, closed under + and ·, closed under negation — because the four purely universal axioms are inherited automatically and only the three existential ones need checking.
  3. Both constructions hand you the same kind of map. The projection π and the inclusion i each respect +, respect ·, and send 1 to 1. The third condition is not free: ι(a) = (a, 0) respects both operations and fails it. That triple becomes the definition of a ring homomorphism in aa-08.
The habit being trained here is the categorical one: an object is only half the data, and the maps are the other half. Everything from functoriality of homology to persistence modules is this habit, applied to something larger.
