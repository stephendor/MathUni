Euclidean domains, PIDs, UFDs; the field of fractions (aa-12)

aa-11 took ℤ apart and found that half of what you believed about it was a hypothesis in disguise. Today we name the hypothesis.

One property does almost all the work: every ideal is generated by a single element. Assume it and Bézout comes back, "irreducible" and "prime" become the same word again, ascending chains of ideals must stop, and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic reappears — for an entire class of rings at once. The chain is Euclidean ⟹ PID ⟹ UFD, each arrow a theorem and each strict. And "PID" is not an incidental piece of vocabulary: it is the exact hypothesis the structure theorem of aa-20 will demand, and therefore the exact hypothesis a persistence barcode rests on.

Predict before we start. ℤ is a PID: every ideal is (n). Is ℤ[x] a PID?

The ideal (2, x) of ℤ[x] is not principal. aa-11's Remark 6.17 already did the work: if (2, x) = (d) then d divides 2 and divides x, forcing d to be a unit, so (d) = (1) — but 1 ∉ (2, x), since every element of (2, x) has even constant term. So ℤ[x] is not a PID. Aluffi's blunt summary: the vast majority of integral domains are not PIDs. Being a PID is a strong, rare, and therefore useful condition. — Aluffi §6.3, Definition 6.25, p. 113

Why this matters for the mission: 'Principal ideal domain' is the exact hypothesis the persistence structure theorem will demand.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Division with remainder, axiomatised — and what one generator buys

Definition 6.24 (Euclidean domain). An integral domain R is a Euclidean domain if there is a function v from the nonzero elements of R to ℤ≥0 such that for all a ∈ R and all b ≠ 0 there exist q, r ∈ R with

a = bq + r,    and either r = 0 or v(r) < v(b).

The function v is a Euclidean valuation. — Aluffi §6.3, Definition 6.24, pp. 112–113

You know one example already: ℤ, with v(n) = |n|. That is aa-01's Theorem 1.3 with the uniqueness clause dropped — Definition 6.24 asks only that a division be possible, not that it be unique. Two more are coming: k[x] for a field k (aa-13's Theorem 7.2), and the Gaussian integers ℤ[i]. — Aluffi §6.3, p. 113; §1.2, Theorem 1.3, p. 6

Definition 6.25 (PID). An integral domain R is a principal ideal domain if every ideal of R is principal: for each ideal I there is a ∈ R with I = (a). — Aluffi §6.3, Definition 6.25, p. 113
Theorem 6.26. Euclidean domains are PIDs. — Aluffi §6.3, Theorem 6.26, p. 113
Proof. Let I be an ideal of a Euclidean domain R. If I = (0) it is principal, so assume not. The set

S = { v(s) : s ∈ I, s ≠ 0 } ⊆ ℤ≥0

is a nonempty set of nonnegative integers, so by well-ordering it has a least element; pick b ∈ I, b ≠ 0, attaining it.

Certainly (b) ⊆ I. Conversely take a ∈ I and divide: a = bq + r with r = 0 or v(r) < v(b). Now r = a − bq ∈ I, since a ∈ I and bq ∈ I. If r ≠ 0 then v(r) < v(b) contradicts the minimality of v(b). So r = 0 and a = bq ∈ (b). Hence I = (b). ∎ — Aluffi §6.3, pp. 113–114

You have seen this proof three times. Take the least element of a set of nonnegative integers, divide, show the remainder must be 0. It proved Bézout in aa-02, classified the ideals of ℤ in aa-09, and produced the characteristic in aa-07. Definition 6.24 is precisely the abstraction of what those arguments needed.

Examples 6.27 and 6.28. ℤ is a PID — every ideal is nℤ, which aa-09 proved by hand and Theorem 6.26 now delivers as a special case. And once k[x] is shown Euclidean (aa-13), every ℚ[x], ℝ[x], ℂ[x], (ℤ/pℤ)[x] is a PID. But not ℤ[x]: the ideal (2, x) is not principal. — Aluffi §6.3, Examples 6.27 and 6.28, p. 114

Fields are PIDs too, trivially — their only ideals are (0) and (1). Aluffi's own comment: "This is not so exciting."

What one generator buys: Bézout, restored. Let R be a PID and a, b ∈ R. The ideal (a, b) is principal, say (a, b) = (d). Then (d) is tautologically the smallest principal ideal containing a and b, so d is a gcd of a and b in the sense of aa-11's Definition 6.16 — gcds exist. And (a, b) is by construction the set of linear combinations of a and b, so

d = (a linear combination of a and b),

which is Bézout. Both of the failures Remark 6.17 warned about disappear at once. In particular a, b are relatively prime in a PID exactly when (a, b) = (1). — Aluffi §6.3, p. 114

Remark 6.29. In a Euclidean domain it is even better: the Euclidean algorithm of aa-02's Theorem 1.14 generalises verbatim, so gcds are not merely available but computable, as soon as you can divide with remainder. — Aluffi §6.3, Remark 6.29, p. 114

Proposition 6.30. In a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. — Aluffi §6.3, Proposition 6.30, p. 114
Proof. Let I be a nonzero prime ideal. Since R is a PID, I = (p) with p ≠ 0, and p is a prime element by Definition 6.18. By Theorem 6.23, p is irreducible. By Lemma 6.22, (p) is maximal among proper principal ideals — and in a PID every ideal is principal, so (p) is maximal among proper ideals outright. Theorem 6.9 finishes it. ∎ — Aluffi §6.3, p. 115

This is the better explanation. aa-11's Example 6.11 proved the same thing for ℤ using finiteness of ℤ/nℤ; that argument was correct but accidental. The real reason is that ℤ is a PID — and the theorem now covers every PID at once. Notice too where Lemma 6.22's odd phrase "maximal among principal ideals" finally pays off: in a PID that qualifier is vacuous.

Remark 6.31, briefly. Chains of prime ideals (0) ⊊ I₁ ⊊ ⋯ ⊊ In measure a ring's Krull dimension, and it agrees with geometric dimension: fields have dimension 0 (isolated points), ℂ[x, y] has dimension 2 with (0) ⊊ (x) ⊊ (x, y) as long as it gets. Proposition 6.30 says a PID that is not a field has dimension 1 — so PIDs are "curves", and ℤ is one of them. — Aluffi §6.3, Remark 6.31, p. 115

Self-check 1. In the proof that Euclidean domains are PIDs, what is the element b?

b is chosen by well-ordering, as an element attaining the minimum of { v(s) : s ∈ I, s ≠ 0 }. Minimality is the whole argument: it is what makes the remainder r = a − bq, which lies in I and has smaller valuation, impossible unless r = 0. An arbitrary nonzero element would not do. (Calling b a gcd is true after the fact, but it is a conclusion, not the definition used.) — Aluffi §6.3, Theorem 6.26, p. 113

Ascending chains, and the Fundamental Theorem for PIDs

Proposition 6.32. In a PID every ascending chain of ideals stabilises: if I₁ ⊆ I₂ ⊆ I₃ ⊆ ⋯ then there is an m with Im = Im+1 = ⋯. — Aluffi §6.3, Proposition 6.32, p. 116
Proof. Let I = ⋃j≥1 Ij. A union of a chain of ideals is an ideal — any two elements lie in a common Ij, so closure and absorption are inherited. Since R is a PID, I = (r) for some r; and r, being in the union, lies in some Im. Then

I = (r) ⊆ Im ⊆ Im+1 ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ I,

so every inclusion from Im on is an equality. ∎ — Aluffi §6.3, p. 116

The condition is the ascending chain condition, and rings satisfying it are called Noetherian, after Emmy Noether. Every ideal being finitely generated is enough for it; a PID generates each ideal with a single element, so PIDs are Noetherian in a very strong sense. aa-19 returns to this. — Aluffi §6.3, p. 116

In ℤ the phenomenon is easy to feel. Start anywhere and climb: each step must strictly enlarge the ideal, which by aa-09's classification means passing to a proper divisor, and there is a ceiling. Aluffi's own chain,

(0) ⊊ (80) ⊊ (40) ⊊ (20) ⊊ (10) ⊊ (2) ⊊ (1),

cannot be extended. Try it below: pick a starting n and climb by removing one prime factor at a time. — Aluffi §6.3, p. 116

Proposition 6.33. In a PID, every irreducible element q is prime; indeed (q) is a maximal ideal. — Aluffi §6.4, Proposition 6.33, p. 117

Proof: Lemma 6.22 makes (q) maximal among proper principal ideals; in a PID all ideals are principal, so (q) is maximal (Theorem 6.9), hence prime (Proposition 6.3), so q is a prime element. ∎ Together with Theorem 6.23 this upgrades aa-02's Theorem 1.21 — irreducible ⟺ prime, proved for ℤ using Bézout — to every PID. And that is the honest account of why it held in ℤ: not because ℤ is the integers, but because ℤ is a PID. — Aluffi §6.4, p. 117; §1.4, Theorem 1.21, p. 14

Theorem 6.35. Let R be a PID and a ∈ R nonzero and not a unit. Then a = q₁⋯qr for finitely many irreducibles qi, and the factorisation is unique: if a = q₁⋯qr = p₁⋯ps with all factors irreducible, then s = r and, after reordering, (p₁) = (q₁), …, (pr) = (qr). — Aluffi §6.4, Theorem 6.35, p. 117
Proof — existence, by the ascending chain condition. Suppose some nonzero non-unit a has no factorisation into irreducibles. Then a is not itself irreducible, so a = a₁b₁ with neither factor a unit, and (a) ⊊ (a₁). If both a₁ and b₁ factored, so would a; so one of them — say a₁ — does not. Repeating,

(a) ⊊ (a₁) ⊊ (a₂) ⊊ ⋯

is an infinitely ascending chain, contradicting Proposition 6.32. ∎

Uniqueness, by well-ordering. If uniqueness fails, take the smallest r for which some a has two different factorisations a = q₁⋯qr = p₁⋯ps. Then r ≥ 2 (if r = 1 then q₁ is irreducible and equals p₁⋯ps, forcing s = 1 and the factorisations to agree). Now q₁⋯qr ∈ (p₁), and p₁ is irreducible hence prime by Proposition 6.33, so some qi ∈ (p₁); reorder so i = 1. Since q₁ is irreducible, Lemma 6.22 makes (q₁) maximal among proper principal ideals, and (q₁) ⊆ (p₁) ≠ R forces (q₁) = (p₁). So q₁ = p₁ up to a unit, and cancelling p₁ (legal: R is a domain) gives

q₂⋯qr = p₂⋯ps,

a factorisation with r − 1 < r factors, which by minimality of r must be unique — so r = s and the factors match. Contradiction. ∎ — Aluffi §6.4, pp. 117–119

Compare aa-02 line by line. There, existence used well-ordering on ℤ and uniqueness used Theorem 1.21. Here, existence uses the ascending chain condition — which is what replaces "there is no infinite descent among positive integers" — and uniqueness uses Proposition 6.33, which is Theorem 1.21's replacement. The architecture is identical; only the two supports have been rebuilt from a weaker hypothesis.

Definition 6.37 (UFD). An integral domain is a unique factorisation domain if the conclusion of Theorem 6.35 holds in it. — Aluffi §6.4, Definition 6.37, p. 119

Remark 6.38 — and the containment is strict. Theorem 6.35 says PIDs are UFDs. But the class of UFDs is much larger: ℤ[x] and ℂ[x, y] are UFDs and are not PIDs. So the chain of classes reads

fields ⊆ Euclidean domains ⊆ PIDs ⊆ UFDs ⊆ integral domains,

and each inclusion is strict — ℤ is a PID and not a field, ℤ[x] is a UFD and not a PID, and ℤ[√−5] from aa-11 is a domain that is not a UFD, since there 2 is irreducible without being prime. — Aluffi §6.4, Remark 6.38, p. 119

Faded example — running the chain on ℤ.

ℤ is a Euclidean domain with valuation v(n) = ⟨your step: what function of n?⟩, because Theorem 1.3 supplies q and r with a = bq + r and 0 ≤ r < |b|, so r = 0 or v(r) ⟨your step: which inequality?⟩ v(b).

By Theorem 6.26, ℤ is therefore a ⟨your step⟩, so every ideal of ℤ is (n) for a single integer n.

By Theorem 6.35, ℤ is therefore a UFD — and unwinding Definition 6.37 in ℤ, where the units are ±1 and (p) = (q) means p = ±q, that statement is exactly ⟨your step: which theorem of aa-02?⟩.

Now go the other way and check the arrows are strict: ℤ is not a field because ⟨your step: which element has no inverse?⟩ has no inverse, so the second inclusion in the chain is strict at ℤ.

Self-check 2. Which hypothesis does the existence half of Theorem 6.35 use?

A non-factorable element manufactures a strictly increasing chain of principal ideals, and Proposition 6.32 forbids that. Primality is not used — exactly as in aa-02, where existence needed only Definition 1.17 and well-ordering while uniqueness needed Theorem 1.21. The split survives the generalisation unchanged, which is a good sign that it was the right split. — Aluffi §6.4, Theorem 6.35, pp. 117–118

Self-check 3. Which of these is a UFD but not a PID?

ℤ[x] is a UFD (Remark 6.38) and not a PID, since (2, x) is not principal. ℤ is a PID, so it is on the wrong side of the strict inclusion. And ℤ[√−5] is not a UFD at all: aa-11 showed 2 is irreducible there without being prime, and Proposition 6.33 says that cannot happen in a PID — so it is a domain outside both classes. Three rings, three different positions in the chain. — Aluffi §6.4, Remark 6.38, p. 119

Segment 3 · ~25 min

The field of fractions

ℚ is "the smallest field containing ℤ", and the precise content of that phrase is a universal property: for any injective homomorphism i : ℤ → k into a field, there is a unique injective homomorphism ℚ → k compatible with it, namely p/q ↦ i(p)i(q)−1. Every integral domain has such a field. — Aluffi §6.5, p. 121

The construction. Let R be an integral domain and put F̂ = { (r, s) ∈ R × R : s ≠ 0 }, with

(r₁, s₁) ∼ (r₂, s₂) ⟺ r₁s₂ = r₂s₁.

Lemma 6.42. ∼ is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate. For transitivity, from r₁s₂ = r₂s₁ and r₂s₃ = r₃s₂,

(r₁s₃)s₂ = (r₁s₂)s₃ = (r₂s₁)s₃ = (r₂s₃)s₁ = (r₃s₂)s₁ = (r₃s₁)s₂,

and cancelling s₂ — legal because s₂ ≠ 0 and R is a domain — gives r₁s₃ = r₃s₁. ∎ — Aluffi §6.5, Lemma 6.42, p. 122

Both hypotheses are consumed in that one line. Aluffi points it out explicitly: transitivity needs R to be an integral domain and needs the second coordinate to be nonzero. Neither is decoration, and neither can be dropped.

Definition 6.43 (field of fractions). F = F̂/∼ with

[(r₁, s₁)] + [(r₂, s₂)] := [(r₁s₂ + r₂s₁, s₁s₂)],    [(r₁, s₁)]·[(r₂, s₂)] := [(r₁r₂, s₁s₂)].

— Aluffi §6.5, Definition 6.43, p. 122

Claim 6.44. Those operations are well defined. Both formulas are written in representatives, so — for the fourth time in this module, after aa-04's Claim 2.12, aa-09's Lemma 5.25, and aa-10's Proposition 5.39 — the check must be made. For addition: if r₁s′₁ = r′₁s₁ then expanding both sides of (r₁s₂ + r₂s₁)(s′₁s₂) = (r′₁s₂ + r₂s′₁)(s₁s₂) gives the same element, using commutativity. — Aluffi §6.5, Claim 6.44, pp. 122–123

F is a field. The ring axioms transfer from R (associativity of + is a page of expanding, and it works because R is commutative), with 0F = [(0, 1)] and 1F = [(1, 1)]. Since R is a domain, 0 ≠ 1 in R, hence (0, 1) ≁ (1, 1) and 0 ≠ 1 in F. And every nonzero class is invertible: if [(r, s)] ≠ [(0, 1)] then r·1 ≠ 0·s, i.e. r ≠ 0, so (s, r) ∈ F̂ and

[(r, s)]·[(s, r)] = [(rs, sr)] = [(1, 1)] = 1F.

At this point the notation earns its retirement: write r/s for [(r, s)]. Then r₁/s₁ = r₂/s₂ ⟺ r₁s₂ = r₂s₁, and the operations read exactly as you would write them. — Aluffi §6.5, pp. 123–124

The map ι : R → F, ι(r) = r/1, is an injective ring homomorphism, so R sits inside F as a subring — just as ℤ sits inside ℚ. — Aluffi §6.5, p. 124

Theorem 6.45 (universal property). Let R be an integral domain with field of fractions F and inclusion ι : R → F. For every field k and every injective ring homomorphism i : R → k there is a unique injective ring homomorphism j : F → k with j ∘ ι = i. — Aluffi §6.5, Theorem 6.45, p. 124
Proof sketch — the statement dictates the map. If j exists, then since j is a ring homomorphism and j ∘ ι = i,

j(r/s) = j(r/1)·j(s/1)−1 = i(r)i(s)−1.   (6.9)

There is no choice, so j is unique before anything is proved. The rest is verification: i(s) ≠ 0 because s ≠ 0 and i is injective, so the inverse exists in k; well-definedness follows from r₁s₂ = r₂s₁ giving i(r₁)i(s₂) = i(r₂)i(s₁); the homomorphism clauses are a routine expansion; injectivity is automatic because every homomorphism out of a field is injective; and j(ι(r)) = i(r)i(1)−1 = i(r). ∎ — Aluffi §6.5, pp. 125–126

Aluffi's own gloss is the lesson here: the proof looks imposing, but the statement tells you how to define the map, and everything after that is bookkeeping. This is the third universal property in the module — after the quotient set in aa-03 and Proposition 5.39 in aa-10 — and all three have the same shape: uniqueness is free because the diagram forces the formula, and the work is showing the forced formula is legal.

Remark 6.46. F is R with every nonzero element made invertible by hand. The general version is localisation: for a multiplicative subset S ⊆ R one builds S−1R in which every element of S becomes a unit, and the field of fractions is the case S = R \ {0}. — Aluffi §6.5, Remark 6.46, p. 126

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi remarks that the field of fractions is unique up to isomorphism, that this follows from the universal property alone, and encourages you to prove it yourself — adding that "you do not need to know anything about a specific construction of F in order to do it." Suppose F, ι : R → F and F′, ι′ : R → F′ both satisfy the universal property of Theorem 6.45. Prove F ≅ F′. Use nothing but the property itself.

Reveal model answer

Apply F's universal property to the field F′ and the injective homomorphism ι′ : R → F′. It gives a unique homomorphism j : F → F′ with j ∘ ι = ι′.

Symmetrically, apply F′'s universal property to F and ι, giving a unique j′ : F′ → F with j′ ∘ ι′ = ι.

Now compose: j′ ∘ j : F → F satisfies (j′ ∘ j) ∘ ι = j′ ∘ ι′ = ι. But the identity map idF also satisfies idF ∘ ι = ι, and F's universal property says there is exactly one homomorphism F → F compatible with ι. Hence j′ ∘ j = idF. The same argument on the other side gives j ∘ j′ = idF′.

So j is a homomorphism with a two-sided inverse homomorphism, and by aa-08's Corollary 4.31 it is an isomorphism: F ≅ F′. ∎

Grade yourself on the step that applies uniqueness to a map you already know — the identity. That move is the entire proof, and it is the standard argument for every object defined by a universal property. Note what was never used: the pairs (r, s), the relation ∼, the formula for addition. A universal property determines its object without describing it.

Self-check 4. Where does the proof of Lemma 6.42 use that R is an integral domain?

The transitivity computation ends at (r₁s₃)s₂ = (r₃s₁)s₂, and concluding r₁s₃ = r₃s₁ requires cancelling s₂ — which needs s₂ to be a non-zero-divisor (aa-06's Proposition 3.26) and needs s₂ ≠ 0, guaranteed by the definition of F̂. Aluffi flags both. Over a ring with zero-divisors the relation genuinely fails to be transitive, which is why localisation in general needs more care. — Aluffi §6.5, Lemma 6.42, p. 122

Self-check 5. In Theorem 6.45, why is j unique?

Write r/s = (r/1)(s/1)−1. A ring homomorphism must send that to j(r/1)j(s/1)−1, and the compatibility j ∘ ι = i evaluates both factors: i(r)i(s)−1. So the formula is forced before any construction is examined — uniqueness costs nothing, and the actual work is showing the forced formula is well defined and a homomorphism. That is the shape of every universal property in this module. — Aluffi §6.5, p. 125

  1. Euclidean ⟹ PID (Theorem 6.26): given a valuation, take the element of least valuation in an ideal and divide — the remainder must be 0. Being a PID immediately restores gcds and Bézout, because (a, b) = (d) means d is a gcd and is a linear combination; and it makes nonzero primes maximal (Proposition 6.30), which is the real reason that held in ℤ.
  2. PID ⟹ UFD (Theorem 6.35). Existence of factorisations comes from the ascending chain condition (Proposition 6.32) — no infinite chain (a) ⊊ (a₁) ⊊ ⋯ — and uniqueness from irreducible ⟹ prime (Proposition 6.33). Both inclusions in fields ⊆ ED ⊆ PID ⊆ UFD ⊆ domains are strict: ℤ, ℤ[x], ℤ[√−5] sit at three different levels.
  3. Every integral domain sits inside a smallest field (Definition 6.43, Theorem 6.45): pairs (r, s) with s ≠ 0, modulo r₁s₂ = r₂s₁ — a relation whose transitivity is exactly where the domain hypothesis is spent — written r/s. Its universal property forces any map out of it, so F is unique up to isomorphism without reference to the construction.
The single sentence to keep: almost everything you believed about ℤ is a consequence of every ideal having one generator. That is why aa-20 will insist on a PID, and why a persistence barcode exists over k[x] and not over an arbitrary ring.
