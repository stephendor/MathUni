Classification, simplicity, and Sylow's theorems (aa-29)

Prime factorisation for groups: Sylow's theorems pull p-subgroups out of thin air.

Lagrange's theorem says a subgroup's order divides the group's order. aa-28 showed the converse fails — A₄ has order 12 and no subgroup of order 6. So which divisors are realised? Sylow's answer is startling in its precision: every prime power dividing |G| is realised, the maximal ones are all conjugate to each other, and their number is pinned down modulo p. Three theorems, and most of the classification problem for small orders collapses.

Predict before we start. aa-28 showed A₄ has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6. Does it have a subgroup of order 4?

Yes: the Klein four-group V of aa-26's Example 11.63 is exactly it. And that is not luck. Sylow's first theorem guarantees a subgroup of order pk for every prime power pk dividing |G|. The failure at 6 is precisely because 6 is not a prime power — the converse of Lagrange fails only away from prime powers. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.29, p. 277

Why this matters for the mission: The deep structural payoff — how a finite symmetry group is actually taken apart.

25 min Segment 1 — What a classification would mean

One more piece of vocabulary first, and it is orbit–stabilizer applied to subgroups rather than elements.

Definition 12.24. The normalizer of a subgroup H is NG(H) = {g in G : gHg⁻¹ = H}, its stabilizer under the conjugation action on subsets. It is the largest subgroup of G containing H as a normal subgroup. — Aluffi Underground, Definition 12.24, p. 274

The slogan Aluffi says to remember: the number of conjugates of H equals the index of its normalizer — Theorem 12.10 with the conjugation action on subsets in place of elements. — Aluffi Underground, p. 274 Compare aa-28's centralizer slogan; it is the same theorem twice.

Now the goal. A classification is a complete list of the objects up to isomorphism. aa-22 achieved one for finite abelian groups, and Aluffi notes what that bought: any question about them can in principle be settled by testing it against the list. For general groups, here is how far aa-28's tools reach.

The list so far. Aluffi tabulates what is already known for small orders:
  • |G| = 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13: G is cyclic, since a group of prime order is Cp and the trivial group is C₁.
  • |G| = 4 or 9: G ≅ Cp² or Cp × Cp, since order p² forces commutativity and aa-22 classifies from there.
  • |G| = 6: C₆ or S₃ — or something else?
  • |G| = 8: C₈, C₄ × C₂, C₂ × C₂ × C₂, D₈ — or something else?
  • |G| = 10: C₁₀ or D₁₀ — or something else?
  • |G| = 12: C₁₂, C₂ × C₆, D₁₂, A₄ — or something else?

The tools used are exactly aa-28's Proposition 12.5 and Corollary 12.23 together with aa-22's Theorem 10.18, plus the fact that Cm × Cn ≅ Cmn exactly when m and n are coprime. — Aluffi Underground, the tools listed on p. 274 — Aluffi Underground §12.3, p. 275 D₆ is omitted from the list because it is isomorphic to S₃; both D₁₂ and A₄ appear because they are non-isomorphic noncommutative groups of order 12. — Aluffi Underground §12.3, p. 275

The question marks are the point. Aluffi's strategy for organising them borrows aa-18's exact sequences: a normal subgroup H of G gives

1 → H → G → G/H → 1,

and the extension problem asks to reconstruct the middle from the two ends. It is hard, and not uniquely solvable — but if it could be solved, classifying all finite groups would reduce to classifying those with no proper nontrivial normal subgroup. — Aluffi Underground §12.3, p. 275

Definition 12.25. A nontrivial group G is simple if it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroups. The trivial group is not simple. — Aluffi Underground §12.3, Definition 12.25, p. 275

Example 12.26. A nontrivial abelian group is simple if and only if it is Cp for a positive prime p. If |G| is not prime, take a prime factor p; Cauchy's theorem for abelian groups gives an element g of order p, so ⟨g⟩ is a proper nontrivial subgroup, and in an abelian group every subgroup is normal. — Aluffi Underground §12.3, Example 12.26, p. 275

That argument settles the finite case and only the finite case: if G is infinite there is no |G| to factor, and Cauchy's theorem has nothing to say. The stated classification is still correct, and the missing step is two lines. Let G be abelian, simple and infinite, and take g ≠ e. If g has finite order, ⟨g⟩ is finite and therefore proper in an infinite G, as well as nontrivial and — G being abelian — normal. If g has infinite order, then ⟨g²⟩ is nontrivial, sits properly inside ⟨g⟩ and hence properly inside G, and is again normal; this is the copy of 2ℤ inside ℤ. Either way G has a proper nontrivial normal subgroup, so no infinite abelian group is simple and Cp remains the whole list. This unit's problem set makes the same case split.

Aluffi is candid about the scale of the reduced problem: classifying finite simple groups has been achieved, and "it should be counted among the most complex single achievements in the whole of mathematics". — Aluffi Underground §12.3, p. 275 The realistic question, and the one this unit answers, is narrower: given n, can we decide whether a simple group of order n exists?

Example 12.27. A group of order pr with r > 1 is not simple: its centre is nontrivial by aa-28's Corollary 12.22, and the centre is normal. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Example 12.27, p. 276

Written that compactly the argument has a gap worth closing yourself, because simplicity asks for a normal subgroup that is proper as well as nontrivial. If Z(G) ≠ G, the centre is exactly such a subgroup and the argument is complete. If Z(G) = G, then G is abelian — and an abelian group of order pr with r > 1 is not simple by Example 12.26, since its order is not prime; concretely, Cauchy's theorem supplies an element of order p, and the subgroup it generates is proper, nontrivial and automatically normal. Either way G is not simple, and the case split costs one sentence.

That settles 4, 8, 9 at once, while 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 are simple by Example 12.26. Orders 6, 10, 12 need work.

Example 12.28 — the ad hoc argument, kept deliberately. Let |G| = 2p with p > 2 prime. Element orders are 2, p or 2p. Could all non-identity elements have order 2? If x, y and xy all have order at most 2 then xy = (xy)⁻¹ = y⁻¹x⁻¹ = yx, so G would be commutative — and then by the classification of finite abelian groups it would be cyclic, so not every element would have order 2. Contradiction.

So G has an element of order p or 2p. Order 2p gives G ≅ C2p, not simple. Order p gives a subgroup ⟨g⟩ of index 2, normal by aa-26's Example 11.59, again not simple. Hence there are no simple groups of order 2p, and in particular none of order 6 or 10. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Example 12.28, p. 276

Aluffi's own verdict on this: it "should strike you as rather ad hoc, and require a certain amount of cleverness". The tools of Segment 2 "remove the need for any cleverness whatsoever". Keep this argument in view — you will watch it be replaced by two lines. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, p. 276

Self-check 1. Why is the trivial group excluded from the definition of simple?

The trivial group has no proper nontrivial normal subgroups for the empty reason that it has no proper nontrivial subgroups at all, so the condition holds vacuously. Aluffi excludes it explicitly, exactly as 1 is excluded from the primes and for the same reason: it would be a degenerate member that has to be exempted from every subsequent theorem. — Aluffi Underground §12.3, Definition 12.25, p. 275

25 min Segment 2 — Sylow I, II, III

Aluffi's preview of what the three say: G must contain p-groups of the largest size Lagrange allows; any two such maximal subgroups are conjugate; and their number divides |G| and is congruent to 1 mod p. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, p. 276

Theorem 12.29 (Sylow I). If p is prime and pk divides |G| for a finite group G, then G contains a subgroup of order pk. In particular if |G| = prm with p not dividing m, then G contains subgroups of order pr. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.29, p. 277

This is the partial converse to Lagrange that aa-28's Example 12.6 seemed to rule out — and it is consistent, because 6 is not a prime power. For abelian groups it follows from the classification theorem; lacking one for general groups, Aluffi notes a standalone argument is needed. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Remark 12.30, p. 277

Definition 12.31. A p-Sylow subgroup of a finite group G is a subgroup of order pr, where pr is the maximum power of p dividing |G|. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Definition 12.31, p. 277

Theorem 12.32 (Sylow II). If P is a p-Sylow subgroup of G and H is any p-group inside G, then H is contained in some conjugate gPg⁻¹. In particular any two p-Sylow subgroups are conjugate. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.32, p. 277

Theorem 12.33 (Sylow III). If |G| = prm with p not dividing m, then the number of p-Sylow subgroups divides m and is ≡ 1 mod p. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.33, p. 277

Two immediate dividends. Theorem 12.34 (Cauchy's theorem): if a prime p divides |G| then G has an element of order p — by Sylow I take a subgroup H of order p, which is cyclic by aa-28's Proposition 12.5, and its generator has order p. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.34, p. 277 This upgrades the abelian version of aa-22 to arbitrary groups, and note the direction of dependence: Sylow I's own proof will use the abelian version, not this one.

Corollary 12.35: a finite abelian group A with |A| = p₁r₁⋯psrs contains a unique subgroup of order piri for each i — because by Sylow II the pi-Sylow subgroups are conjugate, and in a commutative group P = gPg⁻¹ always, so there is only one. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Corollary 12.35, p. 277

That last argument is the pattern for everything in Segment 3, so isolate it: Sylow II plus "there is only one of them" equals normality. A unique p-Sylow subgroup must be its own conjugate, and a subgroup equal to all its conjugates is normal.

Self-check 2. What makes a subgroup a p-Sylow subgroup rather than merely a p-subgroup?

Maximality of the p-power, nothing else. In a group of order 24 = 2³·3, a subgroup of order 4 is a 2-group but not a 2-Sylow subgroup; order 8 is required. Sylow I guarantees they exist, Sylow II says all of them are conjugate, and normality is the special case where there is only one — which is exactly what Sylow III is used to force. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Definition 12.31, p. 277

Self-check 3. Why does a unique p-Sylow subgroup have to be normal?

Conjugation is an automorphism, so it carries a subgroup of order pr to another of order pr — still a p-Sylow subgroup. If there is only one, conjugation cannot move it, which is Definition 11.52 exactly. p-subgroups are certainly not always normal: aa-26's Example 11.61 showed the flip subgroups of D2n are not. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Corollary 12.36, p. 278

25 min Segment 3 — The sieve

Corollary 12.36. Let |G| = prm with r ≥ 1, m > 1, p not dividing m. If the only divisor of m congruent to 1 mod p is 1, then G is not simple — it has a unique, hence normal, p-Sylow subgroup. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Corollary 12.36, p. 278

Proof. By Sylow III the number of p-Sylow subgroups divides m and is ≡ 1 mod p, so under the hypothesis it is 1. That subgroup P is nontrivial since r ≥ 1 and proper since m > 1, and it is normal by the uniqueness argument above. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, p. 278

Worked, then faded — Example 12.37 replaces Example 12.28. Let |G| = 2p with p an odd prime, and apply Corollary 12.36 with the prime p: here r = 1 and m = 2. The divisors of 2 are 1 and 2, and 2 ≡ 1 mod p would need p to divide 1, which is false for p > 1. So the only divisor of m congruent to 1 mod p is 1, and G is not simple — indeed G has a unique normal subgroup of order p. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Example 12.37, p. 278

Two lines, where Example 12.28 needed a page. Aluffi: "Hopefully this impresses you a little."

Now fade it. Aluffi later asserts that Corollary 12.36 "implies right away that there are no simple groups of order 40 or 45", and asks "Do you agree?" — Aluffi Underground §12.4, p. 279 Check both. For 40 = 2³·5 take p = ⟨?⟩, so r = ⟨?⟩ and m = ⟨?⟩; the divisors of m greater than 1 are ⟨?⟩, and none is ≡ 1 mod p because ⟨?⟩. For 45 = 3²·5 take p = ⟨?⟩ and run the same check.

Check

40 = 2³·5. Take p = 5, so r = 1 and m = 8. The divisors of 8 greater than 1 are 2, 4, 8, with residues 2, 4, 3 mod 5 — none equal to 1. So the number of 5-Sylow subgroups is 1, that subgroup has order 5, and it is normal. Not simple.

45 = 3²·5. Take p = 3, so r = 2 and m = 5. The only divisor of 5 greater than 1 is 5, and 5 ≡ 2 mod 3. So the 3-Sylow subgroup of order 9 is unique and normal. Not simple.

Note that the prime you choose matters. For 40 with p = 2 you would get m = 5, whose divisor 5 is ≡ 1 mod 2, and the corollary would say nothing. The corollary is an existence claim over primes: it suffices that some prime works.

Proposition 12.38. If |G| = prm with p prime, r ≥ 1 and 1 < m ≤ p, then G is not simple. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Proposition 12.38, p. 278

Proof. If m = p then |G| is a p-group of order at least p², not simple by Example 12.27. If 1 < m < p then no divisor of m exceeding 1 can be ≡ 1 mod p, since it is smaller than p; Corollary 12.36 applies. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, p. 278

Which leaves 60 — and no amount of Sylow will remove it, because A₅ is a simple group of order 60.

Proposition 12.42. There are no noncommutative simple groups of order less than 60. — Aluffi Underground §12.5, Proposition 12.42, p. 280

Aluffi appends a pointed parenthesis: hard as you may try, you will not be able to use Sylow's theorems to rule out order 60 — and asks why you think that may be. — Aluffi Underground §12.5, p. 280 The answer is that no argument can rule it out, because A₅ exists; Sylow's theorems are not failing, the statement is false at 60.

Guided proof. Prove Proposition 12.43: if p < q are positive primes with q not ≡ 1 mod p, then every group of order pq is cyclic. The Sylow bookkeeping is done for you; you supply the count.

Let Np and Nq be the numbers of p-Sylow and q-Sylow subgroups. Sylow III gives Nq | p with Nq ≡ 1 mod q; since p is prime and p < q this forces Nq = 1. Similarly Np is 1 or q, and q ≢ 1 mod p rules out q, so Np = 1. So G has a unique subgroup H of order p and a unique K of order q. Now count.

Model answer

By aa-28's Proposition 12.4 an element's order divides pq, so it is 1, p, q or pq. The identity is the only element of order 1. An element of order p generates a subgroup of order p, which by Sylow II must be H — so all elements of order p lie in H, and there are exactly p − 1 of them. Likewise q − 1 elements of order q, all in K.

That accounts for 1 + (p − 1) + (q − 1) elements, leaving pq − (p − 1) − (q − 1) − 1 = pq − p − q + 1 = (p − 1)(q − 1) elements unaccounted for. Since p and q are primes greater than 1, (p − 1)(q − 1) > 0, so at least one element remains — and its order can only be pq. A group with an element of order equal to its own order is cyclic, so G ≅ Cpq. — Aluffi Underground §12.5, Proposition 12.43, p. 280

Example: 15 = 3·5 with 5 ≢ 1 mod 3, so there is exactly one group of order 15. Under 60 the orders this settles are 15, 33, 35 and 51. Aluffi adds that when q ≡ 1 mod p there are exactly two groups of order pq — the cyclic one and a noncommutative one — and that for order 2q with q odd prime the two are C2q and D2q. — Aluffi Underground §12.5, p. 280

Self-check 4. Aluffi says the proofs of Sylow's theorems are examples of the power of judicious use of group actions. Which action proves Sylow II?

Sylow II acts with the p-group H on G/P by left multiplication. Since |G/P| = [G : P] is coprime to p, aa-28's Corollary 12.15 forces a fixed point gP, and hgP = gP for all h unwinds to g⁻¹Hg ⊆ P, that is H ⊆ gPg⁻¹. The class equation is what Sylow I uses, to show p divides |Z(G)| in the minimal counterexample. — Aluffi Underground §12.5, p. 281 — Aluffi Underground §12.5, p. 280

Self-check 5. Why does Sylow's third theorem let you prove non-simplicity so often?

Two constraints on one integer, one multiplicative and one additive, and they collide constantly — for m < p no divisor above 1 can be ≡ 1 mod p at all, which is Proposition 12.38. A count of 1 gives normality, hence non-simplicity. The first option is Sylow I misremembered and is false for non-prime-powers; nontrivial centres belong to p-groups only, by aa-28's Corollary 12.22. — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Theorem 12.33, p. 277 — Aluffi Underground §12.4, Corollary 12.36, p. 278

Then check yourself
  1. The programme. Classification means a list up to isomorphism. Normal subgroups split a group as 1 → H → G → G/H → 1, and if the extension problem could be solved, classification would reduce to the groups with no such splitting — the simple ones (Definition 12.25). Abelian simple groups are exactly the Cp (Example 12.26). The normalizer (Definition 12.24) is the stabilizer of a subgroup under conjugation, and the number of conjugates of H is [G : NG(H)].
  2. The three theorems. Sylow I: every prime power dividing |G| is the order of a subgroup (Theorem 12.29), so the maximal ones — the p-Sylow subgroups (Definition 12.31) — exist. Sylow II: all p-subgroups sit inside conjugates of a p-Sylow subgroup, so any two are conjugate (Theorem 12.32). Sylow III: their number divides m and is ≡ 1 mod p (Theorem 12.33). Dividends: Cauchy's theorem in full generality (Theorem 12.34) and uniqueness of Sylow subgroups in abelian groups (Corollary 12.35).
  3. The sieve. A unique p-Sylow subgroup is normal, so when Sylow III forces the count to 1 the group is not simple (Corollary 12.36) — which replaces the page-long Example 12.28 with the two lines of Example 12.37, and generalises to Proposition 12.38 whenever 1 < m ≤ p. Over the orders up to 60 that leaves nine holdouts, of which 40 and 45 fall to the full corollary and 12, 24, 30 to individual Sylow counts. The conclusion is Proposition 12.42: no noncommutative simple group of order under 60. And Proposition 12.43 turns the same bookkeeping into a classification: for p < q with q ≢ 1 mod p, the only group of order pq is Cpq.
The mission strip is the sentence "how a finite symmetry group is actually taken apart". A finite group is assembled from simple pieces along normal subgroups, and Sylow's theorems are the tool that locates those normal subgroups from the prime factorisation of the order alone. The same instinct — read a global object off local prime-by-prime data — is what a spectral sequence does to a homology group in the third semester, and what localisation does to a module.
