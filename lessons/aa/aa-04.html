Arithmetic in ℤ/nℤ; Fermat's little theorem and RSA (aa-04)

In ℤ/12ℤ, 7 + 7 = 2. Not approximately, not by convention — the two classes really do add to that class, and the clock on your wall has been doing this arithmetic your whole life.

aa-03 built the set ℤ/nℤ. Today we give it + and ·, and the first thing that happens is that the properties of ℤ start behaving selectively: some survive intact, one fails spectacularly in ℤ/4ℤ, and one that ℤ never had at all appears out of nowhere in ℤ/5ℤ. By the end of the lesson a single theorem will explain all three, and the same arithmetic will be encrypting a message.

Predict before we start. In ℤ/nℤ, ask that every nonzero class have a multiplicative inverse. For which n does that hold?

Exactly the primes, and it is Theorem 2.15 — one of three equivalent statements, the other two being "n is prime" and "ℤ/nℤ has no product of nonzero classes equal to zero". In ℤ/4ℤ the class [2]₄ has no inverse: no row of the multiplication table headed by 2 contains a 1. In ℤ/5ℤ every nonzero row does. Note this property fails in ℤ itself — 2 has no integer inverse — so it is something the quotient gains. — Aluffi §2.4, Theorem 2.15, p. 32

Why this matters for the mission: Finite rings are the coefficient worlds Z/2, Z/p that TDA computes homology over.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Giving ℤ/nℤ its operations — and earning the right to

We want [a]n + [b]n to be [a + b]n. That is written in terms of representatives, and aa-03 ended on the warning that a class has infinitely many names. So the definition has to be paid for first.

Lemma 2.9. Let n ≥ 0 and let a, a′, b, b′ be integers. If a ≡ a′ mod n and b ≡ b′ mod n, then

(i) a + b ≡ a′ + b′ mod n,    (ii) a·b ≡ a′·b′ mod n.

— Aluffi §2.3, Lemma 2.9, p. 26
Proof. Write a′ − a = nk and b′ − b = nℓ. For (i),

(a′ + b′) − (a + b) = (a′ − a) + (b′ − b) = nk + nℓ = n(k + ℓ),

so n ∣ (a′ + b′) − (a + b). For (ii), substitute a′ = a + nk and b′ = b + nℓ:

a′b′ − ab = (a + nk)(b + nℓ) − ab = naℓ + nbk + n²kℓ = n(aℓ + bk + nkℓ),

so n ∣ a′b′ − ab. ∎ — Aluffi §2.3, p. 27

Notice that (ii) is where all the content is: addition of remainders is obvious, and it is multiplication that could plausibly have leaked. The reason it does not is that the error terms are all multiples of n by construction.

Definition 2.10 (the operations). For n ≥ 0 and classes [a]n, [b]n in ℤ/nℤ, define

[a]n + [b]n := [a + b]n,     [a]n · [b]n := [a · b]n.

The + and · on the right are the familiar operations in ℤ; the ones on the left are new. — Aluffi §2.3, Definition 2.10, p. 27

Aluffi asks you to ponder this carefully at least once, and the way he makes the subtlety visible is by trying the same trick on a partition where it fails.

Example 2.11 — the same recipe, on a different partition, producing nonsense. Split ℤ into two classes by sign:

A = {…, −3, −2, −1, 0}    and    B = {1, 2, 3, 4, …}.

This is a partition, so it comes from a perfectly good equivalence relation. Now try the analogue of Definition 2.10. Using the names 0 for A and 1 for B,

A + B = [0] + [1] = [0 + 1] = [1] = B.

But A is also named by −4 and B is also named by 2, so with those names

A + B = [−4] + [2] = [−4 + 2] = [−2] = A.

So A + B is both B and A, and A ≠ B. The recipe is not well defined: its answer depends on which names you happened to pick. We throw it away. — Aluffi §2.3, Example 2.11, pp. 27–28

Aluffi's phrasing is worth keeping: the operation should depend on the class itself, "not on your mood of the moment on how you decide to call the class."

Claim 2.12. The operations of Definition 2.10 do not depend on the choice of representatives: if [a]n = [a′]n and [b]n = [b′]n, then [a + b]n = [a′ + b′]n and [a·b]n = [a′·b′]n. — Aluffi §2.3, Claim 2.12, p. 28
Proof. This is Lemma 2.9 restated in the language of classes, using aa-03's identity (2.1) to move between "≡" and "same class" in both directions. ∎

That is the whole architecture of the section: Lemma 2.9 is the work, Claim 2.12 is the payoff, and Example 2.11 is the control showing the payoff was not free. Congruence mod n is special precisely because it is compatible with + and · in ℤ; an arbitrary equivalence relation is not.

Self-check 1. Why exactly does the sign partition of Example 2.11 fail to inherit an addition?

A = [0] = [−4] and B = [1] = [2], yet 0 + 1 lands in B while −4 + 2 lands in A. One input, two outputs — so the recipe is not a function on classes. The class count is irrelevant (ℤ/2ℤ also has two classes and works fine); what matters is whether the relation is compatible with the operation, which is exactly what Lemma 2.9 establishes for congruence and nothing establishes here. — Aluffi §2.3, p. 28

What survives, what breaks, what appears — and one theorem for all three

Here are the tables for ℤ/4ℤ, writing a for [a]₄ as Aluffi does: — Aluffi §2.4, p. 29

Read the entry "3·3 = 1" as [3]₄·[3]₄ = [9]₄ = [1]₄, which is Definition 2.10 doing its job.

What breaks. In ℤ we have the implication Aluffi numbers (2.2):

ab = 0 ⟹ a = 0 or b = 0.

It holds in ℤ/3ℤ — list the four products of nonzero classes and no zero appears. It fails in ℤ/4ℤ: [2]₄·[2]₄ = [4]₄ = [0]₄ with [2]₄ ≠ [0]₄. In the table, that is the 0 sitting in the middle of the multiplication grid, away from row 0 and column 0. It is not rare: [2]₆·[3]₆ = [0]₆, [3]₁₂·[4]₁₂ = [0]₁₂, [2]₁₀·[5]₁₀ = [0]₁₀. — Aluffi §2.4, pp. 29–31

What survives. The long list Aluffi labels (2.3) — closure, associativity of +, an additive identity 0, additive inverses, commutativity of +, associativity of ·, a multiplicative identity 1, and both distributive laws, plus the separately-listed commutativity of · — all hold in ℤ. And: — Aluffi §2.4, p. 30

Theorem 2.13. For every positive integer n, all of those properties hold for ℤ/nℤ with 0 = [0]n and 1 = [1]n. — Aluffi §2.4, Theorem 2.13, p. 30
Proof — and why Aluffi says it "proves itself". Closure is immediate from Definition 2.10. Every other item reduces to the same property in ℤ. Distributivity, in full: given classes a, b, c pick representatives a = [k]n, b = [ℓ]n, c = [m]n. Then

a·(b + c) = [k]n·[ℓ + m]n = [k(ℓ + m)]n = [kℓ + km]n = [kℓ]n + [km]n = a·b + a·c,

where the middle equality is distributivity in ℤ and every other equality is Definition 2.10. Each remaining property goes the same way. ∎ — Aluffi §2.4, p. 31

Remark 2.14. From here on we write 0 and 1 for [0]n and [1]n. In "0 = [0]n" the two zeros mean different things — the left one is an element we choose to call 0 in ℤ/nℤ, the right one is the integer. Spend a moment on that; the abuse is universal and permanent. — Aluffi §2.4, Remark 2.14, p. 30

The list in (2.3), minus the lone commutativity item, is the definition of a ring (§3.1); with it, a commutative ring. ℤ, every ℤ/nℤ, and ℚ, ℝ, ℂ are all commutative rings — which is aa-05's business.

What appears. Nothing in (2.3) asks for multiplicative inverses. Aluffi numbers that missing property (2.4):

for all a ≠ 0 there is a′ with a·a′ = a′·a = 1.

It is false in ℤ (2n = 1 has no integer solution) and true in ℚ. In a multiplication table it says: every row except row 0 contains a 1. Row 2 of ℤ/4ℤ above contains no 1. But every nonzero row of ℤ/5ℤ does, so ℤ/5ℤ satisfies a property ℤ does not — it is a field. — Aluffi §2.4, p. 32

Theorem 2.15. Let n > 1. The following are equivalent:

(i) n is prime;   (ii) in ℤ/nℤ, ab = 0 ⟹ a = 0 or b = 0;   (iii) in ℤ/nℤ, every a ≠ 0 has a multiplicative inverse.

— Aluffi §2.4, Theorem 2.15, p. 32
Proof (the cycle (i) ⟹ (iii) ⟹ (ii) ⟹ (i)).

(i) ⟹ (iii). Let n = p be prime, hence irreducible (aa-02's Theorem 1.21), and let a = [ℓ]p ≠ 0, i.e. p ∤ ℓ. By Lemma 1.18, gcd(p, ℓ) = 1, so by Bézout (Theorem 1.8) there are integers q, k with 1 = qp + kℓ. Read that modulo p: since [qp]p = [0]p,

[1]p = [qp]p + [kℓ]p = [k]p·[ℓ]p,

so [k]p is the inverse of a.

(iii) ⟹ (ii). If ab = 0 and a ≠ 0, let a′ be an inverse of a; then b = 1·b = (a′a)b = a′(ab) = a′·0 = 0. (The last step, that a·0 = 0 in ℤ/nℤ, is Aluffi's Exercise 2.11 — it does not come free from the list, it is deduced from distributivity.)

(ii) ⟹ (i). Suppose n ∣ km; we must show n ∣ k or n ∣ m, which is Definition 1.19. Put a = [k]n, b = [m]n. Then ab = [km]n = [0]n = 0, so by (ii) a = 0 or b = 0 — that is, n ∣ k or n ∣ m. ∎ — Aluffi §2.4, p. 33

What each arrow used. (i) ⟹ (iii) is the only one that touched ℤ: it ran on Bézout. (iii) ⟹ (ii) never mentioned modular arithmetic at all — it is a formal manipulation using only the properties in (2.3), which is why the same argument reappears verbatim for arbitrary rings in aa-06. (ii) ⟹ (i) is Definition 1.19 read through the projection.

Proposition 2.16. Let n > 1. A class [ℓ]n has a multiplicative inverse in ℤ/nℤ if and only if gcd(n, ℓ) = 1. — Aluffi §2.4, Proposition 2.16, p. 34
Proof. (⟸) If gcd(n, ℓ) = 1 then Bézout gives qn + kℓ = 1, so [k]n[ℓ]n = [kℓ]n = [1 − qn]n = [1]n, and commutativity gives the other side. (⟹) If [k]n[ℓ]n = [1]n then n ∣ (1 − kℓ), say 1 − kℓ = qn, so 1 = qn + kℓ is a linear combination of n and ℓ; Corollary 1.9 then gives gcd(n, ℓ) = 1. ∎

This is the (i) ⟹ (iii) argument with the primality stripped out, and it is strictly more informative: it tells you which classes are invertible for every n, not just that all of them are when n is prime. In ℤ/26ℤ, [7] is invertible (gcd = 1) and [13] is not (gcd = 13).

Remark 2.17 (cancellation). When p is prime, ab = ac with a ≠ 0 forces b = c: from a(b − c) = 0 and (ii). It genuinely fails otherwise — [2]₆·[2]₆ = [4]₆ = [2]₆·[5]₆ while [2]₆ ≠ [5]₆. — Aluffi §2.4, Remark 2.17, p. 34

Faded example — inverting a class by hand. Find the inverse of [7]₂₆ in ℤ/26ℤ.

First check it exists: gcd(26, 7) = ⟨your step⟩, and Proposition 2.16 says an inverse exists exactly when that is 1.

Now run the Euclidean algorithm backwards, as in aa-02: 26 = 7·3 + 5; 7 = 5·1 + 2; 5 = 2·2 + 1. So 1 = 5 − 2·2, and 2 = 7 − 5, giving 1 = 5 − 2(7 − 5) = ⟨your step: collect the 5s⟩·5 − 2·7. Then 5 = 26 − 3·7, so 1 = 3(26 − 3·7) − 2·7 = 3·26 − ⟨your step⟩·7.

Read that mod 26: [1]₂₆ = [−11]₂₆·[7]₂₆, and [−11]₂₆ = [⟨your step⟩]₂₆. Check your answer by multiplying it by 7 and reducing.

Self-check 2. Theorem 2.15 assumes n > 1. Which case is excluded by that, and why?

Both are excluded and for different reasons. ℤ/0ℤ is a copy of ℤ, where ab = 0 does force a factor to vanish but 2 has no inverse — so (ii) and (iii) come apart. Aluffi points at the cause: 0 is prime under Definition 1.19 but is not irreducible, and the (i) ⟹ (iii) arrow ran on irreducibility. For n = 1, ℤ/1ℤ has one element, so 0 = 1 there and (ii) and (iii) are vacuously true; that trivial ring is deliberately excluded from being a domain or a field in §3.3. — Aluffi §2.4, p. 34

Fermat's little theorem, and why RSA works

Theorem 2.18 (Fermat's little theorem). Let a, p be integers with p positive and prime. Then [a]pp = [a]p — that is, ap ≡ a mod p. — Aluffi §2.5, Theorem 2.18, p. 35

Primality is essential: 2⁴ = 16 is not congruent to 2 modulo 4. And the theorem saves real work — with a = 100 and p = 13 it tells you at once, with no computation, that 100¹³ ≡ 100 mod 13. — Aluffi §2.5, p. 35

Proof of Theorem 2.18. If p ∣ a then [a]p = [0]p and the claim reads [0]pp = [0]p, which is true. So assume p ∤ a; it suffices to prove [a]pp−1 = [1]p, since multiplying both sides by [a]p then gives the statement.

The clever step. Consider the p − 1 classes

[a]p, [2a]p, …, [(p−1)a]p.

They are distinct. By cancellation (Remark 2.17, available because p is prime and [a]p ≠ 0), [ℓa]p = [ka]p implies [ℓ]p = [k]p; contrapositively, distinct [ℓ]p, [k]p give distinct [ℓa]p, [ka]p. And [1]p, …, [p−1]p are distinct by aa-03's Lemma 2.6.

The cleverest step. All p − 1 of them are nonzero, and there are only p − 1 nonzero classes in ℤ/pℤ. So the list [a]p, …, [(p−1)a]p is the list [1]p, …, [p−1]p in some order. Multiply each list out; the products must agree:

[(p−1)!]p · [a]pp−1 = [(p−1)!]p.

Finally [(p−1)!]p ≠ [0]p, because p is prime and divides none of 1, …, p−1. Cancel it (Remark 2.17 again) to get [a]pp−1 = [1]p. ∎ — Aluffi §2.5, pp. 35–36

Both uses of cancellation are uses of primality, funnelled through Theorem 2.15(ii). Take primality away and the reordering step collapses — in ℤ/4ℤ, multiplying by [2]₄ sends {1, 2, 3} to {2, 0, 2}, which is neither a permutation nor even inside the nonzero classes.

Aside — testing primality without factoring. If 1 < a < n and [a]nn−1 ≠ [1]n, then Theorem 2.18 says n is not prime; such an a is called a witness. Example: 2 witnesses that 4 is composite, since 2³ = 8 ≢ 1 mod 4. This matters because computing a power mod n is fast while factoring n is not — so a large n can be shown composite quickly, with no factorisation.

Aluffi's estimate is that most composite n have roughly n/2 witnesses, so ten random trials give confidence about 1 − 1/2¹⁰. Read the word "most" carefully: it is a statement about the bulk of composites, not about all of them, and the argument gives a high-confidence verdict rather than a proof. That is why the method is used to generate candidate primes and not to certify them. — Aluffi §2.5, p. 36

Theorem 2.19 (RSA algorithm). Let p ≠ q be positive primes and let n = pq, k = (p−1)(q−1), let e be relatively prime to k, let d satisfy de ≡ 1 mod k, let a be any integer, and let b be a representative of [a]ne. Then a ≡ bd mod n. — Aluffi §2.5, Theorem 2.19, p. 38
Proof. Since b ≡ ae, we have bd ≡ ade mod n, so it is enough to show ade ≡ a mod n.

It suffices to show ade ≡ a mod p and mod q: for then p and q both divide ade − a, and since p, q are distinct primes their product n divides it too. That last step deserves its name — write ade − a = py; then q ∣ py, and gcd(q, p) = 1 because p, q are distinct primes, so Corollary 1.11 gives q ∣ y and hence pq ∣ ade − a. It fails without coprimality: 4 and 6 both divide 12, while 24 does not. — Aluffi §1.3, Corollary 1.11, p. 10

Take p. If p ∣ a then both sides are 0 mod p. Otherwise, since de ≡ 1 mod k there is an integer ℓ with de = 1 + kℓ, and

ade = a1+kℓ = a·(ap−1)(q−1)ℓ ≡ a·1(q−1)ℓ = a mod p,

using Fermat's little theorem in the form ap−1 ≡ 1 mod p, valid because p is prime and p ∤ a. The argument for q is identical with the roles swapped. ∎ — Aluffi §2.5, pp. 38–39

Where the security sits. The eavesdropper may know n, e and the encrypted b and still be stuck. But k = (p−1)(q−1) requires p and q, and once k is known, d is just the inverse of e mod k — one run of the Euclidean algorithm. So factoring n breaks the scheme completely. No efficient classical factoring method is known; that, and nothing stronger, is what the security rests on. — Aluffi §2.5, p. 39

Worked example — RSA on numbers you can check. Take p = 3, q = 11. Then n = 33 and k = (3−1)(11−1) = 20. Choose e = 3; gcd(3, 20) = 1, so e is admissible. For d we need 3d ≡ 1 mod 20, and d = 7 works since 21 = 20 + 1.

Encrypt the message a = 5: b ≡ 5³ = 125 = 3·33 + 26, so b = 26.

Decrypt: 26⁷ mod 33. Do it in stages — 26² = 676 = 20·33 + 16, so 26² ≡ 16; 26⁴ ≡ 16² = 256 = 7·33 + 25, so 26⁴ ≡ 25; then 26⁷ = 26⁴·26²·26 ≡ 25·16·26. Now 25·16 = 400 = 12·33 + 4, so that is 4·26 = 104 = 3·33 + 5, giving 5. The message is recovered. ✓

Watch what the theorem did and did not promise: it says a ≡ bd mod n, so you recover a's class. Recovering a itself needs a to have been in the range 0 ≤ a < n to begin with — which is why the scheme insists messages be broken into blocks smaller than n.

You try (solo). Run the same machine with p = 5, q = 11, so n = 55 and k = 40. Choose e = 3 and find d. Then encrypt a = 7 and decrypt it back. Two checks along the way: your d must satisfy 3d ≡ 1 mod 40, and your intermediate powers should always be reduced mod 55 before the next multiplication — the whole point is that you never handle a large number.

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi states Fermat's little theorem twice — as ap ≡ a mod p for all a (Theorem 2.18), and in Fermat's own 1640 form, ap−1 ≡ 1 mod p when p ∤ a — and defers the equivalence to Exercise 2.18. Prove the two forms are equivalent, and say exactly which part of Theorem 2.15 you need, and in which direction.

Reveal model answer

Second form ⟹ first. Let a be any integer. If p ∣ a then ap ≡ 0 ≡ a mod p and we are done. If p ∤ a, multiply ap−1 ≡ 1 through by a to get ap ≡ a. No hypothesis beyond the second form is used here.

First ⟹ second. Suppose ap ≡ a mod p and p ∤ a. In ℤ/pℤ this reads [a]p·[a]pp−1 = [a]p·1, with [a]p ≠ 0. To strike out the [a]p we need cancellation, which is Remark 2.17, resting on part (ii) of Theorem 2.15 — the implication ab = 0 ⟹ a = 0 or b = 0, available because p is prime. Cancelling gives [a]pp−1 = [1]p. ∎

Grade yourself on the direction, not the algebra. The first implication is free; the second is the one that consumes primality, and if your proof cancelled a factor without saying why cancellation is legal, it is not finished. In ℤ/4ℤ you may not cancel a [2]₄, and that is not a technicality — it is the same failure as [2]₄·[2]₄ = 0.

Self-check 3. In the proof of Fermat's little theorem, why must [a]p, [2a]p, …, [(p−1)a]p be a rearrangement of [1]p, …, [p−1]p?

Distinctness comes from cancellation, non-vanishing from Theorem 2.15(ii) (a product of nonzero classes is nonzero when p is prime), and then counting closes the argument — p − 1 distinct items inside a set of size p − 1 exhaust it. Nothing is claimed about a generating anything; that would be a much stronger statement, and it is not needed. — Aluffi §2.5, p. 36

Self-check 4. An eavesdropper intercepts n, e and the encrypted b. What single ability would let them read the message?

Factor n and you have p and q, hence k = (p−1)(q−1), hence d as the inverse of e mod k by one Euclidean-algorithm run — and then Theorem 2.19 decrypts for you as easily as for the intended recipient. That is the whole of RSA's security: not a proof that the scheme is unbreakable, but the absence of a known fast classical factoring algorithm. Aluffi notes that Shor-style factoring on a quantum machine is the reason the qualifier "classical" is there. — Aluffi §2.5, p. 39

Self-check 5. Why does the RSA proof split into "mod p" and "mod q" instead of working mod n directly?

Fermat's little theorem is a statement about prime moduli, and n = pq is composite by construction — so it cannot be applied to n. Aluffi applies it separately to each prime factor, gets p ∣ (ade − a) and q ∣ (ade − a), and then reassembles: since p and q are distinct primes, their product divides the difference too. The reassembly step is the one worth flagging — it is the Chinese Remainder Theorem in embryo, and aa-10 will name it. — Aluffi §2.5, pp. 38–39

Then check yourself
  1. The operations exist because congruence is compatible with them (Lemma 2.9 ⟹ Claim 2.12). Example 2.11 is the control: the same recipe on the sign partition gives A + B = A and A + B = B. "Well defined" means independent of the representative, and it must be checked, not assumed.
  2. Theorem 2.15: for n > 1, n prime ⟺ no zero divisors in ℤ/nℤ ⟺ every nonzero class is invertible. The prime-to-invertible arrow is Bézout; the invertible-to-no-zero-divisors arrow uses only the ring properties and will be reused verbatim for general rings. Proposition 2.16 refines it: [ℓ]n is invertible exactly when gcd(n, ℓ) = 1, for every n.
  3. Fermat (Theorem 2.18): ap ≡ a mod p for prime p, proved by showing multiplication by a permutes the nonzero classes and cancelling (p−1)!. RSA (Theorem 2.19) is this applied twice, once at p and once at q, with de ≡ 1 mod (p−1)(q−1) supplying the exponent that undoes e.
