Cyclic, dihedral, symmetric, and free groups (aa-25)

The zoo: clocks, mirror symmetries, shuffles — and the wildest of all, the free group.

aa-24 ended with Cayley's theorem: every group is a group of permutations. That is a statement about all groups at once, and like most such statements it tells you nothing about any particular one. This unit does the opposite. It walks through four named families, each with a concrete picture attached, and each of which you will meet again by name in the third semester.

Predict before we start. A regular square admits rotations and flips. How many symmetries does it have in total?

Eight. The four rotations by 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees, and four reflections: two through opposite vertices, two through opposite edge-midpoints. This is the group Aluffi calls D8 — his subscript is the order, so the symmetry group of the n-gon is D2n. Not all 24 permutations of the vertices arise, because a symmetry must preserve which vertices are adjacent; you will see exactly which 8 of the 24 survive, drawn, further down. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.32, p. 242

Why this matters for the mission: Free groups are literally the fundamental groups of graphs; symmetric-group signs orient simplices.

25 min Segment 1 — Things that spin, and things that flip

Carter organises this material by what the group is the symmetry group of, and it is worth taking his ordering first because it makes the definitions inevitable rather than arbitrary. The cyclic groups, he says, describe objects that have only rotational symmetry: a propeller, a pinwheel, the three-armed molecule of boric acid. There is exactly one thing you can do to such an object, and everything else is that one thing repeated. — Carter §5.1 "Cyclic groups", p. 64

So Cn has n elements, named 0, 1, …, n − 1, where each number counts repetitions of the single fundamental rotation; there is no element n because n rotations return the propeller to where it started. Carter notes the common alternative name Zn for the same group, drawn from the integers. — Carter §5.1, p. 65 That is aa-05's Z/nZ, arrived at from the opposite direction: aa-05 built it by quotienting, Carter builds it by turning a pinwheel, and the two constructions agree.

Now allow flipping as well. Objects that look the same when turned over have bilateral symmetry as well as rotational, and the regular polygons are the easiest examples. Carter writes Dn for the symmetries of a regular n-gon and observes that it contains twice as many actions as Cn, namely 2n. — Carter §5.3 "Dihedral groups", p. 74

Definition 11.32. Let n > 2 be an integer. The dihedral group D2n is the group of symmetries of a regular n-gon. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.32, p. 242

Read the subscripts carefully — the two books disagree. Aluffi's subscript is the order of the group; Carter's is the number of sides. Aluffi's D2n and Carter's Dn are the same group. Aluffi's D6 is the symmetry group of the equilateral triangle — six elements — which Carter calls D3. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Example 11.33, p. 242 — Carter §5.3, p. 74 Both conventions are in print and neither is going away; the only defence is to check, every time, whether a stated D8 has eight elements or eight sides. From here on this lesson uses Aluffi's convention, as aa-23 and aa-24 already did.

The next case is the square. Aluffi's D8 consists of eight symmetries of the square: the identity, three rotations, and four flips. Because every symmetry permutes the four vertices, and a symmetry is determined by what it does to them, D8 sits inside S4 — as exactly 8 of the 24 permutations. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Example 11.34, p. 243

Aluffi calls the vertex-chasing needed to multiply two such symmetries "mental gymnastics", and then supplies the escape route.

Proposition 11.35. For n > 2, let x be the flip about the axis through the centre and vertex 1, and y the clockwise rotation by 360/n degrees. Then D2n = ⟨x, y⟩, the relations

x² = e, yⁿ = e, xy = y⁻¹x

hold, and every element of D2n is written uniquely as xⁱyʲ with 0 ≤ i < 2 and 0 ≤ j < n. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Proposition 11.35, p. 245

Count them: two choices of i times n choices of j gives 2n elements, recovering Carter's doubling. Aluffi's proof of uniqueness is the cancellation you would expect — if xⁱyʲ = xkyℓ then xi−k = yℓ−j, and since x is not a rotation both sides must be e, forcing i ≡ k mod 2 and j ≡ ℓ mod n. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 245

The relation xy = y⁻¹x is the whole content of non-commutativity here. Carter reaches the identical relation by a physical argument: hold the polygon up between yourself and a friend facing you; performing f swaps your viewpoint with theirs, and a clockwise turn from their side looks counterclockwise from yours. Algebraically, frf = r⁻¹. — Carter §5.3, p. 75 Multiply Carter's relation on the right by f and use f² = e and you have Aluffi's, with f for x and r for y. One relation, two derivations: one by cancelling exponents, one by imagining a friend holding a triangle.

Worked, then faded — Example 11.36. Multiply xy³ and xy⁵ in D14, so n = 7 and y⁷ = e. The rule to apply repeatedly is yx = xy⁻¹, which walks each y leftwards past each x, flipping its sign.

Aluffi's computation: (xy³)(xy⁵) = xyy·xy·y⁵ = xyy·xy⁻¹·y⁵ = xy·xy⁻¹·y⁻¹y⁵ = x²·y⁻¹y⁻¹y⁻¹·y⁵ = y². — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Example 11.36, p. 245

Now fade it and do the same product in D10, where n = 5. Compute (xy³)(xy⁵) again. First reduce y⁵: in D10, y⁵ = ⟨?⟩. So the product is (xy³)·x·⟨?⟩, and one application of the walking rule gives ⟨?⟩.

Checky⁵ = e in D10, so xy⁵ = x and the product is (xy³)x. Walking three y's past the x: (xy³)x = x·y³x = x·xy⁻³ = x²y⁻³ = y⁻³ = y², because y⁻³ = y⁵⁻³ = y². The answer is y² again, and it is not a coincidence. The general rule that falls out of Proposition 11.35 is (xya)(xyb) = yb−a, since xyaxyb = x·xy−a·yb = yb−a. In D14 that is y⁵⁻³ = y². In D10 the exponent 5 is already 0, so it is y⁰⁻³ = y⁻³ = y². Different reductions, one rule.

Self-check 1. What does the relation xy = y⁻¹x tell you?

Rearranged, it says xyx⁻¹ = y⁻¹ (using x⁻¹ = x): flipping, rotating, and flipping back is rotating the other way. That is Carter's friend-holding-the-triangle picture verbatim. Its algebraic use is normalisation: any word in x and y collapses to the form xⁱyʲ, which is why Proposition 11.35 can list the group. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Proposition 11.35, p. 245 — Carter §5.3, p. 75

25 min Segment 2 — Permutations, matrices, and the sign

Drop the requirement that the polygon keep its shape and you are left with all rearrangements of the vertices. Carter defines a permutation as an action that rearranges a collection of things and checks it against his four rules for a group: the list of rearrangements is predefined, each is unambiguous, two done in succession are again one, and each is reversible. The group of all permutations of n things is Sn, the symmetric group, and its order is n factorial — so S4 has 24 elements and S5 has 120. — Carter §5.4 "Symmetric and alternating groups", pp. 78–79 — Carter §5.4, p. 80

Aluffi gives Sn a second face. Let Sn act on an n-dimensional real vector space by permuting the standard basis vectors. The action is faithful — aa-24's word — so it gives an injective homomorphism Sn → GLn(R) identifying Sn with the permutation matrices: the matrices with only 0 and 1 entries, exactly one 1 in each row and exactly one 1 in each column. Composition of permutations becomes ordinary matrix multiplication. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 247

This buys something immediately, because matrices have determinants.

Lemma 11.37. The determinant of a permutation matrix is ±1. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Lemma 11.37, p. 248

Proof. Induct on n. For n = 1 the only permutation matrix is (1). For larger n, expand the determinant along the first row: that row has a single nonzero entry, equal to 1, so det A equals ± the determinant of an (n − 1) × (n − 1) permutation matrix, which is ±1 by induction. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 248

Definition 11.39. The sign of σ in Sn, written (−1)σ, is the determinant of its permutation matrix. σ is even if the sign is 1, odd if it is −1. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.39, p. 248

Because the determinant is a group homomorphism, so is the sign: (−1)στ = (−1)σ(−1)τ. That single sentence is what makes the sign useful, and it is the reason the next definition produces a subgroup rather than merely a subset. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 248

Definition 11.40. The alternating group An is the subgroup of Sn consisting of the even permutations. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.40, p. 249

It is a subgroup precisely because the sign is a homomorphism: An is its kernel, and kernels are subgroups by aa-23's Example 11.9. Carter arrives at the same group without ever computing a determinant — he squares every element of S3 and observes that four of the six square to the identity, so the right-hand column of his table contains only three distinct permutations, and those three are A3. He also notes that A3 therefore has the same structure as C3. — Carter §5.4, p. 80 — Carter §5.4, p. 81 Aluffi records the same coincidence: A3 is the cyclic copy inside S3. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.40, p. 249

Aluffi labels the square's vertices 1, 2, 3, 4 clockwise, with x the flip fixing vertex 1 and y the clockwise rotation, so pressing y first and x second builds the element xy.
What you should seeThe two orders give different permutations, which is the whole point of D8 being non-abelian. With x fixing vertex 1 and swapping 2 with 4, and y sending 1↦2↦3↦4↦1, the composite xy sends 1↦4, 2↦3, 3↦2, 4↦1 — the product of transpositions (1 4)(2 3), hence even. The other order gives yx, which sends 1↦2, 2↦1, 3↦4, 4↦3, that is (1 2)(3 4): also even, also an involution, and not the same element. Both squaring to the identity is Proposition 11.35 in disguise, since xyxy = x(yx)y = x(xy⁻¹)y = x²y⁻¹y = e. The panel should also report xy in normal form as xy and yx as xy³, since yx = xy⁻¹ and y⁴ = e.

Self-check 2. Why is An a subgroup of Sn, rather than just a subset?

Having half the elements is a consequence, not a reason — plenty of half-sized subsets are not subgroups, as Carter warns when he says random choosing will rarely give one. The reason is structural: the determinant is a homomorphism, so the sign is a homomorphism Sn → {1, −1}, and An is the set where it takes the value 1. Lemma 11.37 is what makes the target group {1, −1} in the first place. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.40, p. 249 — Carter §5.4, p. 80

25 min Segment 3 — Cycles, transpositions, and the free group

Writing a permutation as a two-row array is unbearable past n = 5. The standard compression:

Definition 11.41. An r-cycle (a₁ … ar) is the permutation cyclically permuting r elements, sending ai to ai+1 and ar back to a₁, and fixing everything else. A transposition is a 2-cycle. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.41, p. 249

Aluffi immediately flags the notation's two quirks. It does not record what n is — (25) could live in S5 or in S1000 — and since a cycle has no beginning, the same cycle has r different names depending where you start. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Remark 11.42, p. 250 The second remark is the one that does work:

Remark 11.43. Two cycles are disjoint if their nontrivial orbits are disjoint, and disjoint cycles commute — each acts as the identity on the elements the other moves. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Remark 11.43, p. 250

Proposition 11.44. Every permutation other than the identity may be written as a product of disjoint nontrivial cycles. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Proposition 11.44, p. 250

This is aa-24's orbit decomposition wearing different clothes: the cyclic subgroup generated by σ acts on {1, …, n}, its orbits partition the set, and σ restricted to each orbit is a cycle. Disjointness is automatic because orbits are disjoint, and by Remark 11.43 the order of the factors does not matter. Aluffi's running example is (14)(23576) in S7, which has order 10 — the two-cycle returns after 2 steps, the five-cycle after 5, and they commute, so the whole permutation returns after 10. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 250

Cycles then decompose further, into the smallest pieces there are.

Lemma 11.45. (a₁ … ar) = (a₁ar) ⋯ (a₁a₃)(a₁a₂). — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Lemma 11.45, p. 251

Lemma 11.46. An r-cycle is even or odd according to whether r is odd or even: if σ is an r-cycle then (−1)σ = (−1)r−1. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Lemma 11.46, p. 251

Proposition 11.47. Sn is generated by transpositions. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Proposition 11.47, p. 251

The three stack: Proposition 11.44 breaks any permutation into cycles, Lemma 11.45 breaks any cycle into transpositions, and Proposition 11.47 is the two of them read together. Lemma 11.46 then makes the sign computable without ever writing down a matrix — a transposition is odd, and an r-cycle costs r − 1 of them. Aluffi's example (14)(23576) is a 2-cycle times a 5-cycle, so its sign is (−1)¹ · (−1)⁴ = −1: odd, and therefore outside A7.

Guided proof. Aluffi says the verification of Lemma 11.45 "suffices to show that the image of ai under the right-hand side" is correct, and does not write out every case. Do it, then deduce Lemma 11.46 from it. Remember that the right-hand side is applied right to left, so (a₁a₂) acts first.

Case 1: what does the right-hand side do to a₁? Only the rightmost factor (a₁a₂) moves it, to a₂ — and then no later factor mentions a₂, so a₁ ↦ a₂. Now finish:

Model answer

Case 2, 1 < i < r. Reading right to left, the first factor mentioning ai is (a₁ai), which sends ai to a₁. The very next factor is (a₁ai+1), which sends a₁ to ai+1. No later factor mentions ai+1, so the total effect is ai ↦ ai+1, as required.

Case 3, ar. The only factor mentioning ar is the leftmost, (a₁ar), and it is applied last. Everything to its right fixes ar, so ar arrives untouched and is sent to a₁. Correct.

Case 4. An element outside {a₁, …, ar} is mentioned by no factor, so it is fixed — as it must be, since the cycle fixes it too.

Lemma 11.46. The right-hand side lists the transpositions (a₁a₂), (a₁a₃), …, (a₁ar): one for each index from 2 to r, so r − 1 of them. A transposition is odd — its permutation matrix is the identity with two rows swapped, and swapping two rows negates a determinant, so the sign is −1. The sign is a homomorphism (Definition 11.39 and the remark following it), so the sign of a product of r − 1 transpositions is (−1)r−1. Hence an r-cycle is even exactly when r is odd.

Proposition 11.47 is now immediate: every permutation is a product of cycles by Proposition 11.44, and every cycle is a product of transpositions by Lemma 11.45.

The free group. One family remains, and it is built from nothing at all. Take a set A of letters. A word is a string of letters and their inverses; it is reduced when no letter stands next to its own inverse. Multiply two words by juxtaposing them and reducing: b⁻³a⁵b⁵ times b⁻⁵a²b⁻¹ becomes b⁻³a⁵a²b⁻¹, then b⁻³a⁷b⁻¹. The set F(A) of reduced words under this operation is a group; the identity is the empty word, and inverses come from reversing the word and negating each exponent, so the inverse of b⁻³a⁷b⁻¹ is ba⁻⁷b³. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 252

Aluffi calls this the purest way to make a group out of a set: the letters are strung together and no extra information is added. He also warns that F(A) is a rather unwieldy group as soon as A has two or more elements. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, p. 252

The payoff is a universal property, and it is aa-19's free-module story repeated for groups. To define a homomorphism F(A) → G it suffices to choose an image in G for each letter of A; the homomorphism property then determines the value on every word, and because the letters satisfy no relation in F(A) the choices are completely unconstrained. — Aluffi Underground, the universal property of free groups, p. 253

That last clause is the definition of "free" doing its work, and it turns the presentations of Segment 1 into something precise. If A generates G there is a surjective homomorphism F(A) → G, and the relations satisfied by the generators describe its kernel. Read Proposition 11.35 that way: for every n > 2 there is a surjection F({x, y}) → D2n, and x² = e, yⁿ = e, xy = y⁻¹x are a description of what it kills. — Aluffi Underground, Example 11.48, p. 253

And the mission strip is not a metaphor. Aluffi records it himself: the fundamental group of the figure 8 — two circles joined at a point — is isomorphic to the free group F({a, b}) on a two-element set. — Aluffi Underground, Example 11.48 and the paragraph following, p. 253 A loop in a wedge of two circles is a reduced word in a and b, with cancellation of aa⁻¹ corresponding to a homotopy that retracts a doubled-back path. When at1-04 computes fundamental groups of graphs and at1-05 glues spaces with free products, this is the algebra those units will be running.

Self-check 3. What determines a group homomorphism out of a free group F(A)?

Freedom means the absence of relations, so there is nothing for the chosen images to satisfy. Contrast a presented group: a homomorphism out of D2n must send x and y to elements that actually satisfy x² = e, yⁿ = e and xy = y⁻¹x, or it is not well defined. That difference is precisely the kernel of the surjection F({x, y}) → D2n. — Aluffi Underground, the universal property of free groups and Example 11.48, p. 253

Self-check 4. Aluffi's permutation (14)(23576) in S7 — what is its order, and its sign?

The two cycles are disjoint, hence commute (Remark 11.43), so the composite returns to the identity exactly when both do: after a multiple of 2 and a multiple of 5, so after 10. For the sign, Lemma 11.46 gives (−1)2−1 = −1 for the transposition and (−1)5−1 = +1 for the 5-cycle, and the sign is a homomorphism, so the product is −1. The permutation is odd and lies outside A7. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Lemma 11.46, p. 251

Self-check 5. Aluffi writes D8; Carter writes D4. What is the relationship?

Both are the eight symmetries of the square. Aluffi defines D2n as the symmetries of the n-gon, so the subscript is the order; Carter defines Dn the same way, so his subscript is n. Carter's cycle graphs for D4 and D5 — a ring of size n with two-element orbits hanging off the identity — are, in Aluffi's labelling, D8 and D10. — Aluffi Underground §11.3, Definition 11.32, p. 242 — Carter §5.4, p. 78

Then check yourself
  1. Cyclic. Things that only spin — Carter's propeller and pinwheel. Cn has n elements, all powers of one rotation, and is aa-05's Z/nZ arrived at geometrically.
  2. Dihedral. Things that spin and flip. D2n is the symmetry group of the regular n-gon (Definition 11.32), with 2n elements; Example 11.33 is the triangle and Example 11.34 the square, the latter sitting inside S4 as 8 of the 24 permutations. Proposition 11.35 replaces vertex-chasing by x² = e, yⁿ = e, xy = y⁻¹x with unique normal form xⁱyʲ, and Example 11.36 shows the arithmetic in action. Carter's frf = r⁻¹ is the same relation, argued by handing a triangle to a friend. Watch the subscript convention.
  3. Symmetric and alternating. All rearrangements. Permutation matrices embed Sn in GLn(R); Lemma 11.37 says their determinants are ±1, Definition 11.39 calls that the sign, and Definition 11.40 defines An as its kernel. Cycle notation (Definition 11.41) with its quirks (Remark 11.42) and its commuting property (Remark 11.43) makes computation possible: Proposition 11.44 decomposes into disjoint cycles, Lemma 11.45 decomposes cycles into transpositions, Lemma 11.46 reads the sign off the cycle lengths, and Proposition 11.47 concludes that transpositions generate.
  4. Free. Reduced words in an alphabet, multiplied by juxtaposition and cancellation. No relations at all, which is exactly the universal property: a homomorphism out of F(A) is an unconstrained choice of image per letter. Every generated group is a quotient of a free group, and the relations are the kernel — Example 11.48 reads Proposition 11.35 that way.
The mission strip is the fourth and third items in order. The fundamental group of a figure 8 is F({a, b}) on the nose, so the free groups of this unit are the fundamental groups of graphs in Semester 3; and the sign homomorphism of Definition 11.39, which assigns ±1 to a rearrangement of n labels, is what will orient an n-simplex and give simplicial homology its boundary map its signs.
