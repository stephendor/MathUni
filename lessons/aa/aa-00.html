Symmetry: groups before the definition (aa-00)

Note (2026-07-22 restructure): this is the optional motivation aside at the head of the Abstract Algebra module. It predates the switch to Aluffi's rings-first spine, so its forward-pointers use the old unit numbers — the group axioms it previews are now aa-23, symmetric groups aa-25, the isomorphism theorems aa-27. The spine proper begins at aa-01 (the integers).
A rectangle, a light switch, and a dance move walk into a bar — same group.

The rectangle only knows two tricks: flip it over left-to-right, or flip it over top-to-bottom. The light switches on your wall only know one trick each: on or off. A dancer marking a spin only knows how to turn a quarter, a half, three-quarters, or come back to facing front. None of these three things looks remotely like the others — paper, electricity, a body in motion. And yet, if you write down how many ways each one can end up arranged, and what happens when you do one move after another, you get exactly the same pattern, three times over. That repeated pattern has a name. It's called a group, and by the end of this lesson you will have found one with your own hands before anybody hands you a definition for it.

We are going to resist the urge to define anything up front. Instead — like every mathematician before the definition existed — we will play with a rectangle until the rules reveal themselves.

Prediction Gate: Before you touch the rectangle, commit to an answer. Take the sheet of paper, flip it left-to-right, and then flip that top-to-bottom. Where do the corner labels end up?

A half-turn. Neither flip on its own can produce it, and yet the two of them in sequence land exactly there — which is the first hint that the combination of two moves is a move in its own right. Segment 1 has you check this against the canvas; Segment 2 gives the observation its name (Carter's Rule 1.8).

Why this matters for the mission: Optional on-ramp: structure abstracted from examples, the move algebra makes over and over.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Symmetries as actions: the rectangle's four moves

Take a rectangle — an ordinary sheet of paper works — and number its four corners 1, 2, 3, 4 going clockwise from the top left. This is Carter's "not-so-famous toy": a puzzle much like Rubik's Cube, but built from a single sheet of paper. — Carter §2.2, p. 14

The rectangle puzzle has exactly two legal moves: flip it over horizontally (as if turning a page, left edge sweeping over to the right) and flip it over vertically (as if turning it top-to-bottom). Carter is careful to note that "horizontal" and "vertical" here describe the motion of your hands, not an axis of rotation. — Carter §2.2, p. 14, and n. 2

Watch what these two moves do to the corner labels. Click the buttons below the canvas and track where each numbered corner travels.

You should notice something important happening on that fourth button. "Reset" is not cheating — Carter insists you are never allowed to just pick the rectangle up and set it down solved; every move must be a genuine flip. But there is a genuine flip that leaves every corner exactly where it started: do nothing at all. This "do-nothing" move counts as a legitimate action in its own right, on exactly the same footing as the two flips. Hold onto that thought; Segment 2 promotes it to a named citizen of the group.

Notice also that "Rotate 180°" is not one of the two generators — you cannot spin the rectangle like that with a single flip. But it turns out to be reachable by combining the two flips: flip horizontal, then flip vertical, and you land in exactly the same corner-arrangement as a half-turn. Try it — flip horizontal, then flip vertical, and compare against the "Rotate 180°" button. Same destination, two different routes. This is the seed of Carter's Rule 1.8: any sequence of consecutive actions is also an action, whether or not it was on the original list. — Carter §1.4, Rule 1.8, p. 6

Four moves, four configurations — no more, no fewer

Carter's method for studying an object's symmetry is always the same: start solved, apply each generator, see where you land, and keep going until every question "where does this move take me from here?" has an answer. Applied to the rectangle, this exploration terminates after exactly four configurations: solved, horizontal-flipped, vertical-flipped, and both (the half-turn). — Carter §2.3, pp. 15–18 Carter draws this exploration as a map — he calls it a Cayley diagram — with nodes for configurations and arrows for moves. — Carter §2.4, p. 18

Here is the remarkable part. Take two light switches on a wall, side by side, and allow yourself two actions: flip switch 1, flip switch 2. Map out the four configurations (off-off, on-off, off-on, on-on) exactly the way we mapped the rectangle. Carter shows the resulting map has exactly the same shape as the rectangle's — same four nodes, same connecting pattern, just relabelled. — Carter §2.5, pp. 19–20 Two utterly different physical situations — folded paper and household wiring — turn out to be the same mathematical object wearing different clothes. Carter names this shared structure the Klein 4-group. — Carter §2.5, p. 20

The dance-move version from the hook works the same way: a quarter-turn, a half-turn, a three-quarter-turn and standing still, with "turn again" as the only action. That one has a different map (it's a loop of four, not the rectangle's cross-shape) — a preview that not all groups of size 4 look alike, a question aa-25 picks up with Cayley diagrams properly.

Self-check 1. How many distinct configurations does the rectangle puzzle have in total, once you count every combination of flips reachable from the solved state?

Flipping twice in either direction returns you to a state you've already visited (a flip undoes itself), and combining both flips lands you on a fourth, genuinely new configuration. Beyond that, nothing new appears — Carter's exploration closes after exactly four nodes (§2.3, pp. 15–18).

Self-check 2. Why do the rectangle and the two-light-switch puzzle count as "the same group", even though one is paper and the other is electricity?

Carter's point in §2.5 (pp. 19–20) is precisely this: what matters to group theory is the pattern of how actions combine, not the physical material carrying them out. Strip away the rectangle-ness or the switch-ness and the same abstract skeleton — the Klein 4-group — is left underneath.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The rules of the game, discovered informally

Rubik's Cube and the rectangle puzzle look nothing alike, but Carter shows they share four family resemblances — and it is exactly these four resemblances, not cubes or rectangles specifically, that group theory studies. — Carter §§1.2–1.5.1, pp. 4–7

Rule 1.5 — a fixed list of moves. There is a predefined list of actions that never changes. — Carter §1.4, p. 6

For the rectangle: horizontal flip and vertical flip, and nothing else is ever added to the list mid-game.

Rule 1.6 — every move undoes itself (or something does). Every action is reversible. — Carter §1.4, p. 6

Flip the rectangle horizontally, regret it, flip it horizontally again — you're back where you started. Every move on the rectangle happens to undo itself, which is a special (if convenient) case of reversibility.

Rule 1.7 — no randomness. Every action is deterministic. — Carter §1.4, p. 6

A horizontal flip always sends corner 1 to the same place. There is no dice roll involved.

Rule 1.8 — combinations count as moves too. Any sequence of consecutive actions is also an action. — Carter §1.4, p. 6

This is exactly the fact you exercised above: flip-horizontal-then-flip-vertical is not on the original list of two generators, yet it is a perfectly good move of the puzzle — the same move as "rotate 180°."

Carter bundles these four rules into an unofficial working definition: a group is a system of actions satisfying Rules 1.5 through 1.8. — Carter, Definition 1.9, p. 7 Notice this definition is entirely about actions and how they combine — no mention yet of sets, elements, or the word "axiom" in its formal sense. Carter uses "rule" rather than "axiom" deliberately, to keep the discussion informal until later chapters. — Carter §1.4, p. 6

Two rules we haven't named yet — you already found them

Look back at your own play with the rectangle. Every time you performed a flip and then reset with the "identity" button, you were relying on two facts that Rule 1.8 quietly guarantees but doesn't spell out by name:

Discovered fact 1 — a "do-nothing" move exists.

Among the four rectangle configurations, exactly one of them — the solved state — has the property that applying it changes nothing. Carter treats "do nothing" as a legitimate action (it trivially satisfies Rules 1.5–1.8: it's on no list explicitly, but Rule 1.8 lets us treat "flip horizontal, then flip horizontal again" as one combined action, and that combined action changes nothing). This "changes-nothing" action is what later chapters will call the group's identity. It is the mathematical cousin of adding 0, or multiplying by 1.

Discovered fact 2 — every move has an "undo" (your turn to check it).

For each of the rectangle's four configurations, there is another move — possibly the same one — that brings you straight back to solved. Fill in the undo-move for each case:

 Horizontal flip, undone by: ⟨your answer⟩

 Vertical flip, undone by: ⟨your answer⟩

 180° rotation (horizontal-then-vertical), undone by: ⟨your answer: which single combination reverses it?⟩

This "undo move" is what later chapters will call an inverse. Notice that in this particular group every move is its own undo — that will not be true of every group you meet (aa-25's Cayley diagrams will show groups where undoing takes a different move entirely).

You try (solo). The three-wall art-rearranging puzzle: three walls, one picture each, and two allowed actions — swap the left and centre pictures, or swap the centre and right pictures. (a) List all the arrangements reachable from the start. (b) Check Rules 1.5–1.8 against this puzzle. (c) Does "swap left/centre" undo itself? Does combining "swap left/centre" then "swap centre/right" undo itself, or does undoing it require a different combination? — cf. Carter Exercise 1.4, p. 7

Self-check 3. Which of Carter's four rules is being demonstrated when we say "flip-horizontal-then-flip-vertical" counts as a legitimate move of the rectangle puzzle, even though it wasn't on the original list of two generators?

Rule 1.8 says any sequence of consecutive actions is itself an action, even if it never appeared on the original short list of generators (Carter §1.4, p. 6). The generators are a starting point for building the whole group, not a ceiling on it.

Self-check 4. In the rectangle group, what plays the role that "0" plays in ordinary addition?

Just as adding 0 leaves a number unchanged, applying the "do-nothing" action leaves every configuration unchanged. This role — an action that changes nothing — is exactly what aa-23 formalises as the group's identity element.

Guided Proof Practice

Prompt: Segment 1 asserted that the rectangle's exploration terminates after exactly four configurations — that no fifth arrangement is reachable, however long a sequence of flips you perform. Argue this informally, in Carter's pre-axiomatic register, using only what you can check with your hands. Hint: write h for the horizontal flip and v for the vertical one, and find the two facts about sequences of h's and v's that let you shorten any sequence down to a very small list of survivors.

Click to reveal model answer

Model Answer: Write h for the horizontal flip and v for the vertical one. Every configuration you can reach is the result of some finite sequence of h's and v's, because those are the only two moves on the list (Rule 1.5). Two facts about such sequences, both checkable on the paper:

(1) h twice in a row does nothing, and so does v twice in a row. Flip the sheet left-to-right and left-to-right again and every corner is home. So wherever a sequence contains hh or vv, you may delete that pair without changing where you end up.

(2) h then v lands in the same place as v then h. Both routes give the half-turn — the Prediction Gate's answer. So you may swap any adjacent vh into hv.

Now take any sequence at all. Using (2) repeatedly, shuffle every h to the front, leaving a block of h's followed by a block of v's. Using (1) repeatedly, delete pairs from each block until at most one of each survives. What is left is one of exactly four sequences: nothing at all, h, v, or hv. So at most four configurations are reachable.

And all four are genuinely different — the canvas shows four distinct corner arrangements, so none of them is secretly another in disguise. At most four, and no two the same: exactly four. ∎

(Notice what this argument did not need: any definition of "group", any axiom, any set. It needed only the list of moves, and the two observations you made with your hands. That is what it means to find the structure before the definition.)

Exit Ritual: Blank-Page Reconstruction

Before closing this lesson, look away from the screen and reconstruct the three load-bearing claims from nothing:

  1. The rectangle puzzle's exploration closes after exactly four configurations — and the reason it closes (each flip undoes itself; the two flips commute).
  2. Carter's four rules (1.5 fixed list, 1.6 reversible, 1.7 deterministic, 1.8 combinations count), and which one licenses "flip horizontal then flip vertical" as a move in its own right.
  3. The two facts you discovered by hand that the rules do not name: a do-nothing action exists, and every move has an undo. State what later chapters call each.
Check your mental skeleton

If you can say: (1) four — solved, horizontal, vertical, half-turn — because hh and vv delete and hv = vh; (2) Rule 1.8, combinations of actions are actions; (3) do-nothing = the identity, undo = the inverse, and in this particular group every move happens to be its own inverse — then you have the whole of aa-00 without the vocabulary, which is exactly the order Carter intended. The vocabulary arrives in aa-23.
