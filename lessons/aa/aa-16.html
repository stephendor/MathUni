Submodules, direct sums, and quotient modules (aa-16)

In ring theory you cannot divide a ring by a ring. You had to invent ideals first. In module theory that whole detour disappears: every submodule can be quotiented out.

That is the sentence to hold on to, because the quotient it licenses is the one homology is made of. A chain group C carries a submodule of cycles Z and, inside that, a submodule of boundaries B — and the homology group is Z/B, a quotient of a module by a submodule, with no ideal in sight. Today builds that construction in full, together with the two things it needs: what a submodule is, and how modules combine. Along the way the category of modules keeps turning out to be better behaved than the category of rings, and each improvement is worth naming.

Predict before we start. The kernel of a nontrivial ring homomorphism is not a subring. Is the kernel of a module homomorphism a submodule?

There is no identity element for a module to miss: Definition 8.14 has two clauses, not three. So ker f = f−1(0N) is a submodule, immediately, by Proposition 8.32(ii). Aluffi calls this "a point of departure with ring theory" — there the kernel was a rng and needed a whole new notion, the ideal, to be quotiented by. Here it is simply a submodule, and submodules are what you already quotient by. — Aluffi §8.4, Definition 8.36, p. 165

Why this matters for the mission: Z/B is a quotient module. This unit is homology, one abstraction early.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Submodules, and what generates them

Definition 8.22 (submodule). Let M be an R-module and N ⊆ M a subset that is itself an R-module. Then N is a submodule of M if the inclusion i : N → M is an R-module homomorphism — equivalently, if N's addition and R-action are the restrictions of M's. — Aluffi §8.3, Definition 8.22, p. 161
Proposition 8.23. A subset M′ ⊆ M is a submodule if and only if it is nonempty and closed under addition and under multiplication by scalars. — Aluffi §8.3, Proposition 8.23, p. 161
Proof. Axioms (i) and (iv)–(viii) are universally quantified identities holding throughout M, so they hold for elements of M′ automatically — the argument of aa-07's Proposition 4.14. That leaves (ii) and (iii). Since M′ ≠ ∅ there is some m ∈ M′, and then

0 = 0R·m ∈ M′   and   −m = (−1)·m ∈ M′,

both by closure under scalars, using aa-15's Proposition 8.9. ∎ — Aluffi §8.3, p. 161

Two clauses, not four. Compare Proposition 4.14 for subrings, which needed 0 ∈ S, 1 ∈ S, closure, and negatives. Here scalar multiplication produces 0 and −m for free. Compare also aa-09's Proposition 5.9 for ideals, where absorption did the same job — and note that this is not a coincidence: Example 8.24 says an ideal is a submodule of R.

The standard submodules.

8.24. An ideal I ⊆ R is a submodule of R viewed as an R-module. Aluffi suggests adopting this as the definition of left-ideal: the left-ideals of R are exactly its submodules. For commutative R, the ideals of R are precisely its submodules. — Aluffi §8.3, Example 8.24, pp. 161–162

8.25. For m₁, …, mn ∈ M, the set of linear combinations r₁m₁ + ⋯ + rnmn is a submodule — the smallest one containing them all. — Aluffi §8.3, Example 8.25, p. 162

8.26 and 8.27. For submodules N₁, N₂ ⊆ M, the sum N₁ + N₂ = { n₁ + n₂ } is the smallest submodule containing both, and N₁ ∩ N₂ is a submodule — as is any intersection of a family of submodules. — Aluffi §8.3, Examples 8.26 and 8.27, p. 162

Definition 8.28. The submodule of linear combinations of m₁, …, mn is written ⟨m₁, …, mn⟩, the submodule generated by them. — Aluffi §8.3, Definition 8.28, p. 162

Definition 8.29. M is finitely generated if M = ⟨m₁, …, mn⟩ for some finite list, and cyclic if M = ⟨m⟩ for a single m. — Aluffi §8.3, Definition 8.29, p. 162

Examples 8.30 and 8.31 — the dictionary. R/I is a cyclic R-module, generated by the coset 1 + I (aa-15's Example 8.7); Aluffi promises the converse, that every cyclic module is of this form. And for commutative R, an ideal is cyclic as a module exactly when it is principal as an ideal (Definition 5.17). — Aluffi §8.3, Examples 8.30 and 8.31, p. 162

So "principal ideal" and "cyclic module" are the same word twice, and aa-12's whole theory of PIDs is a statement about which submodules of R are cyclic.

Proposition 8.32. Let f : M → N be an R-module homomorphism. (i) If M′ ⊆ M is a submodule then f(M′) is a submodule of N. (ii) If N′ ⊆ N is a submodule then f−1(N′) is a submodule of M. — Aluffi §8.3, Proposition 8.32, pp. 162–163
Proof of (i). By Proposition 8.23 only closure needs checking. If n₁ = f(m₁) and n₂ = f(m₂) with mi ∈ M′, then n₁ + n₂ = f(m₁ + m₂) ∈ f(M′); and r·n = r·f(m) = f(r·m) ∈ f(M′). ∎ Part (ii) is Aluffi's Exercise 8.12 and is your you-try. — Aluffi §8.3, p. 163

In particular im f = f(M) is a submodule of N — which is the first ingredient of the canonical decomposition, exactly as aa-09's Proposition 5.1 was for rings.

Self-check 1. Proposition 8.23 asks for nonempty, closed under +, closed under scalars. Why is "contains 0" not on the list?

Nonemptiness gives some m ∈ M′, and closure under scalars then puts 0R·m = 0 into M′ by Proposition 8.9 — and likewise (−1)·m = −m. The scalar action is strong enough to generate both existential axioms, so the criterion is two clauses shorter than the subring criterion of Proposition 4.14. And the empty set is certainly a subset containing no 0, which is why nonemptiness must be stated. — Aluffi §8.3, Proposition 8.23, p. 161

Direct sums, and why the notation is ⊕ rather than ×

Definition 8.33 (direct sum). For R-modules M, N, the direct sum M ⊕ N is the set M × N with componentwise operations:

(m₁, n₁) + (m₂, n₂) := (m₁ + m₂, n₁ + n₂),    r·(m, n) := (rm, rn).

It extends in the obvious way to M₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Mn. — Aluffi §8.3, Definition 8.33, p. 163
Example 8.34 — this is n-dimensional space. Over a field k, the vector space of n-tuples with componentwise operations is k ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ k, written k⊕n or kn. So ℝ² is the plane and ℝ³ is space, in this notation.

For a general ring R, the module R⊕n is the free R-module of rank n — aa-18's Definition 9.1. And here is the fork that the rest of the chapter turns on: every module over a field is free, but this fails over other rings, even friendly ones. ℤ/2ℤ is not free as a ℤ-module. — Aluffi §8.3, Example 8.34, p. 163

The projections, and — new — the injections. As with products of rings there are surjective homomorphisms πM(m, n) = m and πN(m, n) = n. But there are also injective homomorphisms — Aluffi §8.3, p. 164

ιM : M → M ⊕ N, ιM(m) = (m, 0),    ιN(n) = (0, n).

Why that is a real difference. For rings, the analogous maps fail: aa-07's ι(a) = (a, 0) does not send 1 to 1, and its image is Example 4.10's non-subring. Here there is no 1 to preserve, so ιM is a perfectly good injective module homomorphism and M is a submodule of M ⊕ N — an identification simply unavailable at the level of rings.

Taken with these injections, M ⊕ N is a coproduct as well as a product, which is why Aluffi writes ⊕ rather than ×. Filed for later, with the summary he gives: the category of R-modules is considerably friendlier than the category of rings, and the properties being noticed are those of an abelian category. — Aluffi §8.3, p. 164

Example 8.35. Given ring homomorphisms f : R → S and g : R → T, the map h(r) = (f(r), g(r)) is a ring homomorphism R → S × T, and the R-module structure this induces on S × T agrees with S ⊕ T. — Aluffi §8.4, Example 8.35, p. 164

Self-check 2. Which maps exist for modules but not for rings?

Projections are homomorphisms in both settings — aa-07 proved it for rings. The injections are not: a ↦ (a, 0) sends 1R to (1, 0) ≠ (1, 1), so it fails the third clause of Definition 4.15. Modules have no third clause, so the injections survive, and M genuinely sits inside M ⊕ N as a submodule. That is what the symbol ⊕ is recording. — Aluffi §8.3, p. 164; §4.2, Example 4.10, p. 62

Self-check 3. Is ℤ/2ℤ a free ℤ-module — that is, isomorphic to some ℤ⊕n?

ℤ⊕n is infinite for n ≥ 1 and trivial for n = 0, while ℤ/2ℤ has two elements — so it matches none of them. Over a field every module is free, which is why linear algebra is comparatively easy; over ℤ it is not, and among finitely generated ℤ-modules the ones that are not free are exactly where torsion lives. The finite-generation hypothesis is doing real work: ℚ is torsion-free as a ℤ-module and still not free, since ℚ is divisible and no nonzero free abelian group is. Finitely generated is the setting of aa-20's structure theorem, and the equivalence is a theorem about that setting, not about ℤ-modules at large. aa-20's structure theorem is the statement of how much of a finitely generated module is free and what the rest looks like. — Aluffi §8.3, Example 8.34, p. 163

Quotient modules, and the canonical decomposition

Definition 8.36 (kernel). For an R-module homomorphism f : M → N,

ker f = f−1(0N) = { m ∈ M : f(m) = 0 },

which is a submodule of M — since {0N} is a submodule of N and Proposition 8.32(ii) applies. — Aluffi §8.4, Definition 8.36, p. 165

Aluffi flags this inside the definition, because it is the departure point from ring theory: the kernel of a ring homomorphism is not a ring, and rescuing the situation required inventing ideals. Here the kernel is a module of the same kind as everything else. — Aluffi §8.4, p. 165

As for rings, f(m₁) = f(m₂) ⟺ f(m₁ − m₂) = 0 ⟺ m₂ − m₁ ∈ ker f. So for any submodule K ⊆ M define — Aluffi §8.4, pp. 165–166

m₁ ∼K m₂ ⟺ m₂ − m₁ ∈ K,

an equivalence relation (the proof is aa-09's Proposition 5.20 verbatim, using only that K is closed under + and negatives).

Definition 8.37. The class of m is the coset m + K; the set of cosets is M/K, the quotient of M modulo K. Thus m + K = m′ + K ⟺ m′ − m ∈ K. — Aluffi §8.4, Definition 8.37, p. 166

Operations:   (m₁ + K) + (m₂ + K) := (m₁ + m₂) + K   and   r·(m + K) := (r·m) + K. — Aluffi §8.4, p. 166

Claim 8.38. These operations are well defined. — Aluffi §8.4, Claim 8.38, p. 166

Lemma 8.39. If m′₁ − m₁ ∈ K and m′₂ − m₂ ∈ K then (m′₁ + m′₂) − (m₁ + m₂) ∈ K; and if r ∈ R and m′ − m ∈ K then r·m′ − r·m ∈ K. — Aluffi §8.4, Lemma 8.39, p. 166

Proof of Lemma 8.39 — and the comparison that matters. Both statements are immediate: K is closed under addition, giving the first, and closed under multiplication by scalars, giving r·m′ − r·m = r·(m′ − m) ∈ K.

∎ Now set this beside aa-09's Lemma 5.25, the corresponding step for rings. There, the product a′b′ − ab expanded into aj + ib + ij, with a and b arbitrary elements of R, and only the absorption property could return those terms to the ideal. That is why a ring cannot be quotiented by a subring, and why ideals had to be defined.

Here, the scalar r and the element m′ − m already live on opposite sides of the module structure, and closure under scalars — which every submodule has by definition — is exactly the right strength. Nothing extra is needed. That single difference is why "quotient by any submodule" is legal and "quotient by any subring" is not. — Aluffi §8.4, p. 166; §5.3, Lemma 5.25, p. 85

Theorem 8.40. For a submodule K of an R-module M, (M/K, +, ·) is an R-module with 0M/K = 0 + K = K. — Aluffi §8.4, Theorem 8.40, p. 167

Proposition 8.41. The natural projection π : M → M/K, π(m) = m + K, is a surjective R-module homomorphism with ker π = K. — Aluffi §8.4, Proposition 8.41, p. 167

Remark 8.42. Hence every submodule is the kernel of some homomorphism — the module version of aa-10's Remark 5.36. — Aluffi §8.4, Remark 8.42, p. 167

Aluffi's own comment on Theorem 8.40 is the one to remember: in ring theory there is no such thing as the quotient of a ring by a ring, so quotients had to be taken by a different species of object. With modules there is "no such silliness" — you may quotient a module by any submodule, inventing nothing. — Aluffi §8.4, p. 167

Below is the construction in the smallest interesting case: M = ℤ ⊕ ℤ, and K the submodule generated by one or two elements. The lattice is M; the highlighted sublattice is K; the colours are the cosets, and the number of colours is the size of M/K.

Theorem 8.43 (Canonical decomposition). For every R-module homomorphism f : M → N there is a commutative diagram

M  —π→  M/ker f  —f̃→  im f  —ι→  N,

with π surjective, ι injective, and f̃ an isomorphism. — Aluffi §8.4, Theorem 8.43, p. 167

Every piece is now in place: im f is a submodule by Proposition 8.32, ker f is a submodule by Definition 8.36, M/ker f is a module by Theorem 8.40, and the relation "f cannot distinguish them" is exactly ∼ker f. The First Isomorphism Theorem for modules follows as it did for rings: for surjective f, N ≅ M/ker f. — Aluffi §8.5, Theorem 8.44, p. 168

Faded example — every cyclic module is an R/I. Let M = ⟨m⟩ be cyclic, and suppose R is commutative.

By aa-15's Example 8.18, the rule f(r) = r·m defines an R-module homomorphism f : R → M, and no other data is needed because a homomorphism out of R is determined by ⟨your step: what?⟩.

f is surjective, because M being generated by m means every element of M has the form ⟨your step⟩ for some r ∈ R.

So by the First Isomorphism Theorem, M ≅ R/ker f. And ker f is a submodule of R viewed as an R-module — which, by Example 8.24, is the same thing as ⟨your step: what kind of subset of R?⟩.

Conclusion: every cyclic R-module is isomorphic to R/L for a left ideal L. When R is commutative the left ideals are simply the ideals, and this is the converse Aluffi promised at Example 8.30 — Aluffi §8.3, Example 8.30, p. 162 — which is why his own statement of it, Example 8.45, opens by assuming R commutative. — Aluffi §8.5, Example 8.45, p. 168 In the case R = ℤ this says every cyclic abelian group is ℤ/nℤ for some n ≥ 0 — with n = 0 giving ℤ itself.

Do not drop that word, because for noncommutative R the two-sided version is false. In R = M₂(k) the matrices whose second column is zero form a left ideal L — multiplying on the left cannot disturb a zero column — which is proper and nonzero, so R/L is a cyclic left module of dimension 2 over k. But M₂(k) is simple as a ring: its only two-sided ideals are 0 and R, whose quotients have dimension 0 and 4. So R/L is a cyclic module that is R/L for no two-sided ideal at all. This is the same distinction Example 8.24 drew above, and it is the reason it was drawn.

Guided proof (write it yourself)

The heart of Theorem 8.43 is the middle map. Let f : M → N be an R-module homomorphism and define f̃ : M/ker f → im f by f̃(m + ker f) := f(m). Prove that f̃ is well defined, is an R-module homomorphism, and is bijective. Then say which step would break if ker f were replaced by an arbitrary submodule K ⊆ M.

Reveal model answer

Well defined. If m + ker f = m′ + ker f then m′ − m ∈ ker f, so f(m′) − f(m) = f(m′ − m) = 0, i.e. f(m′) = f(m). The value does not depend on the representative.

Homomorphism. f̃((m₁ + K) + (m₂ + K)) = f̃((m₁ + m₂) + K) = f(m₁ + m₂) = f(m₁) + f(m₂) = f̃(m₁ + K) + f̃(m₂ + K), writing K = ker f; and f̃(r·(m + K)) = f̃(r·m + K) = f(r·m) = r·f(m) = r·f̃(m + K). Both use Definition 8.37 for the operations on the quotient, then Definition 8.14 for f.

Surjective onto im f. Any element of im f is f(m) = f̃(m + ker f).

Injective. If f̃(m + ker f) = f̃(m′ + ker f) then f(m) = f(m′), so f(m′ − m) = 0, so m′ − m ∈ ker f, so m + ker f = m′ + ker f. ∎

What breaks for a general K. Only well-definedness — and only if K ⊄ ker f. If K ⊆ ker f the same computation still gives a well-defined homomorphism M/K → N (the universal property of quotients, aa-10's Proposition 5.39 transplanted), but it need no longer be injective: injectivity used both inclusions, and it holds exactly when K = ker f.

Grade yourself on that last point. Injectivity is the only step that consumes ker f ⊆ K rather than K ⊆ ker f, and it is what turns a homomorphism into an isomorphism.

Self-check 4. Why can a module be quotiented by any submodule, when a ring cannot be quotiented by any subring?

Lemma 8.39's scalar clause is r·m′ − r·m = r·(m′ − m), and closure under scalars — part of being a submodule — settles it. In aa-09's Lemma 5.25 the corresponding expansion produced aj and ib with a, b arbitrary in R, and only absorption could return them to the ideal. Modules do have a multiplication of sorts, the scalar action; the point is that every submodule is already closed under it, whereas not every subring absorbs. — Aluffi §8.4, Lemma 8.39, p. 166

Self-check 5. In M/K, what is the zero element, and what is ker(π : M → M/K)?

0M/K = 0 + K, and as a subset of M that coset is K itself (Theorem 8.40). Then π(m) = 0 + K exactly when m ∈ K, so ker π = K (Proposition 8.41). Together with Remark 8.42 this says every submodule is a kernel — the module twin of aa-10's Remark 5.36, where the same statement about ideals was called striking because kernel and ideal looked nothing alike. Here it is barely a surprise, because both sides were submodules to begin with. — Aluffi §8.4, Proposition 8.41 and Remark 8.42, p. 167

Then check yourself
  1. Submodules are cheap to recognise (Proposition 8.23): nonempty, closed under + and under scalars — two clauses, because 0 = 0·m and −m = (−1)·m come free. Left ideals of R are exactly the submodules of R (Example 8.24) — the same thing as ideals when R is commutative, and not otherwise — so over a commutative R "principal ideal" and "cyclic module" are the same condition, and images and preimages of submodules are submodules (Proposition 8.32).
  2. M ⊕ N has projections and injections (Definition 8.33). The injections m ↦ (m, 0) are homomorphisms here and are not for rings, because there is no 1 to preserve — so M is a submodule of M ⊕ N. R⊕n is the free module of rank n; every module over a field is free, and ℤ/2ℤ shows that this fails over ℤ.
  3. M/K is a module for every submodule K (Theorem 8.40), the projection is surjective with kernel K (Proposition 8.41), and every homomorphism factors as surjection–isomorphism–injection (Theorem 8.43). The reason no ideal-like notion is needed is one line of Lemma 8.39: r·(m′ − m) stays in K by closure under scalars, where the ring version needed absorption.
And the sentence the mission strip is pointing at: a homology group is Z/B, a quotient of a module of cycles by a submodule of boundaries. Everything the construction requires was assembled today — and the fact that it requires no extra hypothesis on B is the content of Theorem 8.40.
