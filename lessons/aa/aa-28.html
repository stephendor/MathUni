Lagrange's theorem and the class equation (aa-28)

Count a group by tiling it with copies of a subgroup — and counting suddenly proves structure.

Everything so far has been structural: subgroups, homomorphisms, quotients, isomorphisms. This unit is arithmetic, and it is startling how much structure falls out of pure counting. Aluffi says he could have introduced Lagrange's theorem much earlier, while discussing cosets of ideals or of subgroups in abelian groups, and waited only because its applications shine in the theory of finite groups. By the end of this unit you will prove that every group of order p² is commutative, using nothing but division.

Predict before we start. aa-26 showed the left cosets of a subgroup H partition G. If G is finite, what do you expect about the sizes of those cosets?

All equal, and the proof is a single cancellation: h ↦ gh is a bijection from H onto gH. That one line is the whole of Lagrange's theorem, since G is then tiled by equal-sized disjoint blocks. Note that H is itself one of the cosets, namely eH, so it is not distinguished by size in any way. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Lemma 12.2, p. 267

Why this matters for the mission: Counting arguments recur in covering-space degrees and orbit decompositions.

25 min Segment 1 — Tiling a group with cosets

Definition 12.1. The index of a subgroup H in G, written [G : H], is the number of left cosets of H in G, when that number is finite. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Definition 12.1, p. 267

It is also the number of right cosets, which Aluffi leaves as Exercise 12.1. Note the care in the phrasing: G/H is the set of left cosets whether or not H is normal — aa-26's Definition 11.50 — and the index is its size. When H happens to be normal, G/H is a group and [G : H] is its order, but the number is meaningful in any case. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 267

Lemma 12.2. For a subgroup H of G and any g in G, the map H → gH given by h ↦ gh is a bijection. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Lemma 12.2, p. 267

Proof. Surjective by the definition of gH; injective by cancellation, since gh₁ = gh₂ gives h₁ = g⁻¹gh₁ = g⁻¹gh₂ = h₂. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 267

Theorem 12.3 (Lagrange's theorem). For a finite group G and a subgroup H, |H| divides |G|; in fact |G| = [G : H] · |H|. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Theorem 12.3, p. 267

Proof. G is the union of [G : H] disjoint cosets, each with |H| elements by Lemma 12.2. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268

Three retrospective payments come due immediately. First, aa-26's Example 11.59 — index two implies normal — becomes almost visual: |K| is half of |G|, so G minus K is the other left coset and the other right coset, the two families coincide, and K is normal. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268 Second, the rotation subgroup of D2n is normal for free, being half the size. Third, x and y generate D2n: a subgroup containing both properly contains the rotations, so its index is strictly between 1 and 2, leaving only index 1. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268

And |An| = n!/2, since [Sn : An] = 2 from aa-27's Example 11.69. Aluffi notes this is otherwise clear — multiply all even permutations by a fixed transposition to get all odd ones — but the point of the theorem is that "having Lagrange's theorem, we are dispensed from the need to come up with such ad hoc arguments". — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268

Proposition 12.4. For a finite group G and g in G, the order |g| divides |G|; equivalently g|G| = e. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Proposition 12.4, p. 268

Proof. |g| is the order of the subgroup ⟨g⟩, so Theorem 12.3 applies. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268

Worked, then faded — Fermat's little theorem falls out. aa-04 proved that if p is a positive prime and p does not divide a, then ap−1 ≡ 1 mod p, and the proof took some cleverness. Aluffi's remark is that after Proposition 12.4 it "should appear evident to you".

Worked: [a]p is an element of the group (Z/pZ)*, whose order is p − 1. By Proposition 12.4, raising any element of a group to the order of the group gives the identity. So [a]pp−1 = [1]p. That is the theorem. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, p. 268

Now fade it and derive Euler's generalisation, which Aluffi sets as Exercise 12.2: for n > 0 and a coprime to n, what is aφ(n) mod n? The relevant group is ⟨?⟩; its order is ⟨?⟩; Proposition 12.4 then gives ⟨?⟩.

CheckThe group is (Z/nZ)*, the units of Z/nZ, and [a]n lies in it precisely because a is coprime to n. Its order is φ(n) by definition of the Euler function — that was aa-04's count of the residues coprime to n. Proposition 12.4 says every element raised to the order of the group is the identity, so [a]nφ(n) = [1]n, that is aφ(n) ≡ 1 mod n. Fermat's is the case n = p, where φ(p) = p − 1.

Nothing here is about number theory. The same one-line argument applies verbatim to any finite group whatever, which is exactly Aluffi's point about ad hoc arguments.

Self-check 1. A group G has order 15. What orders can its elements have?

By Proposition 12.4 the order of an element divides the order of the group, and the divisors of 15 are 1, 3, 5, 15. Order 1 belongs to the identity alone. This is a genuine restriction obtained without knowing anything about G beyond its size — no multiplication table, no generators. — Aluffi Underground §12.1, Proposition 12.4, p. 268

25 min Segment 2 — Orbits are indices

One more consequence, then a warning. Proposition 12.5: if |G| = p is prime then G is cyclic, G ≅ Cp. Take any g ≠ e; then |g| > 1 and |g| divides p, so |g| = p and ⟨g⟩ = G. — Aluffi Underground, Proposition 12.5, p. 269 There is exactly one group of each prime order, and you never have to look at it.

Now the warning, because the natural converse is false. Lagrange says a subgroup of order m forces m to divide |G|. Does every divisor of |G| occur as the order of a subgroup? For commutative G, yes. For general G, no.

Example 12.6 — the missing subgroup of A4. |A4| = 12, so by Lagrange a subgroup has order 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or 12. Suppose there were one of order 6. It would have index 2, hence be normal, hence — by aa-26's Proposition 11.57 — be a union of conjugacy classes. But the conjugacy classes of A4 have sizes 1, 3, 4, 4, and no sub-collection of those including the identity's class sums to 6. Contradiction: A4 has no subgroup of order 6. — Aluffi Underground, Example 12.6, p. 269

Check the arithmetic yourself: from {1, 3, 4, 4} the achievable sums containing the 1 are 1, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12. Six is not among them. Every ingredient — index two implies normal, normal implies a union of classes — has been proved in an earlier unit; only the arithmetic is new.

The index is otherwise very well behaved. Proposition 12.7: for subgroups H inside K inside G, [G : H] = [G : K][K : H]. K is a disjoint union of cosets kiH, and then gK is the disjoint union of the gkiH, so each coset of K splits into [K : H] cosets of H. Aluffi remarks that this is transparent when H and K are normal, via the Third Isomorphism Theorem and Lagrange — but that the hypothesis is not needed. — Aluffi Underground, Proposition 12.7, p. 269

The rest of the unit comes from marrying Lagrange to aa-24's actions. Recall an action of G on A and the orbit of a point.

Definition 12.8. The stabilizer of a in A is StabG(a) = {g in G : ga = a}, a subgroup of G. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Definition 12.8, p. 270

It is a subgroup by the criterion of aa-23: e fixes a, and if g and h both fix a then a = h⁻¹a and so gh⁻¹a = ga = a. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 270

Lemma 12.9. There is a bijection between the orbit of a and the set G/StabG(a) of left cosets of the stabilizer. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Lemma 12.9, p. 270

The map is α(g·StabG(a)) := ga, and the proof is one chain of equivalences used three times over. Well defined: if g₁Stab = g₂Stab then g₁⁻¹g₂ stabilises a, so g₁a = g₂a. Surjective: every b in the orbit is ga for some g. Injective: g₁a = g₂a gives g₁⁻¹g₂a = a, so g₁⁻¹g₂ is in the stabilizer and the cosets agree. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 270 Note that "well defined" and "injective" are the two directions of the same implication — which is why the lemma is really a single observation, not three.

Theorem 12.10 (Orbit–stabilizer). For a finite group G acting on A, |Oa| = [G : StabG(a)]. In particular every orbit size divides |G|. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Theorem 12.10, p. 270

That last sentence is the working tool. Two of Aluffi's illustrations: an action of C35 on a four-element set must fix every point, since the possible orbit sizes are 1, 5, 7, 35 and there is no room for anything but 1. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Example 12.12, p. 271 And every action of D8 on a set of odd size has a fixed point, since the nontrivial orbits all have even size. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Example 12.13, p. 271

Definition 12.14. For a positive prime p, a p-group is a group with |G| = pr for some r ≥ 0. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Definition 12.14, p. 271

Corollary 12.15. If a p-group G acts on a finite set A with fixed-point set F, then |F| ≡ |A| mod p. In particular, if p does not divide |A| then the action has fixed points. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Corollary 12.15, p. 271

Proof. A minus F is a union of nontrivial orbits, each of size a power of p by Theorem 12.10, so |A \ F| is a multiple of p. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 271

Self-check 2. Why does A4 have no subgroup of order 6?

6 divides 12 perfectly well; that is exactly why the example is interesting — Lagrange permits the subgroup and it still does not exist, so the converse of Lagrange is false. And A4 is not simple: it contains the normal Klein four-group V of aa-26's Example 11.63. The argument chains index two to normality to Proposition 11.57 and finishes with arithmetic. — Aluffi Underground, Example 12.6, p. 269

Self-check 3. Orbit–stabilizer says |Oa| = [G : StabG(a)]. What does that immediately give you about orbit sizes?

An index divides the order by Lagrange, so orbit sizes are constrained before you know anything about the action. Orbits need not have equal size — the C₂-flip on a triangle in aa-24 has orbits of sizes 1 and 2 — and the number of orbits is not constrained at all, as the trivial action on a large set shows. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Theorem 12.10, p. 270

25 min Segment 3 — The class equation

Aluffi's second reason Theorem 12.10 is powerful: some objects inside G are themselves orbits of an action of G. Conjugation is the example. Apply orbit–stabilizer to it and a group starts counting itself.

Corollary 12.17. For a finite group G and a in G, the number of distinct conjugates gag⁻¹ of a divides |G|. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Corollary 12.17, p. 272

Proof. The conjugates of a form its orbit under the conjugation action of aa-24's Definition 11.31. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 272

Example 12.18. The conjugacy classes of A4 have sizes 1, 3, 4, 4 — all divisors of 12, as Corollary 12.17 mandates. Aluffi notes that knowing in advance that 8 is impossible, since 8 does not divide 12, would have streamlined the exercise that produced those numbers. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Example 12.18, p. 272

Definition 12.19. The centralizer of a is ZG(a) = {g : gag⁻¹ = a}, its stabilizer under conjugation — that is, the elements commuting with a. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Definition 12.19, p. 272

Definition 12.20. The centre Z(G) = {g : ag = ga for all a} is the intersection of all the centralizers. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Definition 12.20, p. 272

Aluffi gives the slogan to memorise: the number of conjugates of a equals the index of its centralizer. And G is commutative exactly when G = Z(G); in general Z(G) is the union of the trivial orbits of conjugation. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 272 Those two sentences are the class equation in prose.

Theorem 12.21 (Class equation). For a finite group G,

|G| = |Z(G)| + Σ [G : ZG(a)],

the sum running over a set of distinct representatives for the nontrivial conjugacy classes. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Theorem 12.21, p. 272

Proof. G is the union of its conjugation orbits. The trivial ones are collected in Z(G); the nontrivial ones are the nontrivial conjugacy classes, and each has size the index of its centralizer. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 273

Below, the equation drawn. Each conjugacy class is a coloured block; the centre is the run of singletons at the left. Every class size and the total are counted from the group.

You try. Read each group's class equation off the picture, then say what it tells you about the centre. One of the four has a nontrivial centre; predict which before looking.
The four equations
  • S₃: 6 = 1 + 2 + 3. Trivial centre, and the class sizes 2 and 3 both divide 6.
  • D₈: 8 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 2. Centre of order 2 — the half-turn — which is exactly what Corollary 12.22 predicts for a 2-group.
  • A₄: 12 = 1 + 3 + 4 + 4. Trivial centre; this is the arithmetic that killed the order-6 subgroup in Example 12.6.
  • S₄: 24 = 1 + 3 + 6 + 6 + 8. Trivial centre, and every summand divides 24 as Corollary 12.17 requires.
D₈ is the one with a nontrivial centre, and it is the only p-group among the four. That is not a coincidence — it is the next corollary.

Corollary 12.22. A nontrivial p-group has nontrivial centre. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Corollary 12.22, p. 273

Proof. Every term [G : ZG(a)] in the class equation is a multiple of p, so pr = |Z(G)| + a multiple of p with r ≥ 1. Since e is in Z(G), the centre is nonempty, so |Z(G)| is a nonzero multiple of p and in particular exceeds 1. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 273

Aluffi supplies a second route: G acts on itself by conjugation, Z(G) is the fixed-point set, and Corollary 12.15 gives the congruence directly. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 273 Same fact, reached from the fixed-point corollary instead of the class equation.

Guided proof. Prove Corollary 12.23: a group of order p² is commutative. The skeleton is fixed; you supply the elimination.

By Corollary 12.22 the centre is nontrivial, so by Lagrange |Z(G)| is p or p². If it is p², then Z(G) = G and G is commutative, done. The work is ruling out |Z(G)| = p. Suppose it were, pick a not in Z(G), and consider ZG(a).

Model answer

ZG(a) contains Z(G), since elements of the centre commute with everything including a; and it contains a, since a commutes with itself. Because a is not in Z(G), the containment Z(G) ⊆ ZG(a) is proper, so |ZG(a)| > p.

By Lagrange, |ZG(a)| divides p², and the divisors are 1, p, p². Being greater than p leaves only p², so ZG(a) = G. But that says every element of G commutes with a, which is precisely the statement that a is in Z(G) — contradicting the choice of a. So |Z(G)| = p is impossible, and |Z(G)| = p², giving G commutative. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Corollary 12.23, p. 273

Aluffi adds that the bound is sharp: D8 is a noncommutative group of order 8 = 2³, so the same conclusion fails one power higher. And since a group of order p² is now known to be abelian, aa-22's classification theorem applies to it: Theorem 10.18 expresses every finite abelian group as a product of cyclic groups Z/d₁Z × ⋯ × Z/dsZ with each di dividing the next. — Aluffi Underground §10.3, Theorem 10.18, p. 212 For order p² the only such lists are (p²) and (p, p), so G is Cp² or Cp × Cp — the whole classification of groups of that order, obtained by counting. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, p. 273

Self-check 4. In the class equation, why is the centre written as a separate term instead of appearing in the sum?

z lies in Z(G) exactly when gzg⁻¹ = z for every g, that is, exactly when its conjugacy class is {z}. Collecting all those singletons gives |Z(G)|, and what remains are the classes of size greater than 1. The centre certainly is a union of conjugacy classes — that is why it is normal, by Proposition 11.57 — and every conjugacy class is stable under conjugation, so the first option distinguishes nothing. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Theorem 12.21, p. 272

Self-check 5. Corollary 12.22 says a nontrivial p-group has nontrivial centre. Which step of the class equation forces it?

Each nontrivial class has size an index dividing pr and greater than 1, hence a positive power of p. Reducing the class equation mod p leaves |Z(G)| ≡ 0, and since the identity is always central, |Z(G)| is a positive multiple of p. p-groups certainly have nontrivial classes — D₈ has three of size 2 — and they are not generally commutative, D₈ again. Order p² is the largest case where commutativity is forced, by Corollary 12.23. — Aluffi Underground §12.2, Corollary 12.22, p. 273

Then check yourself
  1. One bijection. h ↦ gh is a bijection H → gH (Lemma 12.2), so all cosets have |H| elements and |G| = [G : H]·|H| (Theorem 12.3), where the index is the number of cosets (Definition 12.1). Consequences: element orders divide |G| (Proposition 12.4), so Fermat's little theorem is a special case; groups of prime order are cyclic (Proposition 12.5); indices multiply along chains (Proposition 12.7).
  2. The converse fails. A4 has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6, because such a subgroup would be normal of index 2, hence a union of conjugacy classes, and 1, 3, 4, 4 has no sub-sum of 6 (Example 12.6).
  3. Orbits are indices. The stabilizer is a subgroup (Definition 12.8); cosets of the stabilizer biject with the orbit (Lemma 12.9); so orbit size equals index and divides |G| (Theorem 12.10). Hence C35 acting on four points fixes all of them (Example 12.12), D8 acting on an odd set has a fixed point (Example 12.13), and for a p-group (Definition 12.14) the fixed points satisfy |F| ≡ |A| mod p (Corollary 12.15).
  4. The group counts itself. Conjugacy classes are orbits, so their sizes divide |G| (Corollary 12.17, checked against A4's 1, 3, 4, 4 in Example 12.18) and equal indices of centralizers (Definition 12.19). Collecting the singleton classes into the centre (Definition 12.20) gives the class equation (Theorem 12.21). Hence p-groups have nontrivial centre (Corollary 12.22) and groups of order p² are commutative (Corollary 12.23) — sharp, since D₈ has order 2³ and is not.
The mission strip is item 3 in topological dress. For a covering space, the number of sheets over a point is the index of a subgroup of π₁ — the same [G : H] counted here, with the fibre in the role of the orbit and the deck group acting. Orbit–stabilizer is what makes "degree" a well-defined number, and the class equation's habit of turning a partition into an arithmetic identity is the same habit that turns a cell decomposition into an Euler characteristic.
