Free modules; modules from matrices (aa-18)

A finitely presented module is a matrix. Not "is described by" — the matrix is the module, up to isomorphism, and every question you can ask about the module becomes a row-and-column operation.

That is the claim this lesson establishes, and it is why a computer can compute homology at all. The route runs: a free module has a basis; a homomorphism out of a free module is an arbitrary choice of images; a homomorphism between free modules is therefore a matrix; and a module presented by generators and relations is the cokernel of such a matrix. Elementary row and column operations change the matrix and not the cokernel — so simplifying the matrix computes the module. Reducing a boundary matrix is exactly this.

Predict before we start. M is an abelian group and f : ℤ/2ℤ → M is a homomorphism. Can f(1) be any element of M you like?

f is determined by f(1), but not freely: 2·f(1) = f(2·1) = f(0) = 0, so the image must be killed by 2. Any such element works, and there may be many — in ℤ/4ℤ both 0 and 2 qualify — but the choice is constrained. That constraint is exactly what "not free" means. Over a free module the images of the basis may be chosen with no constraint at all (Proposition 9.8), and Aluffi calls that "a huge simplification". — Aluffi §9.1, Example 9.10, pp. 176–177

Why this matters for the mission: Boundary matrices present the chain modules; reducing them is persistence computation.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Free modules, bases, and maps out of them

Definition 9.1. An R-module F is free of rank m if F ≅ R⊕m. — Aluffi §9.1, Definition 9.1, p. 174

Remark 9.2. Infinite rank is a legitimate notion — ℤ[x] is a free ℤ-module of countably infinite rank — but everything here is finite rank. — Aluffi §9.1, Remark 9.2, p. 174

Elements of R⊕m are m-tuples. Write ei for the tuple with 1 in position i and 0 elsewhere. Then every r = (r₁, …, rm) is r₁e₁ + ⋯ + rmem, so the ei generate. — Aluffi §9.1, p. 175

Definition 9.3. Elements x₁, …, xm of an R-module are linearly independent if r₁x₁ + ⋯ + rmxm = 0 forces r₁ = ⋯ = rm = 0. — Aluffi §9.1, Definition 9.3, p. 175

Definition 9.5. A tuple (x₁, …, xm) is a basis if it is a linearly independent generating set. — Aluffi §9.1, Definition 9.5, p. 175

The ei are independent, since r₁e₁ + ⋯ + rmem = 0 says (r₁, …, rm) = (0, …, 0) — so they are a basis. Remark 9.4 notes that "linearly independent set" is a slight abuse: a genuine set forbids repetitions, and x₁ = x₂ would give the nontrivial relation x₁ − x₂ = 0. — Aluffi §9.1, Remark 9.4, p. 175

Proposition 9.6. F is free of rank m if and only if it has a basis (x₁, …, xm). — Aluffi §9.1, Proposition 9.6, p. 175
Proof. One direction is the paragraph above. Conversely, given a basis, define φ : R⊕m → F by φ(r₁, …, rm) = r₁x₁ + ⋯ + rmxm. It is surjective because the xi generate (aa-17's Example 8.46), and injective because they are independent — φ(r) = 0 is exactly the relation r₁x₁ + ⋯ + rmxm = 0, which forces every ri = 0, so ker φ = {0}. Hence φ is an isomorphism. ∎ — Aluffi §9.1, p. 175

Read the two halves of "basis". Generating gives surjectivity; independence gives injectivity. That is not a coincidence of notation — it is the reason both conditions appear in Definition 9.5.

Example 9.7 — most modules are not free. ℤ/2ℤ is not free as a ℤ-module: a free ℤ-module of rank ≥ 1 is infinite, and rank 0 is trivial. Its single nonzero element x = [1]₂ generates it but is not linearly independent over ℤ, because 2x = 0 while 2 ≠ 0 in ℤ.

But ℤ/2ℤ is free as a module over itself — it is a field, hence a vector space over itself, with basis (x). Free is a property of the pair (module, ring), not of the set. — Aluffi §9.1, Example 9.7, p. 176

Proposition 9.8. Every R-module homomorphism f : R⊕m → M has the form

f(r₁, …, rm) = r₁a₁ + ⋯ + rmam,   where ai = f(ei);

and conversely every choice of a₁, …, am ∈ M determines such a homomorphism. — Aluffi §9.1, Proposition 9.8, p. 176

Proof: R-linearity gives f(r) = f(Σ riei) = Σ rif(ei); and the formula defines a homomorphism for any choice of the ai. This generalises aa-15's Example 8.18, which was the case m = 1. Aluffi's Remark 9.9 notes this is another universal property — in the professional phrasing, that "the free functor is left-adjoint to the forgetful functor" — mentioned, he says, purely to amuse. — Aluffi §9.1, Remark 9.9, p. 176

The content is the word "every". For a free module the images of the basis may be assigned with no relations to satisfy. Example 9.10 is the contrast: a homomorphism out of ℤ/2ℤ is determined by f(1), but f(1) must satisfy 2·f(1) = 0. That is the prediction gate, and it is what non-freeness costs. — Aluffi §9.1, Example 9.10 and the following remark, pp. 176–177

Self-check 1. Which of these is a basis of ℤ⊕2 as a ℤ-module?

(1,0) and (1,1) generate — (0,1) = (1,1) − (1,0) — and are independent, since a(1,0) + b(1,1) = (a+b, b) = 0 forces b = 0 then a = 0. The first list generates but is dependent: (1,1) − (1,0) − (0,1) = 0 is a nontrivial relation. The third is independent but does not generate: (1,0) is not an integer combination of (2,0) and (0,2). A basis needs both halves, which is precisely why Proposition 9.6 gets both surjectivity and injectivity out of it. — Aluffi §9.1, Definition 9.5, p. 175

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Homomorphisms between free modules are matrices

Corollary 9.11. Suppose R is commutative. For a homomorphism f : R⊕n → R⊕m there is an m × n matrix A over R with f(r) = A·r for all r; the jth column of A is f(ej). Conversely every m × n matrix determines such a homomorphism. — Aluffi §9.1, Corollary 9.11, p. 177

Note the index switch: the map goes from R⊕n to R⊕m — n first — and the matrix is m × n — n second. The number of columns is the rank of the source. Remark 9.12: remember firmly that the jth column is f(ej); Aluffi calls it invaluable in concrete computations. — Aluffi §9.1, Remark 9.12, p. 177

Example 9.13. A homomorphism ℤ⊕3 → ℤ⊕2 is the same as a 2 × 3 integer matrix. The matrix

A = ( −4   1   7 / −2   0   4 )

writing rows separated by a slash, represents the f with f(e₁) = (−4, −2), f(e₂) = (1, 0), f(e₃) = (7, 4) — that is, f(r₁, r₂, r₃) = (−4r₁ + r₂ + 7r₃, −2r₁ + 4r₃). Aluffi's comment: the matrix form is easier to parse. — Aluffi §9.1, Example 9.13, p. 178

And by Proposition 9.14, composition of homomorphisms corresponds to matrix multiplication — which is what makes the correspondence useful rather than merely tidy. — Aluffi §9.1, Proposition 9.14, p. 179

Elementary operations are composition with isomorphisms. Swapping two rows of A is the same as multiplying A on the left by the identity matrix with those rows swapped; swapping two columns is multiplying on the right by the same thing. The general mnemonic: an operation on rows is left multiplication by that operation applied to the identity; on columns, right multiplication. Since those elementary matrices are invertible, the operations amount to composing f with isomorphisms. — Aluffi §9.1, p. 181
Definition 9.15. Matrices A, A′ over R are equivalent if A′ = PAQ for some P, Q invertible over R. — Aluffi §9.2, Definition 9.15, p. 181

A sequence of elementary operations therefore produces an equivalent matrix. Remark 9.16 adds an honest caveat: the converse can fail — there are rings, even PIDs, with equivalent matrices that no sequence of elementary operations connects. For everything here, elementary operations suffice. — Aluffi §9.2, Remark 9.16, p. 181

Self-check 2. f : ℤ⊕4 → ℤ⊕2 is a homomorphism. What shape is its matrix, and what is its third column?

The source has rank 4, so there are 4 columns; the target has rank 2, so each column is a 2-tuple: a 2 × 4 matrix. And the jth column is the image of the jth basis vector, f(e₃) — not e₃ itself. Aluffi flags both points, the index switch and the column rule, because both are easy to invert under pressure. — Aluffi §9.1, Corollary 9.11 and Remark 9.12, p. 177

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Exact sequences, cokernels, and presentation by a matrix

Definition 9.17 (exact). A sequence of modules and homomorphisms is exact at Mi if im fi+1 = ker fi, and exact if it is exact at every module that is both a source and a target. — Aluffi §9.2, Definition 9.17, p. 182
What exactness says at the ends.

Example 9.18. M —f→ N → 0 is exact ⟺ f is surjective: the second map is trivial, so its kernel is all of N, and exactness demands im f = N. — Aluffi §9.2, Example 9.18, p. 182

Example 9.19. 0 → L —g→ M is exact ⟺ g is injective. — Aluffi §9.2, Example 9.19, p. 182

Example 9.20 — a short exact sequence is 0 → L → M → N → 0, and Aluffi asks you to develop the habit of reading it at once as N is M/L: g embeds L as the submodule im g = ker f, and the First Isomorphism Theorem gives N ≅ M/ker f. The standard example:

0 → ℤ —(×2)→ ℤ → ℤ/2ℤ → 0,

with the injection identifying ℤ with 2ℤ inside itself — aa-15's isomorphism 2ℤ ≅ ℤ, read as an exact sequence. — Aluffi §9.2, Example 9.20, p. 182; §8.5, Theorem 8.44, p. 168

Definition 9.21 (cokernel). For f : M → N,   coker f := N/im f. — Aluffi §9.2, Definition 9.21, p. 183

The kernel measures failure of injectivity; the cokernel measures failure of surjectivity. The name is literal — reverse every arrow in the universal property of kernels and you get the universal property cokernels satisfy. And for any f the sequence — Aluffi §9.2, p. 183

0 → ker f → M —f→ N → coker f → 0

is exact: at N, for instance, because the kernel of N → N/im f is im f.

Definition 9.22. M is finitely generated if there is an exact sequence R⊕m → M → 0 with m finite. — Aluffi §9.2, Definition 9.22, p. 183

Definition 9.23. M is finitely presented if there is an exact sequence R⊕n → R⊕m → M → 0 with both free modules of finite rank. — Aluffi §9.2, Definition 9.23, p. 183

Unwound: M is finitely generated when some R⊕m ↠ M (aa-17's Example 8.46 again), and finitely presented when the kernel of that surjection is also finitely generated, so that some R⊕n ↠ ker f. Equivalently — and this is the sentence the lesson is for — M is finitely presented exactly when it is the cokernel of a homomorphism of finitely generated free modules, hence the cokernel of a matrix. — Aluffi §9.2, pp. 183–184

So a finitely presented module has "an amazingly concrete description": a matrix. Any module-theoretic operation becomes an operation on that matrix, and this is exactly how computer algebra systems represent modules. — Aluffi §9.2, p. 184

Proposition 9.25. Let f : M → N and f′ : M′ → N′ be homomorphisms, and let φ : M′ → M and ψ : N → N′ be isomorphisms with f′ = ψ ∘ f ∘ φ. Then coker f ≅ coker f′. — Aluffi §9.2, Proposition 9.25, p. 184

Corollary 9.26. If A and A′ are equivalent matrices, the modules they define are isomorphic. — Aluffi §9.2, Corollary 9.26, p. 186

Sketch of Proposition 9.25. Compose to get a surjection π′ ∘ ψ : N → coker f′, and compute its kernel:

ker(π′ ∘ ψ) = ψ−1(ker π′) = ψ−1(f′(M′)) = ψ−1(ψ(f(M))) = f(M) = ker π,

using exactness of the bottom row, then commutativity of the diagram together with the surjectivity of φ−1. The First Isomorphism Theorem then gives coker f = N/ker π ≅ coker f′. ∎ — Aluffi §9.2, pp. 184–185

Once more: one surjection, one kernel, Theorem 8.44. That has now proved every structural theorem in three consecutive units.

Faded example — Example 9.27: what module is that matrix? Take A = ( −4   1   7 / −2   0   4 ) from Example 9.13, and let G be the cokernel of the corresponding map ℤ⊕3 → ℤ⊕2. Reduce by elementary operations, each of which leaves the cokernel unchanged by Corollary 9.26.

Swap columns 1 and 2:   ( 1   −4   7 / 0   −2   4 ).

Add (−2)× row 2 to row 1:   ( 1   0   ⟨your step: −4 + (−2)(−2) and 7 + (−2)(4)⟩ / 0   −2   4 ).

Add 2× column 2 to column 3, then column 1 to column 3, clearing the last column to ⟨your step⟩.

Multiply column 2 by −1, a unit in ℤ:   ( 1   0   0 / 0   2   0 ).

Now read the image off: the map sends e₁ ↦ (1, 0), e₂ ↦ (0, 2), e₃ ↦ (0, 0), so im = ℤ ⊕ 2ℤ inside ℤ ⊕ ℤ, and

G ≅ (ℤ ⊕ ℤ)/(ℤ ⊕ 2ℤ) ≅ ⟨your step: which famous abelian group?⟩.

— Aluffi §9.2, Example 9.27, p. 186

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi states Corollary 9.26 and writes: "Make sure you understand why Corollary 9.26 follows from Proposition 9.25." Write that argument out. Let A be an m × n matrix over R and A′ = PAQ with P, Q invertible. Show that the modules coker(A) and coker(A′) are isomorphic, by identifying the isomorphisms φ and ψ that Proposition 9.25 needs and checking the commuting condition. Then say which elementary operations correspond to P and which to Q.

Reveal model answer

Let f : R⊕n → R⊕m be r ↦ A·r and f′ : R⊕n → R⊕m be r ↦ A′·r.

Since Q is invertible, the map φ : R⊕n → R⊕n, φ(r) = Q·r, is an isomorphism (its inverse is multiplication by Q−1, and Corollary 9.11 makes both homomorphisms). Likewise ψ : R⊕m → R⊕m, ψ(s) = P·s, is an isomorphism.

By Proposition 9.14, composition corresponds to matrix multiplication, so

(ψ ∘ f ∘ φ)(r) = P·(A·(Q·r)) = (PAQ)·r = A′·r = f′(r),

i.e. f′ = ψ ∘ f ∘ φ, which is exactly the hypothesis of Proposition 9.25. Therefore coker f ≅ coker f′. ∎

Which is which. P multiplies on the left, so it performs row operations, and it is an automorphism of the target R⊕m — a change of basis downstream. Q multiplies on the right, so it performs column operations, an automorphism of the source R⊕n — a change of the generating set of relations.

Grade yourself on that last paragraph. Column operations do not change the image at all, since they only re-express the same set of columns; row operations move the image by an automorphism of R⊕m, so the submodule genuinely changes while the quotient does not. Both are legal, and confusing them is the standard way to lose track of what a reduction has computed.

You try (solo). (a) Identify the abelian group presented by the matrix ( 2   0 / 0   3 ) — a map ℤ⊕2 → ℤ⊕2 — first directly, then by reducing and comparing with aa-10's Chinese Remainder Theorem. (b) Do the same for ( 6   0 / 0   4 ), and note that the answer is not ℤ/6ℤ × ℤ/4ℤ rewritten arbitrarily: check your answer by counting elements. (c) Present ℤ/2ℤ ⊕ ℤ as the cokernel of a matrix ℤ⊕1 → ℤ⊕2. Then present the same module as the cokernel of a square matrix ℤ⊕2 → ℤ⊕2, and say what the extra column costs you. (Hint for the second: a zero on the diagonal contributes a free summand, exactly as in the faded example above. The moral is that the shape of a presenting matrix is not an invariant of the module — only its Smith normal form is.)

Self-check 3. The sequence 0 → L → M → N → 0 is exact. What is N?

Exactness at L makes the first map injective, so L embeds in M; exactness at M makes that image equal ker(M → N); exactness at N makes M → N surjective. The First Isomorphism Theorem then gives N ≅ M/ker = M/L. Aluffi asks you to make this reading automatic — a short exact sequence is a submodule and a quotient, packaged. — Aluffi §9.2, Example 9.20, p. 182

Self-check 4. What extra condition does "finitely presented" add to "finitely generated"?

Finitely generated means some R⊕m ↠ M; finitely presented means additionally that ker of that surjection is finitely generated, so some R⊕n ↠ ker, giving R⊕n → R⊕m → M → 0. In words: finitely many generators and finitely many relations. Free is much stronger — it means no relations — and finite is unrelated, since ℤ itself is finitely presented and infinite. Whether the second condition is automatic is aa-19's question. — Aluffi §9.2, Definitions 9.22 and 9.23, p. 183

Self-check 5. Why may you freely perform row and column operations when identifying the module a matrix presents?

Each elementary operation is multiplication by an invertible matrix on the left (rows) or right (columns), so the new matrix is PAQ — equivalent in the sense of Definition 9.15. Proposition 9.25 then says the cokernels are isomorphic, which is Corollary 9.26. The matrix certainly does change, and the determinant is not even defined for a non-square matrix; what is preserved is the isomorphism class of the module. — Aluffi §9.2, Corollary 9.26, p. 186

Then check yourself
  1. Free means having a basis (Definition 9.1, Proposition 9.6) — generating gives surjectivity, independence gives injectivity. Most modules are not free: ℤ/2ℤ is not free over ℤ, though it is free over itself. A homomorphism out of a free module is an arbitrary assignment of images to the basis (Proposition 9.8); out of a non-free module the images are constrained by relations, which is what "not free" costs.
  2. A homomorphism R⊕n → R⊕m is an m × n matrix whose jth column is f(ej) (Corollary 9.11), composition is matrix multiplication (Proposition 9.14), and elementary row/column operations are composition with isomorphisms — that is, replacement of A by an equivalent PAQ (Definition 9.15).
  3. A finitely presented module is the cokernel of a matrix (Definitions 9.21–9.23): finitely many generators and finitely many relations, packaged as R⊕n → R⊕m → M → 0. Equivalent matrices give isomorphic modules (Proposition 9.25, Corollary 9.26), so reducing the matrix identifies the module — the 2 × 3 matrix of Example 9.13 reduces to diag(1, 2) and presents ℤ/2ℤ.
That last sentence is the computational half of the module theory, and it is close to the algorithm — but say exactly what it computes. The matrix of ∂k presents its own cokernel, Ck−1/im ∂k; it does not present the chain module Ck, and it is not by itself Hk−1 = ker ∂k−1/im ∂k. Reducing one boundary matrix therefore gives you the image, and homology needs the adjacent map's kernel as well — which is why the persistence algorithm reduces the boundary matrices together rather than one at a time. aa-20 will say how far the reduction can always be pushed when R is a PID.
