Irreducibility and factorization in ℚ[x], ℤ[x] (aa-14)

Over ℂ the irreducible polynomials all have degree 1. Over ℝ they have degree 1 or 2. Over ℚ they have arbitrarily large degree, and there is no efficient test to recognise them.

Three fields, three completely different answers to one question. aa-13 proved that k[x] is a UFD whenever k is a field — so factorisations exist and are unique — but said nothing about which polynomials are the atoms. Today we find out, and the answer degrades sharply as the field gets smaller. The surprise in the middle is Gauss's Lemma: factoring over ℚ turns out to be the same problem as factoring over ℤ, which is not obvious and is what makes the integer tests legitimate.

Predict before we start. A polynomial in ℚ[x] has no rational roots. Must it be irreducible?

x⁴ − 3x² + 1 = (x² + x − 1)(x² − x − 1), and neither factor has a rational root, so the product has none either. "No roots" only rules out factors of degree 1. It settles irreducibility exactly for degrees 2 and 3, where any proper factorisation must contain a linear factor — Proposition 7.15 — and for degree 4 or more it settles nothing. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.32, p. 144

Why this matters for the mission: Rigour and technique; the factorisation habits carry straight into module decomposition.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Irreducibility over ℂ and ℝ — where it is easy

Why care? Because aa-13 showed k[x] is a PID, so by aa-12's Proposition 6.33 the ideal (f) is maximal exactly when f is irreducible — and therefore k[x]/(f) is a field precisely when f is irreducible in k[x]. Irreducibility is the test for whether a quotient is a field. — Aluffi §7.3, p. 136

Example 7.13. Over a field, every degree-1 polynomial is irreducible. Over a general domain this fails: 2x ∈ ℤ[x] has degree 1 and equals 2·x, a product of two non-units. — Aluffi §7.3, Example 7.13, p. 136

Proposition 7.14. Let k be a field and f ∈ k[x] with deg f ≥ 2. If f has a root, f is not irreducible. — Aluffi §7.3, Proposition 7.14, p. 136

Proposition 7.15. Let k be a field and f ∈ k[x] nonzero of degree 2 or 3. Then f is irreducible if and only if f has no roots in k. — Aluffi §7.3, Proposition 7.15, p. 137

Proofs. For 7.14: a root a gives f = (x − a)g with deg g = deg f − 1 ≥ 1, so g is not a unit (aa-13's guided proof: units of k[x] are the nonzero constants). ∎

For 7.15, the forward direction is 7.14 contrapositively. Conversely, if f = q₁q₂ with neither a unit, then neither is constant, and since deg f is 2 or 3 one of them has degree 1, say ax + b with a ≠ 0. Then −ba−1 is a root of f. ∎ — Aluffi §7.3, p. 137

Remark 7.16 — both hypotheses are needed. k must be a field: the proof inverted a. In ℤ[x], (2x − 1)² is reducible and has no root in ℤ — its root ½ lives in ℚ. The degree bound is real: x⁴ − 4 = (x² − 2)(x² + 2) is reducible in ℚ[x] with roots ±√2, ±i√2, none rational. — Aluffi §7.3, Remark 7.16, p. 137

Theorem 7.17 (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). Every nonconstant f ∈ ℂ[x] has a root in ℂ. — Aluffi §7.3, Theorem 7.17, p. 137

That is, ℂ is algebraically closed. Aluffi does not prove it here and neither will we: the standard proof belongs to complex analysis — if f had no root then 1/f would be analytic on all of ℂ, and the maximum modulus principle produces a contradiction — and even the algebraic proof in §15.6 uses real analysis. It is quoted, not derived. — Aluffi §7.3, p. 137

The two corollaries over ℂ.

Corollary 7.18. f ∈ ℂ[x] is irreducible ⟺ deg f = 1. Degree 0 is a unit; degree ≥ 2 has a root by Theorem 7.17, so is reducible by 7.14. — Aluffi §7.3, Corollary 7.18, pp. 137–138

Corollary 7.19. Every f ∈ ℂ[x] of degree d factors into d linear factors, uniquely up to units:

f(x) = u(x − a₁)⋯(x − ad),

with a₁, …, ad the roots. This is Corollary 7.8 (unique factorisation in ℂ[x]) plus the identification of the irreducibles. — Aluffi §7.3, Corollary 7.19, p. 138

Proposition 7.20. f ∈ ℝ[x] is irreducible if and only if either deg f = 1, or deg f = 2 with f = ax² + bx + c and b² − 4ac < 0. — Aluffi §7.3, Proposition 7.20, p. 138
Proof. (⟸) Degree 1 is irreducible; a negative discriminant means no real root, so Proposition 7.15 applies. (⟹) Degree 0 is a unit. Degree 2 with b² − 4ac ≥ 0 has a real root. For deg f ≥ 3, view f in ℂ[x]: by Theorem 7.17 it has a root α ∈ ℂ. If α is real we have a linear factor. If not, then since f has real coefficients, f(ᾱ) = f(α)‾ = 0, so ᾱ ≠ α is also a root, both x − α and x − ᾱ occur in the ℂ[x]-factorisation (ℂ[x] is a UFD), and

(x − α)(x − ᾱ) = x² − (α + ᾱ)x + αᾱ

has real coefficients and divides f. Either way f has a proper factor. ∎ — Aluffi §7.3, pp. 138–139

There is also a cheap calculus argument for part of this: a real polynomial of odd degree tends to +∞ at one end and −∞ at the other, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem its graph crosses the axis and it has a real root. Odd degree ≥ 3 is therefore always reducible. Watch it happen below. — Aluffi §7.3, p. 138

Example 7.21 — x⁴ + 4 over ℝ. By Proposition 7.20 it must be reducible, since its degree exceeds 2 — no computation needed. But run the proof and see the gears turn. Over ℂ, x⁴ = −4 gives the four roots ±1 ± i, in conjugate pairs. The pair 1 ± i contributes

(x − (1 + i))(x − (1 − i)) = (x − 1)² − i² = x² − 2x + 2,

a real quadratic factor; the other pair gives x² + 2x + 2, and indeed x⁴ + 4 = (x² − 2x + 2)(x² + 2x + 2). Note that x⁴ + 4 has no real roots at all, and is reducible anyway. — Aluffi §7.3, Example 7.21, p. 139

Self-check 1. What are the irreducible polynomials of ℝ[x]?

Degree 1 always, and ax² + bx + c exactly when b² − 4ac < 0. Nothing of degree 3 or more survives, because a non-real root drags its conjugate along and the pair contributes a real quadratic factor. "No real roots" is not enough — x⁴ + 4 has none and factors into two real quadratics. Degree 1 only is the answer for ℂ, by Corollary 7.18. — Aluffi §7.3, Proposition 7.20, p. 138

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Gauss's Lemma: factoring over ℚ is factoring over ℤ

First, a real difference between the two rings. Over a field k, f is reducible exactly when it is a product of two polynomials of lower degree. Over ℤ that is false: 2x + 2 = 2·(x + 1) is reducible in ℤ[x], because 2 is not a unit there — while in ℚ[x] the same polynomial is irreducible, since 2 is a unit. — Aluffi §7.4, pp. 139–140

Remarkably, that constant-factor nuisance is the only difference. Proving it takes some cleverness.

Theorem 7.22 (Gauss's Lemma). Let f ∈ ℤ[x] and suppose f = gh with g, h ∈ ℚ[x]. Then there are rationals a, b with ag ∈ ℤ[x], bh ∈ ℤ[x] and f = (ag)(bh). — Aluffi §7.4, Theorem 7.22, p. 140

In words: every factorisation of an integer polynomial over ℚ can be converted into one over ℤ. The converse is trivial. So factoring in ℚ[x] and factoring in ℤ[x] are the same problem. — Aluffi §7.4, p. 140

Lemma 7.23. Let α, β ∈ ℤ[x] and let q be an irreducible integer dividing every coefficient of αβ. Then q divides every coefficient of α, or every coefficient of β. — Aluffi §7.4, Lemma 7.23, p. 140
Proof of Lemma 7.23 — an ideal argument, not a computation. Let I = qℤ and let I[x] be the ideal generated by q in ℤ[x]. There is an isomorphism

(ℤ/I)[x] ≅ ℤ[x]/I[x],

a standard application of the First Isomorphism Theorem. Now chase the properties: q is irreducible, so I = (q) is prime in ℤ, so ℤ/I is an integral domain, so (ℤ/I)[x] is an integral domain, so ℤ[x]/I[x] is an integral domain, so I[x] is a prime ideal. And I[x] is exactly the set of polynomials all of whose coefficients are divisible by q. Primality of I[x] says

αβ ∈ I[x] ⟹ α ∈ I[x] or β ∈ I[x],

which is the statement of the lemma. ∎ — Aluffi §7.4, p. 140

Worth pausing on. The lemma is a concrete claim about integer coefficients, and the proof never touches a coefficient: it translates the claim into "this ideal is prime" and reads the answer off aa-11's Definition 6.1. That is the payoff of the language.

Proof of Gauss's Lemma. Choose any rationals a, b with ag, bh ∈ ℤ[x]. Then (ag)(bh) = ab·f, and we must arrange ab = 1. Consider

S = { c = ab > 0 : ag ∈ ℤ[x], bh ∈ ℤ[x], ab ∈ ℤ },

which is nonempty (scale a or b by a large integer, flipping a sign if needed). By well-ordering take the least c ∈ S, and suppose c ≠ 1. Then c has an irreducible factor q, by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Now q divides every coefficient of ab·f = (ag)(bh), so by Lemma 7.23 it divides every coefficient of ag, say. Then q−1ag ∈ ℤ[x], so c/q = (q−1a)b ∈ S — and c/q < c, contradicting minimality. Hence c = 1. ∎ — Aluffi §7.4, p. 141

Remark 7.24. The contradiction turned on the size of the least element of S. Aluffi notes that a different measure works just as well — the number of irreducible factors of ab counted with repetition — and that recasting the proof that way is what generalises to an arbitrary UFD. — Aluffi §7.4, Remark 7.24, p. 141

Definition 7.25. The content of a nonzero f ∈ ℤ[x] is the gcd of its coefficients; f is primitive if its content is 1. — Aluffi §7.4, Definition 7.25, p. 141

Every f ∈ ℤ[x] splits as f = d·f₁ with d the content and f₁ primitive. — Aluffi §7.4, p. 141

Corollary 7.26. Let f ∈ ℤ[x] be primitive. Then f is irreducible in ℤ[x] if and only if f is irreducible in ℚ[x]. — Aluffi §7.4, Corollary 7.26, p. 141

Corollary 7.27. ℤ[x] is a UFD. — Aluffi §7.4, Corollary 7.27, p. 142

Proof of Corollary 7.26. If f = gh in ℤ[x] with neither a unit, then neither can be a constant — a constant factor would divide every coefficient, contradicting content 1 — so both have positive degree and neither is a unit in ℚ[x]: f is reducible in ℚ[x]. Conversely if f = gh in ℚ[x] with both of positive degree, Gauss's Lemma converts this into a factorisation in ℤ[x]. ∎ — Aluffi §7.4, p. 142

The classification. The irreducible elements of ℤ[x] are exactly: the irreducible integers, and the primitive polynomials that are irreducible in ℚ[x]. Corollary 7.27 follows, and gives aa-12's Remark 6.38 its promised example — ℤ[x] is a UFD that is not a PID. By the same route k[x₁, …, xn] and ℤ[x₁, …, xn] are UFDs. — Aluffi §7.4, pp. 142–143

Guided proof (write it yourself)

Aluffi calls two steps "clear" and moves on. Make them explicit. (a) Prove that if q is an irreducible integer then q is irreducible as an element of ℤ[x]. (b) Deduce the classification: the irreducible elements of ℤ[x] are precisely the irreducible integers together with the primitive polynomials that are irreducible in ℚ[x]. For (a) you will need the units of ℤ[x] and the degree formula from aa-13.

Reveal model answer

(a) By aa-13's guided proof, ℤ[x]∗ = ℤ∗ = {±1}, so q is not a unit in ℤ[x]. Suppose q = g(x)h(x) with g, h ∈ ℤ[x]. Since ℤ is a domain, deg g + deg h = deg q = 0, so g and h are constants, say integers b and c with q = bc. As q is irreducible in ℤ, one of b, c is ±1, i.e. a unit of ℤ[x]. Hence q is irreducible in ℤ[x].

(b) Let f ∈ ℤ[x] be irreducible. Write f = d·f₁ with d the content and f₁ primitive. Since f is irreducible, one of d, f₁ is a unit.

Case f₁ a unit. Then f₁ = ±1 and f = ±d is a constant, an irreducible element of ℤ — by (a) read backwards, since a reducible integer would factor in ℤ[x] too.

Case d a unit. Then d = ±1 and f is primitive, and by Corollary 7.26 f is irreducible in ℤ[x] if and only if it is irreducible in ℚ[x].

Conversely both kinds of element are irreducible in ℤ[x]: irreducible integers by (a), and primitive ℚ[x]-irreducibles by Corollary 7.26. ∎

Grade yourself on the content split in (b). If your argument did not use f = d·f₁, it almost certainly missed that a constant can be an irreducible element of ℤ[x] — which is exactly the phenomenon 2x + 2 exhibits, and exactly what makes ℤ[x] and ℚ[x] behave differently.

Self-check 2. Is 2x + 2 irreducible in ℤ[x]? In ℚ[x]?

2x + 2 = 2·(x + 1). In ℤ[x] neither factor is a unit, so it is reducible; in ℚ[x] the constant 2 is a unit, so this is not a proper factorisation and the polynomial is irreducible (degree 1 over a field). The content is 2, so the polynomial is not primitive — and Corollary 7.26 is stated only for primitive polynomials for exactly this reason. — Aluffi §7.4, p. 140

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Three tests, and their limits

Proposition 7.29 (rational root test). Let f = a₀ + a₁x + ⋯ + adxd ∈ ℤ[x] and let c = p/q ∈ ℚ be a root, with p, q relatively prime. Then p ∣ a₀ and q ∣ ad. — Aluffi §7.5, Proposition 7.29, p. 143
Proof. Substitute and clear denominators:

a₀qd + a₁pqd−1 + ⋯ + adpd = 0.

Isolating gives a₀qd = −(⋯)p and adpd = −(⋯)q, so p ∣ a₀qd and q ∣ adpd. Since gcd(p, q) = 1, aa-02's Corollary 1.11 strips the powers and leaves p ∣ a₀, q ∣ ad. ∎ — Aluffi §7.5, p. 143

So finding rational roots is a finite search: list the divisors of a₀ and ad and test each p/q. If a root turns up and deg f ≥ 2, f is reducible. If none turns up and deg f ≤ 3, f is irreducible (Proposition 7.15). Outside that degree window the test is silent.

Faded example — Example 7.30: is f(x) = 4x³ + 2x² − 5x + 3 irreducible in ℚ[x]?

Degree 3, so by Proposition 7.15 it is enough to check for ⟨your step: what?⟩.

By Proposition 7.29 a root p/q needs p ∣ 3 and q ∣ ⟨your step⟩, so the candidates are ±1, ±3, ±1/2, ±3/2, ±1/4, ±3/4 — ⟨your step: how many?⟩ of them.

Substituting each gives no zero, so f has no rational root, so f is irreducible in ℚ[x]. And since the content of f is gcd(4, 2, 5, 3) = ⟨your step⟩, f is primitive, so by Corollary 7.26 it is irreducible in ℤ[x] too. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.30, p. 144

Now change one digit. Example 7.31 takes g(x) = 4x³ + 2x² − 4x + 3, with the same constant term and leading coefficient and hence the same candidate list. This time g(−3/2) = 0, so g is reducible in ℚ[x] — and by Corollary 7.26 also in ℤ[x], with 2x + 3 as a factor, even though g has no root in ℤ. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.31, p. 144

Proposition 7.33 (reduction mod p). Let f ∈ ℤ[x] and let p be an irreducible integer. If p does not divide the leading coefficient of f, and the reduction of f is irreducible in (ℤ/pℤ)[x], then f cannot be written as a product of positive-degree polynomials; hence f is irreducible in ℚ[x]. — Aluffi §7.5, Proposition 7.33, p. 144
Proof. Contrapositively, suppose f = gh with deg g, deg h > 0. The leading coefficient of f is the product of those of g and h; since p does not divide it and p is prime, p divides neither. (That ℤ/pℤ is a domain at all — indeed a field — is aa-04’s Theorem 2.15, and both this proof and Eisenstein’s below rest on it. — Aluffi §2.4, Theorem 2.15, p. 32) So reduction mod p preserves both degrees, and the reduced f is the product of two positive-degree polynomials — reducible. ∎ Primitivity plus Corollary 7.26 then upgrades ℚ[x]-irreducibility to ℤ[x]. — Aluffi §7.5, p. 145

Example 7.34. f(x) = 675x³ − 23129x + 1573. The rational root test would be impractical here. But the leading coefficient is odd, and mod 2 the polynomial becomes x³ + x + 1, which has no root in ℤ/2ℤ (check 0 and 1) and so — being of degree 3 — is irreducible there. Hence f is irreducible. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.34, p. 145

And the limit of the test. There are polynomials reducible mod every prime that are nonetheless irreducible in ℤ[x]. Aluffi's example is x⁴ + 1. Mod 2 it is (x + 1)⁴; and it is reducible modulo every positive prime, though Aluffi notes that verifying that "requires more sophisticated considerations" than are available here. So the test can never certify x⁴ + 1, whichever prime you try. — Aluffi §7.5, p. 145

Theorem 7.35 (Eisenstein's criterion). Let f = a₀ + a₁x + ⋯ + adxd ∈ ℤ[x] and let p be an irreducible integer with

• p ∤ ad;   • p ∣ ai for all i < d;   • p² ∤ a₀.

Then f is not a product of two positive-degree polynomials, hence is irreducible in ℚ[x]. — Aluffi §7.5, Theorem 7.35, pp. 145–146
Lemma 7.36. If R is an integral domain and g, h ∈ R[x] with gh a monomial, then g and h are both monomials.

Proof. Let r and s be the least exponents with nonzero coefficients in g and h, and m, n the degrees. Then gh = brcsxr+s + ⋯ + bmcnxm+n, and both end coefficients are nonzero because R is a domain. If g is not a monomial then r < m, so r + s < m + n and gh has two distinct nonzero coefficients — not a monomial. ∎ — Aluffi §7.5, Lemma 7.36, p. 146

Proof of Eisenstein. Suppose f = gh with deg g = m > 0, deg h = n > 0. Reduce mod p. By the first two hypotheses the reduction of f is the monomial adxd with ad ≠ 0. Since ℤ/pℤ is a domain, Lemma 7.36 forces the reductions of g and h to be monomials — necessarily bmxm and cnxn. As m, n > 0, the constant terms reduce to 0: p ∣ b₀ and p ∣ c₀. But then p² ∣ b₀c₀ = a₀, contradicting the third hypothesis. ∎ — Aluffi §7.5, p. 146

Each hypothesis is load-bearing and used exactly once: the first keeps the reduction from collapsing in degree, the second makes it a monomial, and the third supplies the contradiction. Drop any one and the argument stops.

What Eisenstein delivers.

Example 7.37. For irreducible p, the polynomial xn − p satisfies the criterion (p ∤ 1, p ∣ −p, p² ∤ −p). So xn − p is irreducible in ℚ[x] — and in particular x² − p has no rational root, which is a proof that √p is irrational, and more generally that the n-th root of p is irrational. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.37, p. 147

Corollary 7.38. ℚ[x] has irreducible polynomials of arbitrarily high degree — take xn − p for any n. Contrast ℂ[x], where irreducibles have degree 1 (Corollary 7.18), and ℝ[x], where they have degree at most 2 (Proposition 7.20). No bound holds over ℚ. — Aluffi §7.5, Corollary 7.38, p. 147

Example 7.39 — a shift of variable. For irreducible p the cyclotomic polynomial γ(x) = 1 + x + ⋯ + xp−1 has all coefficients 1, so Eisenstein seems useless. But γ is irreducible if and only if γ(1 + x) is, and since γ(x) = (xp − 1)/(x − 1),

γ(1 + x) = ((1 + x)p − 1)/x = p + C(p,2)x + ⋯ + C(p,p−1)xp−2 + xp−1,

whose non-leading coefficients are all divisible by p while the constant term p is not divisible by p². Eisenstein applies. — Aluffi §7.5, Example 7.39, p. 147

(Aluffi's hint: write C(p, i) = [(p−1)⋯(p−i+1) / i(i−1)⋯1]·p and argue that the bracket is an integer.) (b) Then decide, with reasons, whether each of x³ − 3x + 1, 2x⁵ − 6x³ + 9x − 3 and x⁴ + 1 is irreducible in ℚ[x] — using the rational root test, Eisenstein, and reduction mod p respectively, and saying which test fails on which polynomial.

Self-check 3. Why does Eisenstein's criterion require p² ∤ a₀?

The reduction becomes a monomial from the first two hypotheses. Lemma 7.36 then forces p ∣ b₀ and p ∣ c₀, so p² ∣ b₀c₀ = a₀ — and the third hypothesis is precisely what makes that impossible. Each of the three conditions is used once, at a different point, which is why none can be dropped. — Aluffi §7.5, p. 146

Self-check 4. x⁴ + 1 factors modulo 2, modulo 3, modulo 5 and modulo 7. Does it follow that it is reducible over ℤ?

Proposition 7.33 says irreducible mod p ⟹ irreducible over ℚ. It says nothing when the reduction factors — that is the "not quite right" direction Aluffi flags. x⁴ + 1 is the standing example: it is reducible modulo every positive prime and irreducible in ℤ[x]. So no accumulation of primes can ever settle it, and no fourth prime, or fortieth, would change that. — Aluffi §7.5, p. 145

Self-check 5. Corollary 7.26 assumes f is primitive. Where does the proof use it?

If f = gh in ℤ[x] with neither a unit, a constant factor would divide every coefficient of f and so divide the content — impossible when the content is 1. That is what forces both factors to have positive degree, hence to be non-units in ℚ[x]. Gauss's Lemma is used in the other direction and needs no primitivity. And 2x + 2 shows the corollary is genuinely false without it. — Aluffi §7.4, Corollary 7.26, pp. 141–142

Then check yourself
  1. Irreducibility depends violently on the field. Over ℂ, the irreducibles are exactly the degree-1 polynomials (Corollary 7.18, from the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra), so every polynomial splits into linear factors. Over ℝ they are degree 1 and the negative-discriminant quadratics (Proposition 7.20), because non-real roots come in conjugate pairs. Over ℚ there is no degree bound at all (Corollary 7.38). Roots decide irreducibility only in degrees 2 and 3 (Proposition 7.15).
  2. Gauss's Lemma (Theorem 7.22) converts any ℚ[x]-factorisation of an integer polynomial into a ℤ[x]-factorisation, using a minimal-denominator argument resting on Lemma 7.23 — which is proved by showing that "all coefficients divisible by q" is a prime ideal. Consequence: for primitive f, irreducible in ℤ[x] ⟺ irreducible in ℚ[x] (Corollary 7.26), the irreducibles of ℤ[x] are the irreducible integers plus those primitive polynomials, and ℤ[x] is a UFD that is not a PID (Corollary 7.27).
  3. Three tests, all one-directional. The rational root test (Proposition 7.29) makes root-finding a finite search but decides irreducibility only up to degree 3. Reduction mod p (Proposition 7.33) certifies irreducibility but never reducibility — and x⁴ + 1 is reducible modulo every prime while irreducible over ℤ. Eisenstein (Theorem 7.35) certifies irreducibility when the coefficients line up, sometimes only after a shift of variable, and gives xn − p, hence irrational n-th roots and unbounded irreducible degrees.
The habit to carry into aa-15 onwards: a factorisation theorem tells you the atoms exist and are unique; identifying which elements are atoms is a separate and usually harder question. The structure theorem of aa-20 will decompose modules into pieces indexed by irreducibles, and this is the lesson about how hard those irreducibles are to name.
