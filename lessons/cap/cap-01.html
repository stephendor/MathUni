Scoping a research question (cap-01)

Three semesters of theorems, and the capstone will turn on a sentence you write before any of them run.

The hardest part of a project is not the mathematics. It is deciding what claim you are trying to support, and what would falsify it. Get that wrong and every theorem downstream is still true and still useless: you will have computed something correct about a question that could not have come out any other way.

This unit has no textbook. What it has instead is better suited to the job: a stack of theorems whose hypotheses you now know, and which between them decide what a claim is even permitted to be about. A scoped question is one the theorems can reach.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Your point sample is accurate to ε in Hausdorff distance — the true set and your sample are within ε of each other. Predict which features of the Rips persistence diagram a claim may legitimately be about.

The bound is on the bottleneck distance, and a bottleneck matching is allowed to send a diagram point to the diagonal at a cost equal to its L∞-distance to it — which for the point (b, d) is (d − b)/2, half its persistence. So a feature of persistence 2ε costs exactly ε to delete, and the theorem permits it to be deleted. The first option confuses "the diagrams are close" with "the features correspond"; the third forgets the factor of two that the distance-to-diagonal introduces. Segment 2 derives this properly, and it is the single most useful sentence in the unit: it converts an assumption about your data collection into a threshold below which you have agreed not to speak.

Why this matters for the mission: A question is well scoped when the topological summary you plan to compute could in principle come out the other way. This is the step that separates a TDA analysis from a TDA demonstration.

A note on sources. This unit's resources are listed as "self-directed" and tdlbook.org, and the second is a book on topological deep learning; Segment 3 says exactly what it does and does not supply. Everything mathematical below is cited to the texts this curriculum has already read, and everything methodological is either cited to a repository artifact or is the unit's own argument, marked as such.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

A claim, and the thing that would kill it

Start with the document the capstone answers to. MISSION.md lists four things that success looks like, and two of them are constraints on this unit's work:

From MISSION.md, "Success looks like". "I can read a current TDA paper (e.g. on multiparameter persistence) and verify its main argument." — "I can explain the stability theorem for persistence diagrams and why it licenses the TDL pipeline's conclusions." — MathUni MISSION.md

Read the second one slowly. It does not say a stability theorem is nice to know. It says a conclusion must be one that a stability theorem licenses — which means there are conclusions that no stability theorem licenses, and that a well-scoped question is one that stays inside the licensed set. That is a mathematical constraint on the claim, available before any data is touched, and Segment 2 makes it a number.

The first constraint is about evidence: verification is an operation on an argument and its hypotheses, so a claim has to be the sort of thing that has hypotheses to check. "The data has interesting topological structure" has none. It is not a claim; it is a mood. No observation contradicts it, because every barcode has bars.

The shape every claim in this curriculum has

Look at what the last three semesters actually proved. Every guarantee has the same form: under hypotheses H, the computed object stands in relation R to the hidden object. Not one is unconditional. Four examples you have already met, with their hypotheses in the open:

Two of the four require a quantity — the reach, the weak feature size — that cannot be computed from a sample. That is not a defect of the theorems; it is information about what a claim can be. It means a capstone claim invoking Theorem 6.11 or Theorem 6.13 is conditional, and scoping the question includes deciding what argument, from the acquisition process rather than from the data, will support the condition.

Worked example: three claims, graded.

Claim A. "Topology reveals hidden structure in the trajectory data." Verdict: not a claim. "Structure" is undefined, "reveals" names no measurement, and there is no observation that would contradict it. The tell is that you cannot write the falsifier down. Try: what output would make you say this was wrong? There is none, because every dataset has a barcode and every barcode has bars.

Claim B. "The trajectory embedding contains a persistent one-dimensional cycle." Verdict: falsifiable in principle, unscoped in practice. It names a dimension and an object, which is progress. But "persistent" has no threshold and no reference: persistent compared to what? Until both are fixed, the claim is decided by a choice you make after seeing the answer, which is the definition of unfalsifiable in practice.

Claim C. "The observed H1 diagram lies further from second-order Markov surrogates, in 2-Wasserstein distance, than those surrogates lie from one another — for features of persistence above 2ε, with ε the assumed sampling accuracy." Verdict: scoped. It names a statistic (a Wasserstein distance, in the sense of Definition 13.8), a reference class (the surrogates), a dimension (H1), and a threshold (2ε, which Segment 2 derives). And it has an explicit falsifier: the observed-to-surrogate distance falls inside the surrogate-to-surrogate distribution.

The pattern. A → B added a dimension and an object. B → C added a statistic, a reference and a threshold. Those three are what turn a sentence into a question, and none of them is supplied by the mathematics — they are decisions, and the point of writing them before running anything is that afterwards you will not be able to tell which of them you chose because it was right and which because it worked.

Self-check 1. Why is "the trajectory embedding contains a persistent one-dimensional cycle" not yet a scoped claim?

The defect is that the deciding quantity is undetermined at the time the claim is made — whatever bar you happen to see can be called persistent afterwards. H1 features are perfectly claimable once a threshold and a reference exist, which is what Claim C supplies, so the second option overshoots. The coefficient field is a real parameter and belongs in the pre-registration, but its absence is not what stops this sentence being a claim.

Self-check 2. Theorem 6.13 requires ε < (1/9)wfs(X). What does that do to a claim invoking it?

The weak feature size of Definition 6.10 is defined through the critical points of the distance field of the hidden compact set; it is not a statistic of the sample and there is nothing to compute. That does not forbid the claim — hypotheses about data can be argued for from how the data were acquired — but it does mean the hypothesis is part of what is being asserted, and hiding it in a footnote misrepresents the result. The third option is the overcorrection that would rule out most of applied mathematics.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

What the theorems let a claim be about

Now the number. Recall the two objects the pipeline builds from a finite sample P in a metric space.

Definition 2.10 (Vietoris–Rips complex). For a finite metric space (P, d) and scale r, 𝕍ℝr(P) is the complex whose simplices are the σ = {p0,…,pk} with d(pi, pj) ≤ 2r for all i, j. — Dey & Wang §2.2, Definition 2.10, p. 32

Definition 6.1 (Čech and Rips distances). For finite point sets P and Q, dCech(P,Q) = maxk dB(Dgmk𝒞(P), Dgmk𝒞(Q)) and dRips(P,Q) = maxk dB(Dgmkℛ(P), Dgmkℛ(Q)). — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.1, p. 181

Definition 6.2 (Hausdorff distance). For compact A, B ⊆ (Z, dZ), dH(A,B) = max{maxa∈A dZ(a,B), maxb∈B dZ(b,A)}. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.2, p. 181

Theorem 6.3(a). For finite P, Q ⊆ (Z, dZ): dCech(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q) and dRips(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q). — Dey & Wang §6.1, Theorem 6.3, p. 182

This is the theorem that converts a statement about your data collection into a statement about your diagram. Assume the sample is within ε of the thing it samples, in Hausdorff distance; then the diagrams are within ε in bottleneck distance, in every dimension. The question is what that buys, and the answer is not "every feature is pinned to within ε".

Visual: the band you have agreed not to speak inside

The shaded wedge is the set of diagram points with d − b ≤ 2ε. As the assumed sampling accuracy grows, the wedge swallows features, and the gold count falls. Nothing about the data changed; what changed is the accuracy you were willing to claim, and with it the number of things you are entitled to talk about. That is the whole of scoping in one picture.

Guided proof

Prompt. Let X be the hidden set, P your finite sample, and suppose dH(P, X) ≤ ε. (1) Using Theorem 6.3(a) and Definition 6.1, state precisely what follows about Dgmkℛ(P) and Dgmkℛ(X). (2) Recall that a bottleneck matching may send a diagram point to the diagonal at cost equal to its L∞-distance to it. Compute that distance for a point (b, d) and deduce the threshold below which a feature of your diagram may be absent from the true one. (3) State the resulting rule as a constraint on a research claim, and say what the rule does not say.

Hint: for (2), the closest diagonal point to (b, d) in the L∞ metric is the midpoint ((b+d)/2, (b+d)/2).

Click to reveal model answer

(1) What follows. Theorem 6.3(a) gives dRips(P, X) ≤ dH(P, X) ≤ ε, and by Definition 6.1 dRips is the maximum over k of the bottleneck distances between the k-th Rips diagrams. So for every k, dB(Dgmkℛ(P), Dgmkℛ(X)) ≤ ε: there exists a matching between the two diagrams in which no point moves more than ε in the L∞ metric, where unmatched points are matched to the diagonal.

(2) The threshold. Fix a point (b, d) with b < d. The diagonal is {(x, x)}, and the L∞-distance from (b, d) to (x, x) is max{|b − x|, |d − x|}, which is minimised by taking x midway, x = (b + d)/2, giving

dist∞((b, d), Δ) = (d − b)/2 = ½ · persistence.

Now suppose (b, d) is a point of Dgmkℛ(P) with (d − b)/2 ≤ ε. Then a matching that sends it to the diagonal pays at most ε for that point, which the bound permits. Hence the ε-bound is consistent with (b, d) having no partner in Dgmkℛ(X) at all: the feature may be entirely absent from the true diagram. Contrapositively, a feature that is guaranteed to have a genuine partner is one that cannot be matched to the diagonal within budget, that is one with

persistence = d − b > 2ε.

(3) The rule, and its limits. The rule: given an assumed sampling accuracy ε, a claim may be made only about features of persistence exceeding 2ε; below that threshold the theorem licenses nothing, and an assertion there is an assertion about the particular sample rather than about what it samples. Fix ε first, derive the threshold, and write both into the claim.

What the rule does not say. It does not say a feature of persistence above 2ε is real — it says only that the stability bound alone cannot dispose of it; the feature could still be an artifact of the metric, the embedding, or the sampling scheme, none of which Theorem 6.3 speaks to. It does not say a feature below the threshold is absent from the truth; it says you cannot tell. And it says nothing about H∗ of the hidden set: relating the diagram to the topology of X is Theorem 6.11's or Theorem 6.13's job, and those carry the further hypotheses on reach or weak feature size that no sample supplies. The rule is a filter on what may be discussed, not a warrant for what survives it.

Theorem 10.15. Given h, h′ : X → ℝ, the interleaving distance between the sublevel set persistent homology sequences is bounded by the L∞ norm of the difference of the height functions: d(Hk(Xt), Hk(X′t)) ≤ ‖h − h′‖∞. — Ghrist §10.6, Theorem 10.15, p. 220

The same move, in the other setting. Theorem 6.3 converts an error in where the points are into a diagram bound; Theorem 10.15 converts an error in what the function says about them into one. Most real pipelines have both kinds of error, and a scoping document should name which one its ε is, because the two are not interchangeable and neither bounds the other.

Definition 6.8 (local feature size; reach). The local feature size at x ∈ X is its distance to the medial axis of X; the reach ρ(X) is the minimum local feature size over X. — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.8, pp. 193–194

Definition 6.10 (weak feature size). With C the set of critical points of the generalized gradient ∇d, wfs(X) = minx∈X infc∈C d(x,c). — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.10, p. 195

And the two theorems that need them: Theorem 6.11, which for a smooth manifold X of reach ρ(X) and a sample with dH(P,X) ≤ ε gives rank(im(i∗)) = dim(H∗(X)) for 3ε ≤ α ≤ (3/16)√(3/5)ρ(X); and Theorem 6.13, which for compact X with ε < (1/9)wfs(X) gives H∗(Xλ) ≅ im(j∗) for α in a stated window. Both are conditional on a number the sample cannot produce. Naming that number, and saying what would justify a bound on it, is part of scoping — not a caveat to be added at the end.

Self-check 3. Your assumed sampling accuracy doubles from ε to 2ε. What happens to the set of claimable features?

The diagram indeed does not depend on ε — but the set of features a theorem licenses you to discuss does, and that is what a claim is made of. Doubling the assumed error doubles the band and evicts every feature of persistence between 2ε and 4ε. The third option mistakes "worst-case" for "ignorable": a worst-case bound is exactly the one a claim must survive.

Self-check 4. Which of Theorem 6.3 and Theorem 10.15 applies when the points are right but the scalar field measured on them is noisy?

The two theorems have different inputs: one takes dH between point sets, the other ‖h − h′‖∞ between functions on a fixed space. Absorbing function noise into point positions is not free — it changes the metric and hence the complex — so the second option smuggles in an unstated assumption. The third is wrong on the face of it: dH is not a sup-norm on functions, and the conclusions are stated for different constructions.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Making "could come out the other way" operational

The threshold of Segment 2 says what a claim may be about. It does not say the claim is testable. For that you need a reference: a distribution of the statistic under the assumption that the claimed structure is absent. That is a null model, and the operational form of the mission strip is this — before the analysis runs, someone else must be able to name the output you have committed to reporting as failure.

Definition 13.8 ((p,q)-Wasserstein distance). For persistence diagrams P and Q, dW,qp(P,Q) := infΠ : P→Q [ Σx∈P ‖x − Π(x)‖pq ]1/q, over bijections Π. — Dey & Wang §13.3, Definition 13.8, p. 406

A statistic of this kind is what a null distribution is built for: compute it between the observed diagram and each surrogate, and separately between pairs of surrogates, and ask whether the first distribution sits outside the second.

Worked example (faded): a null that does nothing. A proposed test: compute the H1 Rips diagram of the point cloud; then permute the class labels attached to the points and recompute; repeat 1000 times.

Step 1 — what does the statistic consume? The diagram comes from the Rips filtration, and by Definition 2.10 the Rips complex is determined entirely by the ⟨your step: by what data?⟩.

Step 2 — what does the null perturb? The labels, which are not among that data.

Step 3 — conclude. Every surrogate has exactly the same complex at every scale, hence exactly the same diagram, so the null distribution is a point mass at the observed value and the test has ⟨your step: how much power?⟩. It will never reject, whatever is true.

Step 4 — the general rule. A null must perturb the object the statistic consumes. Diagnose a proposed null by tracing backwards from the statistic to the data it reads, and asking whether the perturbation touches that. If it does not, the surrogate is a copy of the observation wearing a different label.

Gap solutions: the pairwise distances among the points; and no power at all.

Two more requirements follow, and neither is optional.

Nulls should differ in what they destroy. Two surrogates that break the same structure are one surrogate run twice. An order shuffle that destroys temporal sequence while preserving the marginal distribution of states, and a second-order Markov surrogate that preserves pairwise transition structure and destroys only what sits above it, are different tests; agreement between them is evidence and disagreement is informative. Two shuffles of the same kind tell you the variance of your estimator and nothing else.

A positive control is not optional. A null says what the statistic looks like when the structure is absent. A positive control — a synthetic dataset with a planted signal of known size, pushed through the identical pipeline — says what it looks like when the structure is present. Without one, a non-rejection is ambiguous between "no effect" and "no power", and those are not the same finding. The pattern to look for is the pair: null not rejected and positive control also not rejected. That study is uninformative, not negative, and reporting it as negative would be a mistake.

From the rubric, as a model of pre-registration. curriculum/LESSON-RUBRIC.md fixes in advance what would count as failure, at four ordered gates. Gate 0 is "model-agnostic, free, run before a human looks. Any FAIL ⇒ reject, do not score further." Gate 2 is the correctness gate: "The one gate no script can run and the whole batch leans on. Any single defect here fails the lesson outright, whatever Gates 1/3 look like." — MathUni curriculum/LESSON-RUBRIC.md, Gate 0 and Gate 2

That document is worth reading as an example of the artifact this unit is asking you to produce, in a different domain. It states the checks before the work exists, orders them, says which are mechanical and which require judgement, and — the part most pre-registrations omit — says explicitly what passing the cheap gate does not establish: "Gate 0 proves form and mention, never truth." A falsifiability plan that is only a threshold on a p-value is a Gate 0. Most plans need a Gate 2 row as well: a named human check that the statistic's hypotheses hold on this data.

What tdlbook.org supplies, and what it does not. This unit's resources name tdlbook.org, and resources/RESOURCES.md records it as the "Topological Deep Learning book … Reserve for Semester 4 / capstone alongside the TDL project". It is Topological Deep Learning: Going Beyond Graph Data — combinatorial complexes and combinatorial complex neural networks, higher-order message passing, pooling and unpooling, applications to mesh segmentation and classification, with software in TopoNetX, TopoEmbedX and TopoModelX. It contains no persistent homology, no filtrations, no persistence diagrams and no stability theorems; no guidance on scoping or designing a study; and no null models or hypothesis testing. Mapper appears — §7.4 "Mapper and the CC-pooling operation", §9.4 "Pooling with mapper on graphs and data classification", and Appendix E — but as a pooling operation inside a neural architecture, not as a topological summary of data in tda2-07's sense. So the book supports the framing that data need not be a graph, and offers one genuine point of contact with tda2-07; it supports none of this unit's methodology, which comes from the theorems cited above and from the argument here. Note the name collision that makes this easy to get wrong: "TDL" in cap-02's title is the TDL project of MISSION.md, a persistent-homology pipeline, not Topological Deep Learning.

You try (solo). Take the question a colleague proposes: "Does topological data analysis reveal structure in the trajectory data that clustering misses?" (i) Name every respect in which it is not yet scoped — there are at least four. (ii) Rewrite it as a claim with a statistic, a reference class, a dimension and a threshold. (iii) Suppose your sampling accuracy is ε = 0.4 in the embedding's metric. State the persistence threshold and say what you will do about features just below it. (iv) Design two nulls that destroy different structure, name the positive control, and write the decision rule. (v) Finally, list three things your rewritten claim cannot settle, each with its reason — and check that at least one of the reasons is a hypothesis of a theorem rather than a limitation of the data.

Hint: for (v), the reach and the weak feature size are the obvious candidates, and "the design compares against surrogates, and surrogates are not interventions" is the third.

Self-check 5. A study reports: null not rejected, positive control also not rejected. What has been learned?

This is exactly the pair the positive control exists to detect. A test that cannot find a planted signal of known size tells you nothing when it fails to find an unplanted one, so reporting "no effect" would be a misreading. The third option inverts the logic: a positive control that fails is evidence about the pipeline, and diagnosing whether the fault is the control's design or the pipeline's power is the next task, not a reason to discard the observation.

Self-check 6. Which of these is the best evidence that a question was scoped rather than rationalised?

The operational test in the mission strip is about the design, and the only durable evidence that a design preceded a result is a dated record of it — the difference between a plan and a summary is not visible from the document unless the document says so. Confidence intervals are a property of the reporting, not of the design. Sweeping every parameter is excellent practice and still leaves the claim unfixed: a sweep with no pre-registered decision rule lets the analyst choose the sweep point that worked.
