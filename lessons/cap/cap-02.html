The TDL pipeline end to end (cap-02)

Raw data in, defensible topological claim out — with every parameter choice on the record. There are about a dozen of them, and the data fixes none.

lab-09 let you run an analysis. This unit makes you write down what you ran. The exercise is duller than it sounds for about ten minutes, and then it stops being dull, because the ledger has a column for "which theorem constrains this" and a striking number of rows come back empty.

The rows that are not empty are the interesting ones. Each carries a constant — log 2 here, log(1/(1−ε)) there, 1/(2πσ) at the end — and the obvious next question is what they add up to. That question has a definite answer, and it is not a number.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Sampling error is bounded by Theorem 6.3, sparsification error by Theorem 6.4, vectorisation error by Theorem 13.2. Predict what you get by chaining the three.

Theorem 6.3 bounds a bottleneck distance additively on the linear scale; Theorem 6.4(a) bounds a bottleneck distance multiplicatively at log scale; Theorem 13.2 needs a 1-Wasserstein distance as its input. Adding an increment on the linear scale to one on the log scale is adding a length to a ratio, and feeding a bottleneck bound into a theorem that wants a Wasserstein bound requires an inequality this curriculum has not proved and which in general runs the other way. Segment 3 does the analysis properly, and finds one junction that can be repaired by changing a single choice in the ledger.

Why this matters for the mission: Running the full pipeline forces the choices lab-09 could leave implicit: metric, filtration, coefficient field, sparsification tolerance (tda2-08), vectorisation and its stability constant (tda2-09). Each one is a place the conclusion can quietly be lost.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The ledger, the metric, the filtration

Write the pipeline as a chain of objects, not a chain of verbs. Raw observations become a point set; a point set plus a metric becomes a finite metric space; that becomes a filtered complex; that becomes a diagram; that becomes a vector; that becomes a number compared against a reference. Eight stages, and each introduces parameters:

Count the "none" rows. The encoding, the embedding and the whole statistical stage are bounded by nothing this curriculum has proved — which does not make them wrong, but does make the conclusion conditional on them in a way no stability result softens. That is the ledger's payload, and it is available before any computation runs.

Definition 2.9 (Čech complex). For a metric space (M, d) and finite P, ℂr(P) contains σ = {p0,…,pk} whenever ⋂i B(pi, r) ≠ ∅. — Dey & Wang §2.2, Definition 2.9, p. 32

Definition 2.10 (Vietoris–Rips complex). Let (P, d) be a finite metric space. Given a real r > 0, the Vietoris–Rips complex is the abstract simplicial complex 𝕍ℝr(P) where σ ∈ 𝕍ℝr(P) if and only if d(p, q) ≤ 2r for every pair of vertices of σ. — Dey & Wang §2.2, Definition 2.10, p. 32

The Čech complex is the nerve of the union of balls; the Rips complex is determined by its 1-skeleton, which is why it is the one everybody computes. What that convenience costs is a theorem.

Corollary 6.1. For a fixed r ≥ 0, if the metric ball BZ(x, r) is convex for every x ∈ P, then ℂr(P) is homotopy equivalent to Pr, and thus Hk(ℂr(P)) ≅ Hk(Pr) for any k ≥ 0. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Corollary 6.1, p. 180

Proposition 6.2. If B(x, r) is convex for every x ∈ P and all r ≥ 0, then the persistence module Hk𝒫 is isomorphic to Hk𝒞(P), and Dgmk𝒫 = Dgmk𝒞(P) for any k ≥ 0. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Proposition 6.2, p. 180

And the comparison between the two filtrations: they are multiplicatively 2-interleaved, so at log scale

db(Dgmlog𝒞(P), Dgmlogℛ(P)) ≤ log 2 ≈ 0.693.

That number belongs in the ledger beside the choice. Whether it is cheap depends entirely on the persistence of the features you intend to claim — which cap-01 already made you write down.

Definition 6.2 (Hausdorff distance). For compact A, B ⊆ (Z, dZ), dH(A,B) = max{maxa∈A dZ(a,B), maxb∈B dZ(b,A)}. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.2, p. 181

Definition 6.3 (Gromov–Hausdorff distance). For metric spaces (X, dX) and (Y, dY), a correspondence C ⊆ X × Y meets every x and every y; its distortion is ½ sup(x,y),(x′,y′)∈C|dX(x,x′) − dY(y,y′)|; and dGH(X,Y) is the infimum of distortions. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.3, p. 182

Theorem 6.3. (a) For finite P, Q ⊆ (Z, dZ): dCech(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q) and dRips(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q). (b) For finite metric spaces (P, dP) and (Q, dQ): dCech(P,Q) ≤ 2dGH and dRips(P,Q) ≤ dGH. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Theorem 6.3, p. 182, with Definition 6.1's diagram distances, p. 181

Which part applies is a fact about your design, not your data. If your surrogates are generated in the same coordinate system as the observation, part (a) applies. If they are embedded independently — a separate UMAP fit, say — the two point sets are not in a common ambient space, part (b) applies, and the Čech bound acquires a factor 2 that Dey and Wang trace to the change in metric balls. A pipeline that runs its embedding separately per surrogate and then quotes part (a) has quoted the wrong theorem.

Self-check 1. Your surrogates are each embedded by a separate fit of the dimension-reduction method. Which part of Theorem 6.3 applies?

Part (a) is stated for finite P, Q ⊆ (Z, dZ) — a common metric space. Separate fits produce separate coordinate systems, so there is no such Z, and the applicable statement is part (b) in terms of dGH. Having the same underlying data is not the same as sharing an embedding. And Theorem 6.3 assumes nothing about cardinality; the diagram distances of Definition 6.1 are bottleneck distances, which handle unequal counts by matching to the diagonal.

Self-check 2. What does Proposition 6.2 add to Corollary 6.1, and what does it charge for it?

One isomorphism per radius is not a statement about persistence, which is as much about the maps between scales as about the spaces; Proposition 6.2 asserts the isomorphism of modules and concludes Dgmk𝒫 = Dgmk𝒞(P). Its hypothesis is strictly stronger, not weaker. And it says nothing about Rips — the Rips comparison is the separate multiplicative 2-interleaving with its log 2 bound.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The field, and the tolerance

Two stages whose parameters look like implementation detail and are not.

Why the coefficients must be a field

Ghrist states it flatly, in the outline of the circular-coordinates method at2-08 studied: "For the Structure Theorem (Theorem 5.21), field coefficients are required; for numerical reasons (to avoid roundoff errors), coefficients in a finite field 𝔽p are preferred." Two separate claims. The first is existential: without a field the module need not decompose into intervals, so there is no barcode to report — the output object does not exist. The second is numerical: exact arithmetic in 𝔽p removes a class of error that floating-point rank computation introduces.

The interesting part is what happens if your claim needs to leave 𝔽p. The short exact sequence of coefficients

0 ⟶ ℤ —·p→ ℤ ⟶ 𝔽p ⟶ 0

yields a long exact sequence on cohomology, and the map at issue is ·p : H2(X;ℤ) → H2(X;ℤ), whose kernel is the p-torsional classes. Ghrist writes that "for p > 2 these would seem to be rare occurrences in organic spaces X living behind data sets". By exactness ker(·p) = im δ, and assuming this is zero, the map H1(X;ℤ) → H1(X;𝔽p) is surjective, so persistent 𝔽p-classes lift to integral ones.

Read the hedge. "Would seem to be rare occurrences in organic spaces" is a judgement about where the data came from, not a theorem — and X is the hidden space, so no amount of sampling settles it. This is the same shape as cap-01's reach and weak feature size: an unverifiable hypothesis that must appear in the claim rather than in a footnote. If your analysis never lifts to integral coefficients, you do not need the assumption, and the coefficient note should say so. Half the value of writing these notes is discovering which assumptions you were not actually using.

The sparsification tolerance

Definition 6.4 (nets; net-tower). For finite P ⊂ (ℝd, d), Q ⊆ P is a (γ,γ′)-net if it is a γ-sample (covering: d(p,Q) ≤ γ for every p) and γ′-sparse (packing: d(q,q′) ≥ γ′ for distinct q,q′). A net-tower is a nested family {Nγ} with Nγ a (γ, γ/c)-net for a fixed c. — Dey & Wang §6.2.1, Definition 6.4, pp. 183–184

Definition 6.5 (sparse Rips). With weights wp(α) from the exit-times and d̂α(p,q) = d(p,q) + wp(α) + wq(α), the open sparse Rips complex is Qα = {σ ⊆ Nε(1−ε)α | d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α}; the cumulative complex is 𝕊α = ⋃β≤αQ̄β and the ε-sparse Rips filtration is {𝕊α}. — Dey & Wang §6.2.1, Definition 6.5, pp. 185–186

Theorem 6.4. For P ⊂ ℝd with |P| = n, d constant and fixed 0 < ε < 1/3: (a) 𝕊(P) and ℛ(P) are multiplicatively 1/(1−ε)-interleaved, so Dgmk𝕊(P) is a log(1/(1−ε))-approximation of Dgmkℛ(P) at log scale; (b) the total number of k-simplices ever appearing in 𝕊(P) is Θ((1/ε)kdn). — Dey & Wang §6.2.1, Theorem 6.4, p. 186

Worked example: putting a number on the tolerance. Take n = 105 points in ℝ3, ε = 0.1, and count triangles (k = 2).

The error. Theorem 6.4(a): log(1/(1 − 0.1)) = log(1/0.9) ≈ 0.105 at log scale. That is the whole of what sparsification costs the diagram, and it is a number you chose.

The size. Theorem 6.4(b): Θ((1/ε)kdn) = Θ(106 · 105) = Θ(1011) triangles. Compare the exact filtration: once the scale passes the diameter of P, every subset spans a simplex and the d-skeleton has Θ(nd+1) = Θ(1020) simplices. Nine orders of magnitude saved — and still 1011.

The reading. "Linear in n" is exactly true and hides a constant exponential in kd. What sparsification buys is the removal of the nd+1 growth, not smallness. And the two quantities move in opposite directions: as ε → 0 the error log(1/(1−ε)) → 0 while the constant (1/ε)kd → ∞. No ε makes both small.

The alternative. Definition 6.6's weak interleaving of vector space towers — where the maps exist and the diagrams commute only at discrete indices — supports the simpler simplicial-tower sparsification of Theorem 6.8, at 3log(1+ε) with O((1/ε)O(kd)n) simplices. For ε = 0.1 that is 3log(1.1) ≈ 0.286 against 0.105: about 2.7 times worse, and the factor 3 is exactly the price of weak rather than strong interleaving. Conceptually simpler, provably worse — and the ledger should record which you chose and why.

Self-check 3. Why can you not simply take ε very small and stop worrying about the sparsification error?

Both quantities in Theorem 6.4 depend on ε and they oppose each other, which is what makes the choice a genuine decision rather than a formality. The hypothesis is ε < 1/3, not >, and it is used in the packing argument for part (b) to absorb the factor (1 − ε). And log(1/(1−ε)) does tend to 0 — that is precisely why the temptation exists.

Self-check 4. Your analysis reports 𝔽2-barcodes and never lifts to ℤ. What do you owe the reader about torsion?

The p-torsion condition enters only when converting an 𝔽p-class to an integral one, so an analysis that stays in 𝔽2 does not use it — and discovering that is one of the reasons to write the note. What is still owed is the field requirement itself, which is existential: without a field there is no barcode. The third option treats as detail the one choice without which the output object does not exist.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Vectorisation, and the budget that does not add up

Definition 13.2 (positive, negative semidefinite kernel). k : X × X → ℝ is positive semidefinite if symmetric and Σi,jaiajk(xi,xj) ≥ 0 for any n, any x1,…,xn and any a1,…,an with Σai = 0; negative semidefinite with the inequality reversed. — Dey & Wang §13.1.2, Definition 13.2, p. 393

Four vectorisations, four stability statements — and the differences between them are the whole content of this segment.

Theorem 13.1. For persistence diagrams D and D′, Λ∞(D, D′) ≤ dB(D, D′). — Dey & Wang §13.1.1, Theorem 13.1, p. 393 — the landscape, stable against the bottleneck distance, constant 1

Definition 13.3 and Theorem 13.2. The PSSK feature map Φσ(D)(x) = (1/4πσ)Σy∈D[e−‖x−y‖²/4σ − e−‖x−ȳ‖²/4σ] satisfies ‖Φσ(D) − Φσ(E)‖𝓛²(Ω) ≤ (1/2πσ) dW,1(D, E). — Dey & Wang §13.1.2, p. 394 — stable against the 1-Wasserstein distance, constant 1/(2πσ)

Definition 13.4 and Theorem 13.3. With persistence surface μD(z) = Σu∈T(D)ω(u)φu(z) and pixel integrals ID[𝗉] = ∫𝗉μD, the persistence images satisfy ‖ID − IE‖1 ≤ (√5|∇ω| + √(10/π)‖ω‖∞/σ)·dW,1(D, E). — Dey & Wang §13.1.3, p. 395 — 1-Wasserstein, constant in ω and σ

Definition 13.5 (persistence weighted kernel). For a C0-universal kernel k on Ω and strictly positive ω, Ψk,ω(D) = Σx∈Dω(x)k(·,x) induces Kk,ω(D,E) = Σx∈D;y∈Eω(x)ω(y)k(x,y). — Dey & Wang §13.1.4, Definition 13.5, p. 396

And the two gaps tda2-09 established. For the persistence weighted Gaussian kernel, §13.1.4 says stability results with respect to both dB and dW,1 exist in the cited work and then explicitly omits them — no constant is available here. For the persistence Fisher kernel, §13.1.6 proves positive definiteness (Corollary 13.8) and states no stability result at all. Positive definiteness says the object is a legitimate kernel; it says nothing about robustness to perturbing the diagram. A pipeline choosing either has an unquantified vectorisation stage, and the ledger should say so rather than leaving the row blank.

Click to reveal model answer

(1) The three bounds.

Sampling. Theorem 6.3(a) with Definition 6.1: dRips(P, X) ≤ dH(P, X) ≤ εsamp, where dRips is the maximum over k of the bottleneck distances between the k-th Rips diagrams. Additive, on the linear scale.

Sparsification. Theorem 6.4(a): 𝕊(P) and ℛ(P) are multiplicatively 1/(1−εsp)-interleaved, giving db(Dgmlog𝕊, Dgmlogℛ) ≤ log(1/(1−εsp)). Bottleneck — but multiplicative, and stated at log scale.

Vectorisation. Theorem 13.2: ‖Φσ(D) − Φσ(E)‖ ≤ (1/2πσ)·dW,1(D, E). Additive on the linear scale, and stated against the 1-Wasserstein distance.

(2) The junctions.

Junction 1: sampling → sparsification. Both bound bottleneck distances, so the metric matches. What does not match is the axis. εsamp is an increment in the scale parameter; log(1/(1−εsp)) is an increment in the logarithm of the scale parameter, equivalently a multiplicative factor 1/(1−εsp). Adding them is adding a length to a ratio. One may of course convert, but only by fixing where on the scale axis the features sit — a feature at scale s suffers a multiplicative perturbation of absolute size s·(1/(1−εsp) − 1), which depends on s. So there is no single number: the sparsification error, expressed additively, is different for different features, and any composite must be stated per feature or per scale band.

Junction 2: diagrams → vectorisation. Here the mismatch is the metric itself. Theorem 13.2 requires a bound on dW,1; everything upstream supplies a bound on dB. Nothing in the sections this curriculum has read supplies an inequality bounding dW,1 above by dB — and one should not expect one, since by Definition 13.8 a Wasserstein distance is built from a sum over matched pairs while the bottleneck distance is a maximum over them, so the general relation runs the other way. The upstream bound therefore does not feed the downstream theorem at all. The composite is not loose; it is unavailable.

(3) What may be reported, and the repair. Three true statements, each in its own metric between its own pair of objects: the sampled diagram is within εsamp of the true diagram in bottleneck distance; the sparse diagram is within log(1/(1−εsp)) of the exact one in log-scale bottleneck distance; and the feature vector moves by at most (1/2πσ) times the 1-Wasserstein distance between whatever two diagrams are actually compared. What may not be reported is a single end-to-end error, because no theorem in the curriculum joins them.

The repair for Junction 2 is one line of the ledger: choose a vectorisation whose stability is stated against the bottleneck distance. Theorem 13.1 gives exactly that — Λ∞(D, D′) ≤ dB(D, D′), with constant 1 — so the landscape accepts precisely the bound the upstream stages produce. Junction 1 has no such repair; the linear-versus-log mismatch is intrinsic to the fact that Theorem 6.4's guarantee is multiplicative, and the honest treatment is to report it as a factor rather than convert it to a length.

What this derivation does and does not license. It licenses reporting each stage's bound and refusing to sum them, and it identifies a design change that makes one junction compose. It does not show that no end-to-end bound exists — a bound relating dW,1 to dB under extra hypotheses, such as a cap on the number of off-diagonal points, would change the picture, and this curriculum simply has not proved one. The correct claim is about what you can currently justify, not about what is true.

Self-check 5. Why does swapping the PSSK for a persistence landscape close junction 2?

The junction fails because Theorem 13.2 needs a dW,1 bound and the upstream supplies a dB bound; the fix is a downstream theorem stated against dB, which is Theorem 13.1. Losslessness is a separate and genuine virtue of landscapes but has nothing to do with metric compatibility. And a constant of 1 is convenient rather than decisive — a constant of 17 against the bottleneck distance would close the junction just as well.

Self-check 6. A row of the ledger has "none" in the theorem column. What follows?

An empty theorem column is information, not a verdict: it marks a place where nothing you have proved bounds the effect of the choice, which is precisely why it must be recorded and, where affordable, swept. Treating it as unimportant inverts the meaning — the unbounded rows are the ones most in need of attention. And declaring the analysis invalid would rule out most applied work, including every pipeline whose encoding and embedding are chosen rather than derived.
