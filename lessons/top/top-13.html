Quotient constructions: cones, suspension, CW complexes (top-13)

Cone a space to a point, suspend it, or build it cell by cell — the Lego kit of algebraic topology. Every piece of the kit is one construction wearing different clothes: take a product with an interval, or a disjoint union, and collapse something. Cone, suspension, join, wedge, smash, and the attaching step of a cell complex are six instances of top-12's §22, and once you see that, the only thing left to check in each case is which subspace gets collapsed.

Predict before we start. The cone is CX = (X × I)/(X × {0}) and the suspension collapses both ends of X × I. Predict how many non-singleton classes each partition has.

One and two. Everything with 0 < t < 1 is left alone — those points are singleton classes — and the collapsing happens only at the ends. That is the whole content of the definitions, and it is why the two constructions differ by exactly one class.

Why this matters for the mission: Finite simplicial and CW complexes are the spaces whose homology a computer can compute — the on-ramp to at1 and the lab.

Three of the strip's four nouns are absent from the pages this unit reads, and the fourth is the interesting one.

Searching Munkres §22 and Hatcher's "Cell Complexes" and "Operations on Spaces" (printed 5–10): simplicial complexes — not present; Hatcher introduces them with simplicial homology in Chapter 2, which is at1-07's. Homology — not present. An algorithm, or any notion of computation — not present; that is lab-02 and lab-03. So "the spaces whose homology a computer can compute" is a forward promise about three later modules, and this unit supplies only the spaces.

But "finite" is present, and it earns its place. Hatcher, printed 8, on products of cell complexes: "The topology on X × Y as a cell complex is sometimes finer than the product topology, with more open sets than the product topology has, though the two topologies coincide if either X or Y has only finitely many cells, or if both X and Y have countably many cells." So the word "finite" in the strip is not a convenience of implementation — it is a hypothesis under which the cell structure and the product topology agree. That is a real content-bearing clause, and it is the one part of the strip these pages support.

And Hatcher defers the rest. He writes on printed 5 that the reader "who wonders about various point-set topological questions lurking in the background of the following discussion should consult the Appendix for details", and that even the explanation of the letters "CW" is given there. This unit does not read the Appendix and asserts nothing from it.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cone and suspension: two partitions of X × I

Definitions (Hatcher, printed 8). For a space X, the suspension SX is the quotient of X × I obtained by collapsing X × {0} to one point and X × {1} to another point. One can regard SX as a double cone on X, the union of two copies of the cone CX = (X × I)/(X × {0}).
Worked example — the two partitions, written out. Both constructions are top-12's machinery with a different subspace chosen.

Cone. Partition X × I into
• the singletons {(x,t)} for x ∈ X and 0 < t ≤ 1, and
• the single class X × {0}.
Every point is in exactly one class and the classes are disjoint, so this is a partition; there is exactly one non-singleton class. Give the set of classes the quotient topology of §22 and you have CX.

Suspension. Same, but with two big classes: X × {0} and X × {1}, and singletons for 0 < t < 1. Exactly two non-singleton classes.

Saturated open sets. By top-12's description, an open set of the quotient is a collection of classes whose union is open in X × I. So a saturated open set of CX containing the cone point must contain all of X × {0} — and, being open in the product, must therefore contain X × [0, ε) for some ε in the case where X is compact. That is what makes the cone point's neighbourhoods large.

The double cone, made precise. Hatcher's remark is a statement about subspaces: the images under the quotient map of X × [0, 1/2] and X × [1/2, 1] are each homeomorphic to CX, their union is SX, and they meet in the image of X × {1/2}, a copy of X. Note that this is a claim about subspaces of SX, not a new construction.

Self-check 1. A saturated open set of X × I containing the cone class must contain —

All of X × {0}, because saturation means containing every class it meets and X × {0} is a single class. That is exactly Munkres's description on printed 139 — "the typical open set of X* is a collection of equivalence classes whose union is an open set of X" — applied here.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The universal property, and suspending a map

Theorem 22.2 (Munkres, printed 142). Let p : X → Y be a quotient map. Let Z be a space and let g : X → Z be a map that is constant on each set p⁻¹({y}), for y ∈ Y. Then g induces a map f : Y → Z such that f ∘ p = g. The induced map f is continuous if and only if g is continuous; f is a quotient map if and only if g is a quotient map.

at1-01 and at1-02, both already shipped, cite this unit for the property in the form "a map out of a quotient is continuous exactly when its composite with the quotient map is". That is Theorem 22.2's continuity clause, no more and no less — it says nothing about f being injective, surjective or open. It lives in §22, which is the resource of top-12 and of this unit alike; top-12 proves it and this unit uses it.
Worked example — suspending a map, which is the property's first real use. Hatcher, printed 8: "a map f : X → Y suspends to Sf : SX → SY, the quotient map of f × 𝟙 : X × I → Y × I." Unpacked, that is an application of Theorem 22.2.

Write p_X : X × I → SX and p_Y : Y × I → SY for the quotient maps, and set g = p_Y ∘ (f × 𝟙) : X × I → SY. It is continuous, being a composite of continuous maps.

g is constant on the fibres of p_X. There are three kinds.
• On a singleton {(x,t)} with 0 < t < 1, any map is constant.
• On X × {0}: (f × 𝟙)(x, 0) = (f(x), 0) for every x, and every point of Y × {0} is sent by p_Y to the same point — the first suspension point of SY. So g is constant there.
• On X × {1}: identically, landing on the second suspension point of SY.

By Theorem 22.2, g induces a map Sf : SX → SY with Sf ∘ p_X = g, and Sf is continuous because g is. ∎

What you did not have to do. You never described a single open set of SX. The whole point of the universal property is that maps out of a quotient are settled upstairs, in the space you started with, where you can see everything.
Guided proof. Prove that S(g ∘ f) = Sg ∘ Sf and S(𝟙_X) = 𝟙_{SX}.

Both follow from one observation about maps that agree after composing with a surjection. State that observation first, then apply it twice.
Model answer The observation. If p : A → B is surjective and u, v : B → C satisfy u ∘ p = v ∘ p, then u = v. Indeed, given b ∈ B choose a with p(a) = b; then u(b) = u(p(a)) = v(p(a)) = v(b).

Identities. S(𝟙_X) is by definition the map induced by p_X ∘ (𝟙_X × 𝟙) = p_X. And 𝟙_{SX} also satisfies 𝟙_{SX} ∘ p_X = p_X. Since p_X is surjective — every quotient map is — the observation gives S(𝟙_X) = 𝟙_{SX}.

Composites. Let f : X → Y and g : Y → Z. By construction Sf ∘ p_X = p_Y ∘ (f × 𝟙) and Sg ∘ p_Y = p_Z ∘ (g × 𝟙). Hence
(Sg ∘ Sf) ∘ p_X = Sg ∘ (p_Y ∘ (f × 𝟙)) = (Sg ∘ p_Y) ∘ (f × 𝟙) = p_Z ∘ (g × 𝟙) ∘ (f × 𝟙) = p_Z ∘ ((g ∘ f) × 𝟙).
But S(g ∘ f) is defined by exactly the equation S(g ∘ f) ∘ p_X = p_Z ∘ ((g ∘ f) × 𝟙). By the observation, with p_X surjective, S(g ∘ f) = Sg ∘ Sf. ∎

What this is and is not. Preserving identities and composites is what a functor does, and cat-01 has the vocabulary. But §22 gives no category here, and this lesson claims none — only the two equations, proved from surjectivity of the quotient map.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Cell complexes, and where the topology is chosen rather than found

Construction (Hatcher, printed 5). A cell complex is built inductively: start with a discrete set X⁰, whose points are 0-cells; form the n-skeleton Xⁿ from Xn−1 by attaching n-cells enα via maps φ_α : Sn−1 → Xn−1; and either stop at a finite stage or take X = ⋃_n Xⁿ with the weak topology — a set A ⊂ X is open if and only if A ∩ Xⁿ is open in Xⁿ for each n.

The attaching step is a quotient, and saying so makes the universal property available. Xⁿ is the quotient of Xn−1 ⨿ (⨿_α Dnα) by the partition whose class at y ∈ Xn−1 is {y} together with every boundary point mapping to y, and whose other classes are the singletons of the open cells. Theorem 22.2 then says: a map out of Xⁿ is continuous exactly when its restrictions to Xn−1 and to each disc are continuous and agree on the boundaries. That is the sentence at1-01 and at1-02 use, in the place they use it.

Theorem 22.1 has a use here too, but the saturated subspace to take is not the obvious one. Xn−1 by itself is not saturated: it meets the class of y ∈ Xn−1 without containing the boundary points φα⁻¹(y) that belong to that class. What is saturated — and closed — is Xn−1 ⨿ (⨿α ∂Dnα), since every class either lies wholly inside it or is a singleton in an open cell. So Theorem 22.1(1) applies to that subspace, and the restriction of the attaching map to it is again a quotient map onto Xn−1. Separately, the disjoint union of the open cells is saturated and open, and its restriction is a quotient map too — which is the sense in which the cells are attached without disturbing what was already there.

The weak topology is a condition, not a consequence. X is being defined as a set with a topology chosen, and nothing about the skeleta forces that choice. Hatcher says as much implicitly by deferring: on printed 5 he tells the reader with point-set questions to consult the Appendix, and adds that the explanation of the letters "CW" is given there too.

The one place the mission strip's "finite" is earned. Hatcher, printed 8, on products: "The topology on X × Y as a cell complex is sometimes finer than the product topology, with more open sets than the product topology has, though the two topologies coincide if either X or Y has only finitely many cells, or if both X and Y have countably many cells."

Read that as a statement about two topologies on one set. The cell-complex topology on X × Y and the product topology of top-05 — whose basis Theorem 19.1 describes, with all but finitely many factors equal to the whole space — are both defined; they need not be equal; and finiteness of one factor is a sufficient condition for equality. So "finite CW complexes" is not shorthand for "small enough to compute with" — it is a hypothesis under which two constructions that could disagree do not.

He adds "In practice this subtle issue of point-set topology rarely causes problems, however", which is a judgement about practice and not a theorem, and this lesson records it as his.

Self-check 4. The weak topology on X = ⋃Xⁿ is —

A condition. Nothing about the skeleta determines a topology on their union; Hatcher declares that A is open iff A ∩ Xⁿ is open for every n. The fourth option is the near-miss worth naming: each individual attaching step is a quotient, but the passage to the infinite union is a separate choice.

Self-check 5. Which of the six constructions in this unit is not of the form "collapse one subspace to one point"?

The join. At t = 0 it collapses each {x} × Y × {0} to a point, one class for each x ∈ X, and at t = 1 each X × {y} × {1}. That is two whole families of classes, not one subspace. The suspension is the borderline case — two subspaces, each to its own point — and the cone, wedge and smash each collapse a single subspace.

Check against this 1. Every construction in the kit is one move. Take a product with I, or a disjoint union, and collapse. Cone: one non-singleton class, X × {0}. Suspension: two. Join: two whole families, one at each end of I. Wedge and smash: one each. The only thing to check in a new case is which subspace is collapsed, and top-12's §22 supplies the topology.

2. Theorem 22.2 is what makes the kit usable. Maps out of a quotient are settled in the space upstairs, so suspending a map needs no description of a single open set of SX — only the observation that p_Y ∘ (f × 𝟙) is constant on the fibres of p_X. Functoriality then follows from surjectivity of the quotient map alone, and this is the property at1-01 and at1-02 were promised.

3. Cell complexes are quotients at each stage and a choice at the limit. The attaching step is a quotient of a disjoint union, so a map out of Xⁿ is continuous exactly when its restrictions are and they agree on boundaries. The weak topology on the union is imposed, not derived. And Hatcher's warning on printed 8 — that the cell-complex topology on a product can be strictly finer than the product topology, unless one factor is finite — is the one place the mission strip's word "finite" is doing mathematical work.
