Limit-point and local compactness (top-09)

Three flavours of compactness — and when the neighbourhood, not the whole space, is what behaves finitely. The three flavours are the interesting part, because they are genuinely three: covering compactness, limit point compactness and sequential compactness are different properties, with Munkres giving explicit spaces that have one and not another. They collapse into one thing only under a hypothesis, and Theorem 28.2 names it.

Predict before we start. "Compact", "every infinite subset has a limit point", and "every sequence has a convergent subsequence". Predict how many of these three are equivalent in a general topological space.

One implication survives: compact ⇒ limit point compact, which is the positive half of Theorem 28.1. The rest fail in general, and Munkres supplies the witnesses — ℤ₊ × Y with Y indiscrete is limit point compact and not compact (Example 1, printed 179). Theorem 28.2 says all three coincide if X is metrizable, and that hypothesis is doing all the work in the familiar cases.

Why this matters for the mission: Local compactness underwrites the well-behaved spaces (manifolds) that real data is sampled from.

Half of the strip's vocabulary is not defined in these sections.

§29 defines local compactness and proves a good deal about it. It defines no manifold. In Munkres the word first appears in the Chapter 4 introduction, printed 189, glossed in passing as "the higher-dimensional analogue of a surface", and manifolds are the subject of §36 "Imbeddings of Manifolds", printed 224 — neither of which this unit reads beyond that sentence.

So the strip asserts an implication — manifolds are locally compact, therefore local compactness underwrites them — of which §29 supplies exactly one term. The implication is not stated here, not proved here, and could not be, because one of its two notions is undefined in this chapter. What §29 does supply is everything about the term it does define, including Theorem 29.1's construction of a one-point compactification and Theorem 29.2's neighbourhood criterion.

And the strip cannot be applied to the sample. A point cloud is finite, hence discrete (top-06), hence compact (top-08), hence locally compact — trivially, since {x} is an open compact neighbourhood. That is now the third property in three units to come out vacuously true of a point cloud from finiteness alone. If local compactness underwrites anything for TDA, it does so for the space being sampled, not for the sample; §29 does not say so, and neither does this unit.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Three properties, and the hypothesis that fuses them

Definitions (Munkres, printed 178–179). A space X is said to be limit point compact if every infinite subset of X has a limit point.

If (x_n) is a sequence in X and n₁ < n₂ < ⋯ is an increasing sequence of positive integers, the sequence (y_i) with y_i = x_{n_i} is a subsequence. The space X is sequentially compact if every sequence of points of X has a convergent subsequence.
Theorem 28.1 (printed 179). Compactness implies limit point compactness, but not conversely.

Theorem 28.2 (printed 179). Let X be a metrizable space. Then the following are equivalent: (1) X is compact; (2) X is limit point compact; (3) X is sequentially compact.

The positive half of Theorem 28.1 is a contraposition worth having in hand. An infinite A ⊂ X with no limit point contains all its limit points vacuously, so it is closed by Corollary 17.7 of top-03; and each a ∈ A has a neighbourhood meeting A only in a. Those neighbourhoods together with X − A cover X, and each captures a single point of the infinite A, so no finite subfamily covers. Note also what Theorem 28.2 is not: it says nothing about non-metrizable spaces, and the sequence machinery it rests on — Lemma 21.2 of top-06 — carried the same hypothesis for the same reason.

Worked example — Munkres's Example 1, and why the counterexample is so cheap. Let Y = {y₁, y₂} with the indiscrete topology {∅, Y}, and let X = ℤ₊ × Y with the product topology.

X is limit point compact. A basis for X consists of the sets {n} × Y: ℤ₊ is discrete in the order topology (top-02, §14 Example 3), and Y has only the two open sets. Now take any non-empty A ⊂ X and pick (n, y₁) ∈ A. Every open set containing (n, y₂) contains a basis element about it, hence contains all of {n} × Y, hence contains (n, y₁) — a point of A different from (n, y₂). So (n, y₂) is a limit point of A. Indeed every non-empty subset has a limit point, not merely every infinite one.

X is not compact. The sets U_n = {n} × Y form an open covering, and they are pairwise disjoint and non-empty, so no proper subcollection covers X — in particular no finite one.

Why this costs so little. The two points of each fibre {n} × Y have exactly the same neighbourhoods. X fails the T₁ axiom, so limit points are almost free: each point of a pair is a limit point of the singleton containing the other. That is the general lesson of Example 1 — limit point compactness is weak precisely where points are not separated, which is why Theorem 28.2 can only recover the equivalence once a metric is present and the space is therefore Hausdorff.
Faded example — (2) ⇒ (3), and the hypothesis inside the hypothesis. Let X be metrizable and limit point compact, and let (x_n) be a sequence. Put A = {x_n : n ∈ ℤ₊}.

If A is finite, some value is taken infinitely often, and those indices give a constant subsequence, convergent trivially.

If A is infinite, it has a limit point x. Choose n₁ with x_{n₁} ∈ B(x,1). Given n_{i−1}, Munkres writes: "Because the ball B(x, 1/i) intersects A in infinitely many points, we can choose an index n_i > n_{i−1}…"

⟨your step: that sentence is a citation in disguise. Name the theorem of top-03 that upgrades "x is a limit point of A" to "every neighbourhood of x meets A in infinitely many points", state its hypothesis, and check that hypothesis against a metrizable X — you will need two steps, not one.⟩

⟨your step: finish the proof by showing (x_{n_i}) → x, and say what would break if the balls did not meet A infinitely often.⟩

Self-check 1. Which implication among the three properties holds with no hypothesis at all?

Compact ⇒ limit point compact, the positive half of Theorem 28.1. The second is refuted by ℤ₊ × Y. The third does hold for metrizable spaces by Theorem 28.2, but not in general, and Theorem 28.2 is where the hypothesis is stated. Nothing here is hypothesis-free except the first.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Local compactness: finiteness in the neighbourhood

Definition (Munkres, printed 182). A space X is said to be locally compact at x if there is some compact subspace C of X that contains a neighbourhood of x. If X is locally compact at each of its points, X is said simply to be locally compact.

Read the definition carefully: C must be compact and must contain a neighbourhood — C need not itself be a neighbourhood. That looseness is what makes ℝ locally compact by the one-line argument of Example 1: x lies in some (a,b), which lies in the compact [a,b].

Munkres's three examples are worth the contrast. ℝⁿ is locally compact, because a basis element lies in a product of closed intervals, compact by Corollary 27.2 and Theorem 26.7. ℝω is not, and his reason is worth quoting: "none of its basis elements are contained in compact subspaces". By top-05's Theorem 19.1 a product-topology basis element has U_n = ℝ for all but finitely many n, so it is unbounded in each of those coordinates; pushing a would-be compact superset through a projection — continuous by Theorem 19.6 — would give a compact, hence closed and bounded (Theorem 27.3), subset of ℝ containing all of ℝ. And every simply ordered set with the least upper bound property is locally compact, since a basis element lies in a closed interval, compact by Theorem 27.1. He also records, without proof, that ℚ is not locally compact: "as you can check."

Theorem 29.1 (printed 183). Let X be a space. Then X is locally compact Hausdorff if and only if there exists a space Y satisfying: (1) X is a subspace of Y; (2) the set Y − X consists of a single point; (3) Y is a compact Hausdorff space. If Y and Y′ are two spaces satisfying these conditions, then there is a homeomorphism of Y with Y′ that equals the identity map on X.

Theorem 29.2 (printed 185). Let X be a Hausdorff space. Then X is locally compact if and only if given x in X, and given a neighbourhood U of x, there is a neighbourhood V of x such that V̄ is compact and V̄ ⊂ U.

Corollary 29.3 (printed 185). Let X be locally compact Hausdorff, let A be a subspace of X. If A is closed in X or open in X, then A is locally compact.

Corollary 29.4 (printed 185). A space X is homeomorphic to an open subspace of a compact Hausdorff space if and only if X is locally compact Hausdorff.

The uniqueness clause of Theorem 29.1 is what licenses the definite article in "the one-point compactification", and Munkres's proof of it is a model of using top-08 economically. If U is open in Y and contains the extra point p, then C = Y − U is closed in the compact Y, hence compact (Theorem 26.2); C lies in X, so it is compact as a subspace of Y′; and Y′ being Hausdorff makes C closed there (Theorem 26.3). So h(U) = Y′ − C is open. Two theorems, applied once each, and the whole case is done. The other case — U not containing p — is Lemma 16.2 of top-02: U is open in X and X is open in Y′, so U is open in Y′.

Self-check 2. Why is ℝω not locally compact in the product topology?

Munkres's own reason, printed 182: none of its basis elements are contained in compact subspaces. A product-topology basis element leaves all but finitely many coordinates equal to ℝ, so it is unbounded in each of those. Note ℝω in the product topology is metrizable (top-06, Theorem 20.5) and is Hausdorff (Theorem 19.4), so the first and third options are false as statements, not merely irrelevant.

Self-check 3. Corollary 29.3 says a subspace of a locally compact Hausdorff space is locally compact when it is —

Closed or open. Arbitrary subspaces will not do: ℚ ⊂ ℝ is the standard counterexample, and Munkres flags it in Example 1 as something to check. Note "dense" is exactly the wrong guess — ℚ is dense in ℝ.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Compactifying, and what the sample cannot tell you

Definition (Munkres, printed 185). If Y is a compact Hausdorff space and X is a proper subspace of Y whose closure equals Y, then Y is said to be a compactification of X. If Y − X equals a single point, then Y is called the one-point compactification of X.

Munkres's Example 4 records that the one-point compactification of ℝ is homeomorphic to the circle, and of ℝ² to the sphere S², with ℂ ∪ {∞} known as the Riemann sphere — and adds "as you may readily check", leaving the verification to the reader. It is a good check to do with Theorem 26.6 in hand: build a continuous bijection from the compactification onto the circle and let compact-to-Hausdorff supply the inverse for free.

Guided proof. Show that a finite metric space is locally compact, and then say what the answer is worth.

Use only the definition of local compactness and the two facts already established: that a finite metric space is discrete (top-06), and that a finite space is compact (top-08).
Model answer Let X be a finite metric space and fix x ∈ X. By top-06's Problem 1(c) the metric topology on X is discrete, so {x} is open and is therefore a neighbourhood of x. By Munkres's §26 Example 3 every finite space is compact, so {x} — being a finite subspace — is compact. Thus {x} is a compact subspace of X containing a neighbourhood of x, namely {x} itself. Hence X is locally compact at x, and as x was arbitrary, X is locally compact. ∎

What it is worth. Nothing about the data. Every step used finiteness and no step used a distance. This is now the third consecutive verdict of that shape:
• top-06 — a point cloud's metric topology is discrete, whatever the distances;
• top-08 — a point cloud is compact, because every open covering of a finite space is finite;
• here — a point cloud is locally compact, because singletons are open and compact.

Three true statements, none of which distinguishes one point cloud from another of the same size. Any mission claim of the form "the data is X, so the theorems apply" has to be checked against this pattern: if the property follows from finiteness alone, it is carrying no information about the data, and the object the theorem needs must be something else — the space being sampled, or the filtration's parameter interval.
You try. Munkres asserts in Example 1, printed 182, that "the subspace ℚ of rational numbers is not locally compact, as you can check." Check it.

Fix q ∈ ℚ and suppose C ⊂ ℚ is compact and contains a neighbourhood of q, which you may take to be ℚ ∩ (a,b) with a < q < b. Now ℚ is metrizable, so Theorem 28.2 applies to C: compact and sequentially compact are the same thing there. Build a sequence in ℚ ∩ (a,b) converging in ℝ to an irrational, and ask what its subsequences can converge to in ℚ.

Then say which hypothesis of Corollary 29.3 ℚ fails as a subspace of ℝ — it is neither closed nor open — and note that this is the counterexample showing local compactness is not inherited by arbitrary subspaces.

Self-check 4. Is a point cloud locally compact?

Yes, and for the third time by an argument that never consults a distance. Note the third option is wrong even for infinite discrete spaces: ℤ is discrete and locally compact, since {n} is compact. What fails for infinite discrete spaces is compactness, not local compactness.

Check against this 1. The three compactness properties are three. Only compact ⇒ limit point compact holds in general (Theorem 28.1). ℤ₊ × Y with Y indiscrete is limit point compact and not compact, and it is cheap because the two points of each fibre have identical neighbourhoods — limit points are easy exactly where points are not separated.

2. Metrizability collapses them (Theorem 28.2), and the proof of (2) ⇒ (3) leans on Theorem 17.9, which needs T₁ — supplied in two steps, because a metric space is Hausdorff (top-06, §21) and a Hausdorff space is T₁ by Theorem 17.8. Hausdorffness itself survives products and subspaces by Theorem 17.11, which is why the hypothesis is cheap to carry. So the familiar equivalence of "compact" and "sequentially compact" is a theorem about metric spaces, not a definition.

3. Local compactness asks the neighbourhood, not the space, to behave. ℝ and ℝⁿ have it; ℝω and ℚ do not. Theorem 29.1 makes it exactly the condition for a one-point compactification to exist, with uniqueness proved by Theorems 26.2 and 26.3 applied once each; Theorem 29.2 gives the neighbourhood criterion; Corollary 29.3 restricts inheritance to closed and open subspaces. And a point cloud has all three compactness properties trivially, from finiteness — which is what the mission bound turns on.
