The countability axioms (top-10)

How small is your space's basis? Second-countability quietly powers half the metrization results. Quietly is right — §30 proves no metrization theorem at all, and the axiom's real work here is retrospective: it is the hypothesis top-06's proofs were actually using while claiming to need a metric. Munkres said so at the time, on printed 130, and the first theorem of §30 promotes that remark.

Predict before we start. top-06's sequence lemma held for metrizable spaces. Predict what its proof actually needed.

Only countability. Munkres flagged it on printed 130: "All we really needed was the countable collection B_d(x, 1/n) of balls about x." The metric produced that family; nothing else about it was used. Theorem 30.1 states the lemma with first countability as the hypothesis, and the proof is the same proof with B_d(x,1/n) replaced by B_n = U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_n.

Why this matters for the mission: Countability separates the pathological spaces from the ones data can actually sample.

A finite discrete space satisfies all four countability properties, and that is the fourth such verdict in a row.

Let X be a point cloud. By top-06 its metric topology is discrete, so the singletons form a basis — and there are finitely many of them, so X is second-countable, hence first-countable, and hence Lindelöf and separable by Theorem 30.3. All four, from finiteness, with no distance consulted.

Set that beside the running tally: top-06 found the point cloud discrete, top-08 found it compact, top-09 found it locally compact, and now all four countability axioms hold. Seven properties, seven verdicts, none of which distinguishes one point cloud from another of the same size. So countability does not separate anything about the data. If it separates the spaces "data can actually sample", it does so for the sampled space — and §30 says nothing about sampling.

The hook's "half" is not a claim this unit can assess. §30 proves no metrization theorem. Munkres states the goal in his Chapter 4 introduction, printed 189 — second countability plus regularity gives metrizability — and proves it in §34, printed 214. There is a second metrization theorem at §40, printed 248, whose title is all this unit has seen. Counting the fraction of metrization results that use second countability would need their statements, and we have not read them.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Two axioms, and the debt from §21 repaid

Definitions (Munkres, printed 190–191). A space X is said to have a countable basis at x if there is a countable collection ℬ of neighbourhoods of x such that each neighbourhood of x contains at least one of the elements of ℬ. A space that has a countable basis at each of its points is said to satisfy the first countability axiom, or to be first-countable.

If a space X has a countable basis for its topology, then X is said to satisfy the second countability axiom, or to be second-countable.

Second-countable implies first-countable, and the argument is one line: given a countable basis ℬ, the subcollection of members containing x is countable and is a countable basis at x. The converse fails, and Munkres gives two witnesses in this section alone.

Theorem 30.1 (printed 190). Let X be a topological space.
(a) Let A be a subset of X. If there is a sequence of points of A converging to x, then x ∈ Ā; the converse holds if X is first-countable.
(b) Let f : X → Y. If f is continuous, then for every convergent sequence x_n → x in X, the sequence f(x_n) converges to f(x). The converse holds if X is first-countable.

Put that beside top-06. Lemma 21.2 and Theorem 21.3 are word for word the same statements with "metrizable" where "first-countable" now stands. The proofs are also the same proof: replace the balls B_d(x, 1/n) by B_n = U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_n for a countable basis {U_n} at x, and everything goes through. The intersection is taken rather than using U_n directly because the U_n need not shrink, and the argument's last step — "x_i ∈ U for all i ≥ N" — needs a decreasing family.

Every metrizable space is first-countable, since {B_d(x, 1/n)} is a countable basis at x. The converse fails: S_Ω is first-countable, as Munkres records in §28 (printed 181, left to the reader), and is not metrizable, because it is limit point compact and not compact while top-09's Theorem 28.2 forbids that combination in a metrizable space.

Self-check 1. Which hypothesis does Theorem 30.1 use where top-06's Lemma 21.2 used metrizability?

First countability, and only that. Second countability would be more than needed — the argument is local, taking place entirely inside neighbourhoods of one point x. Munkres makes the point explicitly when he defines first countability in §21 rather than waiting for §30: the axiom is named at the moment the proof reveals what it wanted.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

What survives, what does not, and the table

Theorem 30.2 (printed 191). A subspace of a first-countable space is first-countable, and a countable product of first-countable spaces is first-countable. A subspace of a second-countable space is second-countable, and a countable product of second-countable spaces is second-countable.

Theorem 30.3 (printed 192). Suppose that X has a countable basis. Then: (a) every open covering of X contains a countable subcollection covering X; (b) there exists a countable subset of X that is dense in X.

Munkres names the two properties of Theorem 30.3 — a space with (a) is Lindelöf, one with (b) is separable, "an unfortunate choice of terminology", since it collides with the separation axioms of §31 and has nothing to do with them. His assessment is worth quoting because it is unusually flat: "We shall not use these properties to prove any theorems, but one of them — the Lindelöf condition — will be useful in dealing with some examples."

And they are badly behaved. Second countability passes to subspaces and to countable products, by Theorem 30.2. Lindelöf does neither: Munkres's Example 5 gives a Lindelöf space with a non-Lindelöf subspace (the ordered square, whose subspace I × (0,1) splits into uncountably many disjoint open pieces), and Example 4 gives a Lindelöf space whose square is not (ℝ_ℓ, and the Sorgenfrey plane ℝ_ℓ²). That contrast is the reason second countability is the axiom the book actually uses.

Theorem 30.2's two halves reuse machinery already in hand. The subspace clause is Lemma 16.1 of top-02 — intersecting a basis with a subspace gives a basis — plus the observation that the image of a countable set is countable. The product clause is top-05's Theorem 19.1: a product-topology basis element differs from the whole space in only finitely many coordinates, so it is determined by a finite subset of the index set together with one basis element from each of those factors, and a countable index set makes the totality of such data countable. And Example 5's opening claim that the ordered square is Lindelöf is top-08's Theorem 27.1 in disguise: the ordered square is a closed interval in a linear continuum, hence compact, hence Lindelöf, since a finite subcover is in particular a countable one.

Worked example — Example 2, and the uncountable discrete subspace. Munkres shows ℝω in the uniform topology is first-countable — it is metrizable, by the uniform metric ρ̄ of top-06 — and not second-countable. His route is a lemma worth extracting.

Lemma. If X has a countable basis ℬ, every discrete subspace A of X is countable. Proof: for each a ∈ A the set {a} is open in A, so {a} = U ∩ A for some U open in X; choose B_a ∈ ℬ with a ∈ B_a ⊂ U, so that B_a ∩ A = {a}. The map a ↦ B_a is injective — if B_a = B_{a'} then {a} = B_a ∩ A = {a'} — and lands in a countable set. ∎

The uncountable discrete subspace. Let A ⊂ ℝω be the set of sequences with every entry 0 or 1. Two distinct such sequences differ in some coordinate by exactly 1, so their uniform distance ρ̄ is exactly 1; hence the ρ̄-balls of radius 1/2 about them are disjoint and each meets A in one point. So A is discrete. And A is uncountable, being in bijection with the set of all subsets of ℤ₊.

By the lemma, ℝω in the uniform topology has no countable basis.

Note which topology this is. In the product topology ℝω is second-countable (Example 1). The two verdicts differ because the topologies differ — top-06's Theorem 20.4 places the uniform strictly between product and box for infinite index sets, and Theorem 20.5 metrizes the product one separately. Naming the topology is not pedantry here; it changes the answer.

Self-check 2. Is ℝω second-countable?

It depends on the topology, which is the whole point of Examples 1 and 2. Product: the products of rational-endpoint intervals with all but finitely many factors equal to ℝ form a countable basis. Uniform: the 0–1 sequences form an uncountable discrete subspace, which a countable basis forbids.

Self-check 3. Which of these passes to arbitrary subspaces?

Second countability, by Theorem 30.2 — intersect the basis with the subspace. Lindelöf does not: Munkres's Example 5 has the ordered square, compact and therefore Lindelöf, with the subspace I × (0,1) failing, because that subspace is the union of uncountably many disjoint non-empty open sets and no proper subcollection covers it.

Guided proof. Prove Theorem 30.3: a space with a countable basis is Lindelöf and separable.

Both parts. In the Lindelöf part, be careful that you choose one member of the cover per basis element used, not per point.
Model answer Let ℬ = {B_n}_{n ∈ ℤ₊} be a countable basis for X.

(a) Lindelöf. Let 𝒜 be an open covering of X. Let N be the set of indices n for which there exists some A ∈ 𝒜 with B_n ⊂ A; for each such n choose one such A and call it A_n. The collection {A_n : n ∈ N} is countable, being indexed by a subset of ℤ₊.
It covers X. Given x ∈ X, pick A ∈ 𝒜 with x ∈ A; by the definition of a basis there is n with x ∈ B_n ⊂ A, so n ∈ N, and x ∈ B_n ⊂ A_n. ∎

The care point. Choosing one A per point would give a family indexed by X, which is no smaller than what you started with. The countability comes from indexing the choice by the basis, and the basis is countable.

(b) Separable. Discard the empty members of ℬ and choose x_n ∈ B_n for each remaining n; let D be the set of chosen points, countable as the image of a subset of ℤ₊.
D is dense. Let x ∈ X and let B_n be any basis element containing x. Then B_n is non-empty, so it was not discarded, and x_n ∈ B_n ∩ D. So every basis element containing x meets D, and Theorem 17.5(b) of top-03 — the basis form of the closure criterion — gives x ∈ D̄. Hence D̄ = X. ∎

Why 17.5(b) and not 17.5(a). The hypothesis we have is about basis elements, not about all open sets; 17.5(a) would require checking every neighbourhood, and there is no countable supply of those. The basis form is what makes the countable choice sufficient.

Self-check 4. Why does Munkres call "separable" an unfortunate choice of terminology?

The collision is with the separation axioms — Hausdorff, regular, normal — which are §31's subject and have nothing to do with having a countable dense subset. The last option is wrong on the facts: separability is stated purely in terms of open sets and closures, so it is a topological property in the sense of top-04.

Self-check 5. A point cloud, as a topological space. Which countability properties does it have?

All four. Its topology is discrete (top-06), so the singletons are a basis; there are finitely many, so it is second-countable, hence first-countable and — by Theorem 30.3 — Lindelöf and separable. The third option is true of uncountable discrete spaces and false here. The fourth is the one to notice: the answer is independent of every distance, which is the fourth time in this module that a property of a point cloud has turned out to be settled by finiteness alone.

Check against this 1. Theorem 30.1 is a debt repaid. top-06's sequence lemma and sequential-continuity theorem were stated for metrizable spaces and proved using only a countable shrinking family of neighbourhoods. First countability is that hypothesis named, and the proof is unchanged once B_d(x,1/n) is replaced by B_n = U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_n — the intersection being needed because the U_n need not shrink on their own.

2. Second countability is the axiom that behaves. It passes to subspaces and countable products (Theorem 30.2) and implies both Lindelöf and separable (Theorem 30.3). Lindelöf passes to neither — the ordered square loses it on a subspace, ℝ_ℓ loses it on a square — which is why Munkres says he will not prove theorems with these two and uses them only on examples.
