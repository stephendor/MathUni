The product topology (top-05)

Infinitely many spaces, one product — and the obvious box topology is the wrong answer. Why? Munkres's own reply, on printed 115, is worth reading before you accept the question: "What is not so clear is why we prefer the product topology to the box topology. The answer will appear as we continue our study of topology." He then declines to settle it in this section. One result separates them here, and it is the one this unit is built round.

Predict before we start. Both candidate topologies on ∏X_α have basis elements that are products ∏U_α of open sets. They differ in one clause. Predict which of the two has more open sets.

The box topology. Its basis is every ∏U_α; the product topology's basis takes only those with U_α = X_α for all but finitely many α. Fewer basis elements, and by Lemma 13.3 (top-02) the topology with the smaller sets available is the finer one — so box ⊇ product. Munkres records both consequences on printed 115: for finite products the two are "precisely the same", and in general the box topology is finer.

Why this matters for the mission: Product spaces model multi-parameter data; this subtlety returns in multiparameter persistence.

The subtlety does not return in multiparameter persistence, and Theorem 19.1 is why.

The whole distinction between the box and product topologies is a statement about infinite index sets. Munkres's first consequence of Theorem 19.1, printed 115: "for finite products ∏nα=1 X_α the two topologies are precisely the same". A multiparameter persistence module is indexed by finitely many parameters — two or three in practice — so any product of that many factors is a finite product and the distinction collapses before it can be made. Nothing in §19's subtlety survives the crossing.

It would become relevant only under a change the subject does not make: an indexing by infinitely many parameters. If someone ever writes that down, §19 is the section to reread.

Two smaller bounds. There is no data anywhere in §15 or §19 — no sampling, no persistence, no modules. And the hook's "wrong answer" is not Munkres's verdict. The box topology is a topology, and Theorems 19.2 through 19.5 all hold in it; what he says is that it "is not so important; we shall use it primarily for constructing counterexamples" (printed 115). Less useful is not the same as wrong, and Problem 5(c) makes you say which one this is.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Two factors: the same topology from a basis and from a subbasis

Definition (Munkres, printed 86). Let X and Y be topological spaces. The product topology on X × Y is the topology having as basis the collection ℬ of all sets of the form U × V, where U is an open subset of X and V is an open subset of Y.

The basis check is one identity — (U₁ × V₁) ∩ (U₂ × V₂) = (U₁ ∩ U₂) × (V₁ ∩ V₂) — and Munkres immediately warns that ℬ is not a topology: the union of two disjoint open rectangles is open and is not a product of two sets (printed 86). Basis elements generate; they do not exhaust.

Theorem 15.1 (printed 86–87). If ℬ is a basis for the topology of X and 𝒞 is a basis for the topology of Y, then the collection 𝒟 = {B × C : B ∈ ℬ and C ∈ 𝒞} is a basis for the topology of X × Y.

Theorem 15.2 (printed 88). The collection 𝒮 = {π₁⁻¹(U) : U open in X} ∪ {π₂⁻¹(V) : V open in Y} is a subbasis for the product topology on X × Y.

Theorem 15.2's proof rests on a single identity, U × V = π₁⁻¹(U) ∩ π₂⁻¹(V), and it is the one to carry forward: it says every basis element of the product is a finite intersection of subbasis elements, each of which constrains one coordinate only. That reformulation is what generalises to infinitely many factors, and the basis formulation is what does not.

Self-check 1. Which is an open set of ℝ² that is not a product of two subsets of ℝ?

The union of the two squares. If it were A × B then, since it contains (0.5, 0.5) and (2.5, 2.5), we would need 0.5, 2.5 ∈ A and 0.5, 2.5 ∈ B, hence (0.5, 2.5) ∈ A × B — but that point is in neither square. This is Munkres's remark on printed 86 that ℬ is not a topology. Note ∅ = ∅ × ∅ is a product, and ℝ × (0,1) is one by inspection.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Arbitrarily many factors, and the clause that separates them

Definitions (Munkres, printed 114). For an indexed family {X_α}_{α∈J}, the box topology on ∏X_α is generated by the basis of all sets ∏U_α with U_α open in X_α for each α.

Let π_β : ∏X_α → X_β be the projection, π_β((x_α)) = x_β. Let 𝒮_β = {π_β⁻¹(U_β) : U_β open in X_β} and 𝒮 = ⋃_β 𝒮_β. The topology generated by the subbasis 𝒮 is the product topology, and ∏X_α with it is a product space.

The two definitions are not stated in parallel, and that is deliberate. The box topology is given by a basis; the product topology by a subbasis. Working out what basis the subbasis generates is the computation on printed 115, and it has one step worth naming: intersecting two members of the same 𝒮_β gives nothing new, since π_β⁻¹(U_β) ∩ π_β⁻¹(V_β) = π_β⁻¹(U_β ∩ V_β). So a typical finite intersection runs over distinct indices β₁,…,β_n, and constrains exactly n coordinates — leaving all the others entirely free.

Theorem 19.1, comparison of the box and product topologies (printed 115). The box topology on ∏X_α has as basis all sets of the form ∏U_α, where U_α is open in X_α for each α. The product topology on ∏X_α has as basis all sets of the form ∏U_α, where U_α is open in X_α for each α and U_α equals X_α except for finitely many values of α.

Munkres draws two consequences immediately (printed 115). First, "for finite products ∏nα=1 X_α the two topologies are precisely the same" — with finitely many indices, "all but finitely many" is no restriction at all. Second, "the box topology is in general finer than the product topology", because every product basis element is a box basis element and not conversely.

Then the convention, in his italics: whenever we consider the product ∏X_α, we shall assume it is given the product topology unless we specifically state otherwise. And the reason, deferred: the answer to why "will appear as we continue our study of topology", with the box topology reserved "primarily for constructing counterexamples".

Theorem 19.2 (printed 116). If the topology on each X_α is given by a basis ℬ_α, then the sets ∏B_α with B_α ∈ ℬ_α form a basis for the box topology; and the sets ∏B_α with B_α ∈ ℬ_α for finitely many α and B_α = X_α for the rest form a basis for the product topology.

Theorem 19.3 (printed 116). If A_α is a subspace of X_α for each α, then ∏A_α is a subspace of ∏X_α, if both products are given the box topology or both the product topology.

Theorem 19.4 (printed 116). If each X_α is Hausdorff, then ∏X_α is Hausdorff in both the box and product topologies.

Theorem 19.5 (printed 116). If ∏X_α is given either the product or the box topology, then ∏Ā_α = (∏A_α)‾.

Munkres writes of this group: "most of the proofs are left to the exercises" (printed 115). Theorems 19.2, 19.3 and 19.4 are his Exercises 1, 2 and 3 and are proved nowhere in the section; they are Problems 2(d), 3(a) and 3(b) here. Theorem 19.5 he does prove.

Note what that list means for the argument. Four results in a row, and every one of them holds in both topologies. After Theorem 19.5 Munkres says so himself: "So far, no reason has appeared for preferring the product to the box topology."

Each is an earlier result with the index set widened, and it is worth naming which. Theorem 19.2 is Lemma 13.2 (top-02) applied once in each topology — the candidate sets are open, and every point of an open set has one of them around it inside. Theorem 19.4 widens top-03's Theorem 17.11, whose product clause covered two factors only. Theorem 19.5's proof is Theorem 17.5(a) run coordinatewise: x_α ∈ Ā_α says every open U_α about x_α meets A_α, and choosing one such point in each coordinate assembles a point of ∏A_α inside the given basis element. And Theorem 19.5 is worth comparing with top-04's Theorem 18.1(2), which gave only the inclusion f(Ā) ⊂ f(A)‾ for a general continuous map. Here the relation is an equality, because a product is not an arbitrary continuous image: the projections let the closure condition be tested coordinatewise in both directions, which is exactly what an arbitrary f does not permit.

Self-check 2. On ℝω, is (−1,1) × (−1/2, 1/2) × (−1/3, 1/3) × ⋯ open in the product topology?

No. By Theorem 19.1 a product-topology basis element must have U_α = X_α for all but finitely many α, and here every coordinate is cut down. It is a perfectly good box-topology basis element — which is exactly Munkres's Example 2, and the set that breaks Theorem 19.6.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

The one theorem that tells them apart

Munkres's own hinge sentence, printed 116: "It is when we try to generalize our previous theorem about continuity of maps into product spaces that a difference first arises. Here is a theorem that does not hold if ∏X_α is given the box topology."

Theorem 19.6 (printed 117). Let f : A → ∏_{α∈J} X_α be given by f(a) = (f_α(a))_{α∈J}, where f_α : A → X_α for each α. Let ∏X_α have the product topology. Then f is continuous if and only if each f_α is continuous.
Worked example — the proof, and where the finiteness clause is spent.

Projections are continuous. If U_β is open in X_β then π_β⁻¹(U_β) is by definition a subbasis element of the product topology, hence open.

Forwards. f_β = π_β ∘ f, a composite of continuous maps, continuous by Theorem 18.2(c) (top-04).

Backwards. By the subbasis reduction of top-04 (§18, printed 103) it is enough to check that the preimage of each subbasis element is open. A subbasis element of the product topology is π_β⁻¹(U_β) for a single index β, and
f⁻¹(π_β⁻¹(U_β)) = f_β⁻¹(U_β),
because f_β = π_β ∘ f. That is open in A since f_β is continuous. ∎

The whole argument is in the size of the subbasis. The product topology has a subbasis whose members constrain one coordinate each, so checking continuity reduces to checking one coordinate function at a time. The box topology has no such subbasis: its basis elements constrain all coordinates at once, and there is no way to take them apart. That is the same shape as top-04's basis and subbasis reductions — the coarser the generating collection, the less you have to check — pushed to the point where it decides a theorem.
Faded example — Munkres's Example 2, the failure made concrete. Define f : ℝ → ℝω by f(t) = (t, t, t, …), so the nth coordinate function is f_n(t) = t.

Each f_n is the identity, hence continuous, so f is continuous into the product topology by Theorem 19.6. Now give ℝω the box topology and take the basis element
B = (−1,1) × (−1/2, 1/2) × (−1/3, 1/3) × ⋯

Guided proof. Munkres's Exercise 5, printed 118, reads in full: "One of the implications stated in the maps-into-products theorem holds for the box topology. Which one?"

Answer it, and prove the surviving implication for the box topology. Then say in one sentence which feature of the box topology kills the other.
Model answer The surviving implication is: f continuous ⇒ each f_α continuous.

Proof. Give ∏X_α the box topology and suppose f : A → ∏X_α is continuous. Fix β and let U_β be open in X_β. Then π_β⁻¹(U_β) = ∏U_α with U_β the given set and U_α = X_α for α ≠ β. That is a product of open sets, hence a basis element of the box topology, hence open. So π_β is continuous in the box topology as well, and f_β = π_β ∘ f is continuous as a composite (Theorem 18.2(c)). ∎

Why the other direction dies. The backward direction of Theorem 19.6 worked by checking preimages of subbasis elements, each constraining a single coordinate. The box topology has no subbasis of that kind — its basis elements constrain every coordinate simultaneously — so knowing each f_α separately never assembles into a statement about f. Example 2 shows the gap is real and not merely a gap in the argument.

A remark worth keeping. Note that this makes the box topology finer in exactly the way that hurts: more open sets in the target means more preimages that have to come out open, so continuity into it is a stronger demand. Fineness is not a virtue when the space is a codomain.

Self-check 3. Which direction of Theorem 19.6 survives in the box topology?

The forward one. It only needs the projections to be continuous, and π_β⁻¹(U_β) is a box basis element (all other factors being the whole space), so they are. The converse is exactly what Example 2 refutes: f(t) = (t,t,t,…) has continuous coordinate functions and is not continuous into the box topology. This is Munkres's Exercise 5, set and not answered in the text.

Self-check 4. Does §19 improve on top-04's warning about maps out of a product?

No. Theorem 19.6 generalises Theorem 18.4, which was about maps into a product; the domain case remains what Munkres called it on printed 110, a place with no useful criterion. And top-04's counterexample lives in ℝ × ℝ — a finite product, where box and product agree by Theorem 19.1 — so nothing in §19 touches it.

Self-check 5. For which index sets J do the box and product topologies on ∏_{α∈J} X_α necessarily coincide?

All finite J, by Theorem 19.1's first consequence. But the answer is "and some infinite ones too", which the degenerate case forces: if all but finitely many X_α are indiscrete — one-point spaces being the extreme — then the only open U_α available for those factors are ∅ and X_α, and the finiteness clause bites on nothing. Problem 2(c) asks you to state the sharp condition.

Check against this 1. Two factors admit two equivalent descriptions, and only one of them generalises. The basis description (Theorem 15.1) says the open rectangles generate; the subbasis description (Theorem 15.2) says U × V = π₁⁻¹(U) ∩ π₂⁻¹(V), so the generators each constrain one coordinate. With finitely many factors the two agree. With infinitely many they do not, and each gives a different topology: box from the basis, product from the subbasis.

2. Theorem 19.1 states the difference in one clause — the product topology's basis elements have U_α = X_α for all but finitely many α — and its consequences are that the two agree for finite products and that box is in general finer. Theorems 19.2 to 19.5 then hold in both, so after five theorems Munkres can say that no reason to prefer either has appeared.

3. Theorem 19.6 is where they part, and the reason is the size of the subbasis. Continuity into a product can be checked one coordinate at a time exactly because the product topology has a subbasis whose members constrain one coordinate each. The box topology has no such subbasis, and f(t) = (t,t,t,…) into ℝω is continuous coordinatewise and not continuous. Only the forward implication survives (Exercise 5), because the projections stay continuous. Fineness in a codomain is a cost, not a benefit.
