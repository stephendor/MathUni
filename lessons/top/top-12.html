The quotient topology (top-12)

Glue the edges of a square and a torus falls out — the construction algebraic topology is built on. (Compare la-09, aa-09, aa-16.) Munkres opens §22 with a warning that this one is different in kind: "Unlike the topologies we have already considered in this chapter, the quotient topology is not a natural generalization of something you have already studied in analysis." Everything before this came from ℝ. This comes from cutting and pasting.

Predict before we start. To build a space by gluing, you choose which points to identify — a partition of X. Predict how much freedom remains in choosing the topology on the set of pieces.

None. Munkres states it on printed 138: for a surjection p : X → A "there exists exactly one topology 𝒯 on A relative to which p is a quotient map". The uniqueness is what the biconditional in the definition buys — mere continuity would leave the indiscrete topology as an option, and every topology between.

Why this matters for the mission: Quotients build every cell complex; the fourth and most geometric rehearsal of the collapse move.

Cell complexes are not in §22. Munkres builds quotient spaces here and gives two of them by partition — the ball with its boundary collapsed (Example 4) and the square with its edges identified (Example 5) — but the word "cell complex" does not appear, and neither does any general construction of one. That is Hatcher ch. 0, which is top-13's source; this unit supplies the machine, not the objects built with it.

And the strip's "fourth rehearsal" points at three units that do not exist. The hook names la-09, aa-09 and aa-16 as the earlier rehearsals of the collapse move. All three are S1 syllabus units whose lessons have not been written, so nothing here can lean on them, and nothing here does.

What §22 does deliver — and it is what tda2-06 was promised. That unit, already shipped, says the Reeb graph "is the quotient of X by the relation 'lies in the same connected component of a level set of f' — top-12's quotient topology applied to a scalar field". §22 supplies exactly that: the equivalence between a partition and a relation (printed 139), the unique topology on the set of classes (printed 138), and Theorem 22.2's universal property for maps out of it. It supplies nothing about the result being a graph, and nothing about its being Hausdorff — Corollary 22.3(b) needs a Hausdorff target handed to it, which a bare partition does not provide.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Quotient maps, and the topology they force

Definition (Munkres, printed 137). Let X and Y be topological spaces; let p : X → Y be a surjective map. The map p is said to be a quotient map provided a subset U of Y is open in Y if and only if p⁻¹(U) is open in X.

The "only if" half alone is continuity. The other half is the strengthening, and it is what makes the notion useful: it says Y carries no more open sets than X forces on it. A continuous surjection need not be a quotient map — top-04's F : [0,1) → S¹ is a continuous bijection whose inverse is discontinuous, so some subset of S¹ has open preimage without being open.

Two sufficient conditions are worth having. A surjective continuous map that is an open map is a quotient map: if p⁻¹(U) is open then p(p⁻¹(U)) = U is open, using surjectivity for the equality. The same argument with closed sets shows a surjective continuous closed map is a quotient map. Neither reverses, and Munkres's Example 2 shows the two conditions are themselves independent: π₁ : ℝ × ℝ → ℝ is open — it carries a basis element U × V to U — and is not closed, since the hyperbola xy = 1 is closed in the plane and projects onto ℝ − {0}.

Definitions (Munkres, printed 138–139). If X is a space, A is a set, and p : X → A is surjective, then there exists exactly one topology 𝒯 on A relative to which p is a quotient map; it is called the quotient topology induced by p.

Let X* be a partition of X into disjoint subsets whose union is X, and let p : X → X* carry each point to the element containing it. In the quotient topology induced by p, the space X* is called a quotient space of X.

And here is the sentence tda2-06 leans on, printed 139: "Given X*, there is an equivalence relation on X of which the elements of X* are the equivalence classes." The two data are interchangeable — a partition gives the relation "same block", a relation gives the partition into classes — so "the quotient of X by an equivalence relation ∼" is a definition rather than a licence. Munkres adds the names identification space and decomposition space, and a description worth memorising: "the typical open set of X* is a collection of equivalence classes whose union is an open set of X."

Worked example — Example 5, read as a partition rather than as a picture. Munkres's X is the square [0,1] × [0,1], and X* consists of:
• all one-point sets {x × y} with 0 < x < 1 and 0 < y < 1;
• the two-point sets {x × 0, x × 1} for 0 < x < 1;
• the two-point sets {0 × y, 1 × y} for 0 < y < 1;
• the four-point set {0 × 0, 0 × 1, 1 × 0, 1 × 1}.

Why the corner class has four points and not two. Both identifications act on it. The corner 0 × 0 is glued to 1 × 0 by the left–right rule and to 0 × 1 by the bottom–top rule; chasing both gives 1 × 1 as well. Any class must be closed under both relations, so the four corners collapse to a single point of X*. Every other class is closed under at most one.

What a saturated open set looks like. Munkres's own description: an open set of X that "equals a union of elements of X*". So an open disc in the interior is saturated; an open strip along the left edge is not, unless the matching strip along the right edge is included with it; and any saturated open set meeting a corner must contain a quarter-disc at all four corners at once.

What §22 does and does not then say. It constructs X* and describes its open sets. It does not prove X* is homeomorphic to a torus — Example 5 states the partition and the figures illustrate it, and that is all. The same holds for Example 4, where Munkres writes only "One can show that X* is homeomorphic with the subspace of ℝ³ called the unit 2-sphere".

Self-check 1. How many topologies on a set A make a given surjection p : X → A a quotient map?

Exactly one, printed 138. The definition is a biconditional, so it determines which subsets are open: U is open iff p⁻¹(U) is. Merely requiring p continuous would allow many topologies — the indiscrete one always works — which is why "quotient map" is more than "continuous surjection".

The universal property, which is what everyone actually uses

Theorem 22.1 (printed 140). Let p : X → Y be a quotient map; let A be a subspace of X that is saturated with respect to p; let q : A → p(A) be the restriction of p.
(1) If A is either open or closed in X, then q is a quotient map.
(2) If p is either an open map or a closed map, then q is a quotient map.

Theorem 22.2 (printed 142). Let p : X → Y be a quotient map. Let Z be a space and let g : X → Z be a map that is constant on each set p⁻¹({y}), for y ∈ Y. Then g induces a map f : Y → Z such that f ∘ p = g. The induced map f is continuous if and only if g is continuous; f is a quotient map if and only if g is a quotient map.

Corollary 22.3 (printed 142). Let g : X → Z be a surjective continuous map, and let X* = {g⁻¹({z}) : z ∈ Z} with the quotient topology. (a) g induces a bijective continuous f : X* → Z, which is a homeomorphism if and only if g is a quotient map. (b) If Z is Hausdorff, so is X*.
Worked example — Theorem 22.2's continuity clause, in one identity. Suppose g is continuous and let V be open in Z. Then g⁻¹(V) is open in X. Now
g⁻¹(V) = p⁻¹(f⁻¹(V)),
because f ∘ p = g means x ∈ g⁻¹(V) ⟺ g(x) ∈ V ⟺ f(p(x)) ∈ V ⟺ p(x) ∈ f⁻¹(V) ⟺ x ∈ p⁻¹(f⁻¹(V)).

So p⁻¹(f⁻¹(V)) is open in X. And because p is a quotient map — this is the "if" half of the biconditional, the half that continuity alone would not give — it follows that f⁻¹(V) is open in Y. Hence f is continuous. ∎

This is the property at1-01 and at1-02 were promised. Both, already on the shelf, use the phrasing "a map out of a quotient is continuous exactly when its composite with the quotient map is". That is precisely Theorem 22.2's continuity clause, and nothing more: it says nothing about f being injective, surjective, open or a homeomorphism. Those are separate clauses, and Corollary 22.3 is where they are collected.

Note what makes it worth having. Maps into a quotient are hard — you would have to know the quotient's open sets. Maps out of one are easy, because the theorem hands the question back to X, where you started.

Compare the two neighbouring tools. Theorem 18.3, the pasting lemma of top-04, also builds a map out of a decomposition — but from closed pieces on which the map is already given, and it needs them to agree on overlaps. Theorem 22.2 needs no agreement condition, because being constant on fibres is the agreement condition. And Theorem 19.6 of top-05 is the mirror image: it governs maps into a product coordinatewise, which is exactly the direction Theorem 22.2 does not help with.
Guided proof. Prove Corollary 22.3(a), and then combine it with top-08's Theorem 26.6 to get a usable criterion.

Specifically: show that if X is compact, Z is Hausdorff, and g : X → Z is a continuous surjection, then X* is homeomorphic to Z.
Model answer Corollary 22.3(a). The map g is constant on each g⁻¹({z}) by construction, so Theorem 22.2 induces a continuous f : X* → Z with f ∘ p = g. It is surjective because g is, and injective because distinct elements of X* are distinct fibres g⁻¹({z}) and g⁻¹({z′}) with z ≠ z′. So f is a continuous bijection.

The criterion. Suppose X is compact, Z Hausdorff, g continuous and surjective.
• X* = p(X) is compact, being a continuous image of a compact space (Theorem 26.5) — p is continuous because every quotient map is.
• f : X* → Z is a continuous bijection, by the paragraph above.
• Z is Hausdorff by hypothesis.
By Theorem 26.6 of top-08, f is a homeomorphism. Hence X* ≅ Z. ∎

What this buys, and what it still costs. It converts "is this quotient the space I think it is?" into "can I write down a continuous surjection onto that space?" — no inverse to construct, no open sets to chase. For Munkres's Example 4, applying it would need a continuous surjection from the closed unit ball onto S² whose point-preimages are exactly the elements of his partition. He does not construct one; he writes "One can show that…", and the construction is left. So even with the criterion in hand, Example 4's conclusion remains something this unit has been told rather than shown.
Faded example — Theorem 22.1, and what saturation is for. Let p : X → Y be a quotient map and A ⊂ X saturated, meaning A contains every fibre p⁻¹({y}) that it meets.

The two identities the proof runs on. For V ⊂ p(A): every point of p⁻¹(V) lies in a fibre that meets A, hence lies in A by saturation, so q⁻¹(V) = p⁻¹(V). And for U ⊂ A, p(U) = q(U) trivially.

Self-check 2. Theorem 22.2 makes which kind of map easy to handle?

Out of. The theorem replaces a question about f : Y → Z, where Y's topology is defined indirectly, with a question about g : X → Z on the original space. Maps into a quotient get no such help, and Munkres's Example 7 — the product of two quotient maps need not be a quotient map — is a reminder that quotients do not combine freely.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

What a quotient can destroy

Almost every property in this module survives subspaces and products. Quotients are the operation that does not respect them. Corollary 22.3(b) looks like a Hausdorff-preservation statement and is not: it says that if the quotient happens to map bijectively onto a Hausdorff Z, then it is Hausdorff — the Hausdorffness is imported, not generated.

The honest statement is that a quotient of a Hausdorff space need not be Hausdorff. Collapse ℤ ⊂ ℝ to a single point: any saturated open set containing ℤ must contain an interval about every integer, and there is no way to separate the collapsed point from a nearby ordinary point without one such interval intruding. So ℝ is Hausdorff, normal, metrizable — everything top-06 and top-11 could ask — and its quotient is not even Hausdorff.

You try. Work Munkres's Example 6, printed 142–143.

X is the union of the segments [0,1] × {n} for n ∈ ℤ₊ — countably many disjoint segments. Z is the union of the segments through the points x × (x/n) — countably many segments sharing an endpoint at the origin. There is an obvious surjection X → Z gluing all the left endpoints together.

Show it is a continuous bijection off the glued point and a continuous surjection everywhere, and then show it is not a quotient map by exhibiting a set with open preimage that is not open in Z. Then say which hypothesis of the guided proof's criterion fails — there is exactly one, and naming it is the point of the exercise.

Finally, connect it back: which of the three clauses of Corollary 22.3(a) does this example show cannot be dropped?

Self-check 3. Is a quotient of a Hausdorff space Hausdorff?

Not in general. Corollary 22.3(b) assumes a Hausdorff Z and a map onto it — it imports the property rather than proving it. Collapsing ℤ in ℝ gives a non-Hausdorff quotient of a metric space. This is the operation that breaks the pattern of the last several units, where subspaces and products preserved almost everything.

Self-check 4. tda2-06 says the Reeb graph is "the quotient of X by the relation 'lies in the same connected component of a level set'". What does §22 guarantee about the result?

Only the first. §22 turns the relation into a partition (printed 139), gives the set of classes its unique quotient topology (printed 138), and hands you Theorem 22.2 for maps out of it. Being a graph is an extra hypothesis — tda2-06's own source imposes a tameness condition for exactly that reason — and Hausdorffness would need Corollary 22.3(b)'s imported target. Compactness is inherited, by Theorem 26.5, but §22 does not say so; you have to fetch it from top-08.

Self-check 5. In Example 5's partition of the square, how many points are in the class containing the corner 0 × 0?

Four. The left–right rule glues 0 × 0 to 1 × 0, the bottom–top rule glues it to 0 × 1, and closing under both brings in 1 × 1. A class must be closed under the whole relation, which is why the corner class is the only one of its size in Munkres's list.

Check against this 1. A quotient map is a continuous surjection with the converse clause attached, and that clause is what forces uniqueness: for a surjection p : X → A there is exactly one topology making p a quotient map. Open maps and closed maps are two sufficient conditions, independent of each other — π₁ on ℝ × ℝ is open and not closed.

2. A partition and an equivalence relation are the same data (printed 139), so "the quotient of X by ∼" is defined, and its open sets are the collections of classes whose union is open in X. Example 5's square has four kinds of class, and the corner class has four points because it must be closed under both identifications.

3. Theorem 22.2 makes maps out of a quotient easy and says nothing about maps in. Its proof is the single identity g⁻¹(V) = p⁻¹(f⁻¹(V)) plus the quotient map's biconditional. Corollary 22.3 with top-08's Theorem 26.6 turns it into a working criterion — compact source, Hausdorff target, continuous surjection, and the quotient is the target. But quotients destroy what subspaces and products preserved: collapse ℤ in ℝ and Hausdorffness is gone.
