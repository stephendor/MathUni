Basis, order, and subspace topologies (top-02)

You can't list every open set by hand — so name a basis and let it generate the rest. The real line makes the point: its standard topology has uncountably many open sets, and you have never written one down except as a union of intervals. That is not laziness, it is the definition. But the word "basis" is a false friend here, and Munkres says so on printed 80: the expression of an open set as a union of basis elements is not unique, so the term "differs drastically from its use in linear algebra". A basis in topology is a supply of small sets, not a coordinate system.

Predict before we start. A subset Y of a space X inherits a topology. If Y also happens to carry an order of its own, it has a second, independently defined topology — the order topology of Y. Predict: do they agree?

Not always. Y = [0,1) ∪ {2} inside ℝ is the counterexample on printed 90: the set {2} is open in the subspace topology and not in the order topology of Y. Theorem 16.4 gives the condition that rescues it — Y must be convex in X — and segment 3 shows exactly where the proof would break without it.

Why this matters for the mission: The subspace topology is how sampled data inherits structure from the space it sits inside.

Two bounds on the strip.

There is no data here. §16 defines 𝒯_Y = {Y ∩ U : U ∈ 𝒯} for an arbitrary subset Y of an arbitrary space. Nothing about sampling, finiteness or point clouds is in the section; the reading of Y as "the sample" is TDA's, and it is lab-01 that makes a finite metric space out of a point cloud.

"Inherits structure" is exactly right for the topology and wrong for anything else the ambient space carried. This is the substantive bound, and §16 makes it itself. When X is an ordered set, a subset Y inherits two things — the topology, and the order — and the topology Y inherits need not be the topology its inherited order generates. Munkres puts it in italics on printed 90: "the resulting order topology on Y need not be the same as the topology that Y inherits as a subspace of X". Theorem 16.4 identifies convexity as what makes them agree. So inheritance is a fact about one structure at a time, and a subset that looks like a faithful small copy of its ambient space may not be one.

Bases: generating a topology, and recognising one

Definition (Munkres, printed 78). If X is a set, a basis for a topology on X is a collection ℬ of subsets of X (called basis elements) such that
(1) For each x ∈ X, there is at least one basis element B containing x.
(2) If x belongs to the intersection of two basis elements B₁ and B₂, then there is a basis element B₃ containing x such that B₃ ⊂ B₁ ∩ B₂.

The topology 𝒯 generated by ℬ: a subset U of X is open in X if for each x ∈ U there is a basis element B ∈ ℬ such that x ∈ B and B ⊂ U.

Condition (2) is quantified over points of the intersection, which is why it holds vacuously when two basis elements are disjoint — the commonest way to satisfy it, and the one Problem 1(a) turns on. Note also what the definition of "generated" does not say: it does not say the open sets are the basis elements. Each basis element is itself open (take B = B), but there are many more open sets, and the next lemma says exactly how many more.

Lemma 13.1 (printed 80). Let X be a set; let ℬ be a basis for a topology 𝒯 on X. Then 𝒯 equals the collection of all unions of elements of ℬ.

Proof (Munkres's). Given a collection of elements of ℬ, they are also elements of 𝒯; because 𝒯 is a topology, their union is in 𝒯. Conversely, given U ∈ 𝒯, choose for each x ∈ U an element B_x of ℬ with x ∈ B_x ⊂ U. Then U = ⋃_{x ∈ U} B_x, so U equals a union of elements of ℬ. ∎

The union in that last line is worth pausing on. It contains U because each x ∈ U is in its own B_x; it is contained in U because each B_x ⊂ U. Both directions are needed and both are one line, which is the shape of most arguments in this section.

Lemma 13.1 goes from a basis to its topology. Going the other way — being handed a topology and asked to produce a basis for it — is the move you will make far more often, and Lemma 13.2 is the tool.

Lemma 13.2 (printed 80). Let X be a topological space. Suppose that 𝒞 is a collection of open sets of X such that for each open set U of X and each x in U, there is an element C of 𝒞 such that x ∈ C ⊂ U. Then 𝒞 is a basis for the topology of X.

Read the hypothesis carefully: 𝒞 must consist of open sets, and the covering condition must hold for every open U, not just for X. Both clauses earn their place. Openness is what lets the proof intersect two members of 𝒞 and still have an open set to apply the hypothesis to; and quantifying over every open U is what forces the generated topology to be the whole of 𝒯 rather than something coarser.

Lemma 13.3 (printed 81). Let ℬ and ℬ′ be bases for the topologies 𝒯 and 𝒯′ on X. Then the following are equivalent:
(1) 𝒯′ is finer than 𝒯.
(2) For each x ∈ X and each basis element B ∈ ℬ containing x, there is a basis element B′ ∈ ℬ′ such that x ∈ B′ ⊂ B.

Munkres anticipates the question students actually ask — "which way does the inclusion go?" — and answers it by refining top-01's gravel: think of the pebbles as the basis elements of the old topology, and after they are smashed to dust, the dust particles are the basis elements of the new one. The new topology is finer, and each dust particle sits inside a pebble, "as the criterion states" (printed 81). The metaphor was a rough analogy in §12; here it earns its keep by fixing the direction of an inclusion.

Worked example — Lemma 13.2 applied, and a countable basis for free. Claim: ℬ_ℚ = {(a,b) : a < b, a and b rational} is a basis generating the standard topology on ℝ. (Munkres sets this as his Exercise 8(a), printed 83, and names the lemma to use.)

The members are open. Each (a,b) with rational endpoints is a basis element of the standard topology, hence open. That is the first hypothesis of Lemma 13.2.
The covering condition. Let U be open in the standard topology and let x ∈ U. By the definition of the generated topology there is a standard basis element (a,b) with x ∈ (a,b) ⊂ U. Choose rationals p, q with a < p < x and x < q < b — possible because ℚ is dense in ℝ. Then x ∈ (p,q) ⊂ (a,b) ⊂ U, and (p,q) ∈ ℬ_ℚ.

Lemma 13.2 now says ℬ_ℚ is a basis for the topology of ℝ — that is, for the standard topology, not merely for some topology. And ℬ_ℚ is indexed by pairs of rationals, so it is countable. The standard topology on ℝ therefore has a countable basis, which is a property with a name and a section of its own (top-10).

Why Lemma 13.2 and not Lemma 13.1. Lemma 13.1 would let you rebuild the topology from a basis you already knew to be one. Here the whole question is whether ℬ_ℚ is a basis for the given topology, and that is precisely what Lemma 13.2 certifies.

Self-check 1. On X = {a,b,c,d}, is ℬ = {{a,b}, {b,c}, {b}} a basis?

Condition (1) fails: no member contains d, so ℬ does not cover X. Condition (2) is fine — {a,b} ∩ {b,c} = {b}, and {b} ∈ ℬ. Adding ∅ would not help, since ∅ contains no points either. This is why condition (1) is really a covering condition: ⋃ℬ must be all of X.

Self-check 2. Lemma 13.3(2) says: for each B ∈ ℬ containing x there is B′ ∈ ℬ′ with x ∈ B′ ⊂ B. Which topology does that make finer?

𝒯′. The dust sits inside the pebbles, and the dust is the finer topology. Concretely: every standard interval (a,b) about x contains a lower-limit basis element [x,b), so ℝ_ℓ is finer than the standard topology — the direction the canvas below draws.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Three lines, and an order that generates its own topology

Definition (Munkres, printed 81–82). The topology generated by the open intervals (a,b) = {x : a < x < b} is the standard topology on ℝ. The topology generated by the half-open intervals [a,b) = {x : a ≤ x < b} is the lower limit topology; ℝ with this topology is written ℝ_ℓ. With K = {1/n : n ∈ ℤ₊}, the topology generated by all (a,b) together with all sets (a,b) − K is the K-topology; ℝ with this topology is written ℝ_K.
Lemma 13.4 (printed 82). The topologies of ℝ_ℓ and ℝ_K are strictly finer than the standard topology on ℝ, but are not comparable with one another.

Munkres proves the two "strictly finer" clauses and then writes: "We leave it to you to show that the topologies of ℝ_ℓ and ℝ_K are not comparable." He sets the same thing again as Exercise 6, printed 83. The incomparability clause is stated here and proved nowhere in the section; it is Problem 3(d).
Worked example — ℝ_ℓ is strictly finer, and the two halves are different kinds of argument.

Finer, via Lemma 13.3. Take a standard basis element (a,b) and a point x ∈ (a,b). The lower-limit basis element [x,b) contains x and lies inside (a,b). That is criterion (2) verified with ℬ the standard basis and ℬ′ the lower-limit basis, so (2) ⇒ (1) gives: ℝ_ℓ is finer.

Strictly, and not via Lemma 13.3. Here Munkres argues directly: given the basis element [x,d) of ℝ_ℓ, there is no open interval (a,b) that contains x and lies inside [x,d). Any interval containing x has a < x strictly, so it contains points less than x, and those are not in [x,d). Hence the standard topology is not finer than ℝ_ℓ, and the containment is proper.

The asymmetry matters. Lemma 13.3 is an equivalence, so it certifies "finer" cleanly in one direction; but "strictly finer" is the failure of the reverse containment, and to establish a failure you exhibit a witness rather than verify a criterion. Both halves are in the picture above: the top row is the criterion holding, the bottom row is the witness.
Faded example — ℝ_K is strictly finer, same shape, different witness.

Finer. Every standard basis element (a,b) is itself a basis element of ℝ_K, since the K-basis consists of all the (a,b) together with the sets (a,b) − K. So criterion (2) of Lemma 13.3 holds with B′ = B, and ℝ_K is finer.

Definition — the order topology (Munkres, printed 84). Let X be a set with a simple order relation, with more than one element. Let ℬ be the collection of all sets of the following types:
(1) All open intervals (a,b) in X.
(2) All intervals of the form [a₀,b), where a₀ is the smallest element (if any) of X.
(3) All intervals of the form (a,b₀], where b₀ is the largest element (if any) of X.

ℬ is a basis, and the topology it generates is the order topology.

Types (2) and (3) exist only when X has an extreme element, and they are there for one reason: without them, a smallest element a₀ would lie in no basis element at all, and condition (1) for a basis would fail. Munkres verifies that condition and then writes of condition (2), "Several cases need to be checked; we leave it to you" (printed 84). That is Problem 4(a).

Two of his examples are worth carrying. The order topology on ℤ₊ is the discrete topology: for n > 1 the singleton {n} = (n−1, n+1) is a type-(1) basis element, and {1} = [1,2) is type (2). But on X = {1,2} × ℤ₊ in the dictionary order — write a_n = 1 × n and b_n = 2 × n, so that X looks like a₁, a₂, …; b₁, b₂, … — the order topology is not discrete, and there is exactly one exception. Any basis element containing b₁ must reach below it, and everything below b₁ is some a_i; since the a-sequence has no largest member, no basis element can start immediately below b₁. So {b₁} is not open, while every other singleton is (printed 85).

Self-check 3. Why is {b₁} not open in X = {1,2} × ℤ₊?

It has no immediate predecessor: the elements below b₁ are exactly a₁, a₂, a₃, …, and that sequence has no largest member. Any basis element containing b₁ has the form (a_m, ·), so it contains a_{m+1}, a_{m+2}, … The first option is wrong twice over — b₁ is neither largest nor smallest — and X being infinite is not the reason, since ℤ₊ is infinite and its order topology is discrete.

Finally, the rays. For a ∈ X the sets (a,+∞) = {x : x > a} and (−∞,a) = {x : x < a} are the open rays; [a,+∞) and (−∞,a] are the closed rays (printed 85–86). The open rays are open, by a two-case argument — if X has a largest element b₀ then (a,+∞) = (a,b₀], a type-(3) basis element, and if not then (a,+∞) = ⋃_{x>a}(a,x) — and they form a subbasis for the order topology, in the sense of printed 82: a collection whose union is X, generating all unions of finite intersections of its members. One inclusion is that open rays are open; the other is that (a,b) = (−∞,b) ∩ (a,+∞), while [a₀,b) and (a,b₀], where they exist, are themselves open rays (printed 86).

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Subspaces, and the one place inheritance goes wrong

Definition (Munkres, printed 88). Let X be a topological space with topology 𝒯. If Y is a subset of X, the collection 𝒯_Y = {Y ∩ U : U ∈ 𝒯} is a topology on Y, called the subspace topology. With this topology Y is called a subspace of X; its open sets consist of all intersections of open sets of X with Y.

That it is a topology is two identities (printed 89): (U₁ ∩ Y) ∩ ⋯ ∩ (Uₙ ∩ Y) = (U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ Uₙ) ∩ Y and ⋃_α (U_α ∩ Y) = (⋃_α U_α) ∩ Y, with ∅ = Y ∩ ∅ and Y = Y ∩ X for axiom (1). Each right-hand side is (something open in X) ∩ Y because 𝒯 satisfies the corresponding axiom.

Lemma 16.1 (printed 89). If ℬ is a basis for the topology of X then ℬ_Y = {B ∩ Y : B ∈ ℬ} is a basis for the subspace topology on Y.

Lemma 16.2 (printed 89). Let Y be a subspace of X. If U is open in Y and Y is open in X, then U is open in X.

Theorem 16.3 (printed 89–90). If A is a subspace of X and B is a subspace of Y, then the product topology on A × B is the same as the topology A × B inherits as a subspace of X × Y.

Lemma 16.2 is the one to keep in the front of your mind, because its hypothesis is easy to drop. "Open in Y" and "open in X" are different predicates, and they coincide only when Y is itself open. Take X = ℝ, Y = [0,1], U = [0,1/2): then U = Y ∩ (−1,1/2) is open in Y and is not open in ℝ, because no interval about 0 lies inside it. Munkres makes the distinction explicit on printed 89 and it is worth adopting his phrasing — always say "open in Y" or "open in X", never bare "open", whenever a subspace is in play.

The anomaly, and it is not an edge case. Munkres italicises it on printed 90: for an ordered set X and Y ⊂ X, the resulting order topology on Y need not be the same as the topology that Y inherits as a subspace of X.

His Example 2: Y = [0,1) ∪ {2} ⊂ ℝ. In the subspace topology {2} is open, because {2} = Y ∩ (3/2, 5/2). In the order topology on Y it is not: 2 is the largest element of Y, so a basis element containing it has the form {x ∈ Y : a < x ≤ 2}, and every such set contains points of Y less than 2.

His Example 3 does the same to the unit square. On I × I with I = [0,1], the dictionary order topology differs from the topology inherited from the dictionary order topology on ℝ × ℝ. His witness is the set {1/2} × (1/2, 1], which he says is open in the subspace topology and not in the order topology "as you can check" — asserted and left to the reader, unlike Example 2, which he argues. Munkres names I × I in the dictionary order topology the ordered square, I₀².
Definition and Theorem 16.4 (printed 90–91). A subset Y of an ordered set X is convex in X if for each pair of points a < b of Y, the entire interval (a,b) of points of X lies in Y. Intervals and rays are convex.

Theorem 16.4. Let X be an ordered set in the order topology; let Y be a subset of X that is convex in X. Then the order topology on Y is the same as the topology Y inherits as a subspace of X.
Worked example — where convexity enters the proof of Theorem 16.4. Munkres works with the subbasis of open rays from §14 rather than with the basis, which is what makes the proof four lines.

Consider the ray (a,+∞) of X and intersect it with Y.
• If a ∈ Y, then (a,+∞) ∩ Y = {x ∈ Y : x > a}, which is an open ray of the ordered set Y. Fine, and convexity was not needed.
• If a ∉ Y, then because Y is convex, a is either a lower bound for Y or an upper bound for Y. In the first case (a,+∞) ∩ Y = Y; in the second it is empty.

That second bullet is the whole use of the hypothesis. Without convexity, a point a ∉ Y can have members of Y on both sides of it, and then (a,+∞) ∩ Y is neither all of Y nor empty nor an open ray of Y — it is a chunk of Y cut off at a place Y itself cannot name. That is exactly what happens in Example 2: take a = 1, which is not in Y = [0,1) ∪ {2}; then (1,+∞) ∩ Y = {2}, and Y has points below a and a point above it. The failure of convexity is witnessed by the pair 1/2 < 2 of points of Y, since the interval (1/2, 2) of ℝ is not contained in Y.

The rest is bookkeeping: the same argument for (−∞,a); the sets (a,+∞) ∩ Y and (−∞,a) ∩ Y form a subbasis for the subspace topology and are order-open, giving one inclusion; and every open ray of Y is the intersection of an open ray of X with Y, giving the other.
Guided proof. Prove Theorem 16.3: if A is a subspace of X and B is a subspace of Y, then the product topology on A × B equals the subspace topology A × B inherits from X × Y.

Munkres's proof compares bases rather than open sets. Justify that move explicitly — name the result of §13 that licenses it — and then do the computation.
Model answer Why comparing bases suffices. By Lemma 13.1, a topology generated by a basis is exactly the collection of all unions of that basis's elements. So two topologies on the same set that admit the same basis are equal: each is the set of all unions of the same collection. It is therefore enough to exhibit one collection that is simultaneously a basis for both topologies.

The subspace side. The general basis element of X × Y is U × V with U open in X and V open in Y. By Lemma 16.1, intersecting the basis of X × Y with the subset A × B gives a basis for the subspace topology on A × B. So the general basis element of the subspace topology is (U × V) ∩ (A × B).

The computation. (U × V) ∩ (A × B) = (U ∩ A) × (V ∩ B). A point (x,y) lies in the left side exactly when x ∈ U, y ∈ V, x ∈ A and y ∈ B, which is exactly the condition for it to lie in the right side.

The product side. By the definition of the subspace topology, U ∩ A is the general open set of A and V ∩ B is the general open set of B. So (U ∩ A) × (V ∩ B) is the general basis element of the product topology on A × B.

The two topologies therefore have the same basis, and by the first paragraph they are equal. ∎

Remark. Munkres puts this theorem immediately before the order-topology anomaly, and the contrast is the point: "For product topologies, the result is what one might expect; for order topologies, it is not" (printed 89). Taking a subspace commutes with forming a product, and does not commute with forming an order topology.

Self-check 4. Y = [0,1] as a subspace of ℝ. Is [0,1/2) open in Y? Open in ℝ?

[0,1/2) = Y ∩ (−1, 1/2), so it is open in Y. It is not open in ℝ: no interval about 0 lies inside it. Lemma 16.2 says this can only happen when Y itself is not open in X — and [0,1] is not open in ℝ. Munkres's Example 1 adds the other half of the story: for this particular Y the subspace and order topologies do agree, because [0,1] is convex, so Theorem 16.4 applies.

Self-check 5. Which hypothesis does Y = [0,1) ∪ {2} fail in Theorem 16.4?

Convexity. Take a = 1/2 and b = 2, both in Y; the interval (1/2, 2) of ℝ contains 1, which is not in Y. Openness of Y is a hypothesis of Lemma 16.2, not of Theorem 16.4 — and note Y does have a largest element, 2, which is part of why the order topology on Y behaves as it does.

Check against this 1. A basis is a supply of small sets, and Lemma 13.1 says the topology it generates is exactly all unions of them. Not a coordinate system — the expression is not unique, which Munkres flags as the drastic difference from linear algebra. Lemma 13.2 runs the other way, certifying that a given collection of open sets is a basis for the topology you already have, and it is the workhorse: it is what makes ℬ_ℚ a countable basis for ℝ and what finishes Lemma 16.1.

2. Comparison becomes local once you have bases (Lemma 13.3), but strictness never does. "Finer" is a criterion you verify at every point; "strictly finer" is a containment that fails, so it needs a witness. Lemma 13.4 is three instances: [x,b) inside every (a,b) makes ℝ_ℓ finer, [x,d) containing no open interval about x makes it strict, and (−1,1) − K with the point 0 does the same job for ℝ_K.

3. A subspace inherits one structure at a time. 𝒯_Y = {Y ∩ U} is always a topology, and it commutes with products (Theorem 16.3). It does not commute with the order: Y = [0,1) ∪ {2} inherits an order and a topology, and the topology its inherited order generates is coarser than the topology it inherited. Theorem 16.4 says convexity is what closes the gap, and the proof shows why — convexity is exactly what forces a point outside Y to be a bound for Y rather than a cut through it.
