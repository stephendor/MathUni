Closed sets, closure, interior, and limit points (top-03)

The closure adds exactly the points you can sneak up on — the boundary made precise. Munkres puts the same thought as a riddle on printed 93: how is a set different from a door? A door must be either open or closed and cannot be both; a set can be open, or closed, or both, or neither. [0,1) in ℝ is neither, and in the subspace Y = [0,1] ∪ (2,3) the piece [0,1] is both — the two words are not opposites and never were.

Predict before we start. A sequence in a topological space converges to a point if every neighbourhood of that point eventually contains it. Predict: can a constant sequence converge to a point it never equals?

Yes. On X = {a,b,c} with the topology {∅, X, {b}, {a,b}, {b,c}}, the constant sequence x_n = b converges to a, to b and to c, because every open set containing a or c also contains b. Munkres uses exactly this phenomenon on printed 98 to motivate the Hausdorff axiom, and Theorem 17.10 is the repair.

Why this matters for the mission: Limit points and closure are the raw material of the connectedness and compactness proofs ahead.

Two bounds on the strip, and one on the hook.

Nothing about connectedness or compactness is in §17. Munkres defines connectedness in §23 (printed 148, top-07) and compactness in §26 (printed 163, top-08). The strip is a forward promise about how this material gets used, and this unit does not redeem it. What §17 does supply is the vocabulary those proofs are written in — closed, closure, limit point, neighbourhood, Hausdorff — and the last paragraph of the section (printed 100) says so itself, listing the countability, compactness and connectedness conditions as things still to come.

The one exception is visible and worth naming. Problem 5(d) reaches connectedness by the back door: a subset other than ∅ and X with empty boundary is exactly a proper non-empty clopen set, and the existence of one is what top-07 will call a separation. That is a definition matching up, not a theorem proved here.

The hook overstates, by one word. "The boundary made precise" is not what §17's body does. Munkres defines the interior and the closure on printed 95 — and adds, of the interior, "We shall not make much use of" it. The boundary Bd A = Ā ∩ (X − A)‾ appears only in Exercise 19, printed 102. It is set, not developed. This unit makes the closure precise; the boundary is a problem.

Closed sets, and the closure you can actually compute

Definition (Munkres, printed 93). A subset A of a topological space X is said to be closed if the set X − A is open.
Theorem 17.1 (printed 94). Let X be a topological space. Then the following conditions hold:
(1) ∅ and X are closed.
(2) Arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed.
(3) Finite unions of closed sets are closed.

Compare that with top-01's axioms and notice what moved. For open sets the unrestricted operation was union and the finite one was intersection; for closed sets it is the other way round. De Morgan is the whole reason — X − ⋂A_α = ⋃(X − A_α) turns an arbitrary intersection into an arbitrary union — and the word "finite" travels with whichever operation it was attached to before complementation. Munkres notes on printed 94 that one could have axiomatised topology by the closed sets instead, and adds that this "has no particular advantage over the one we have adopted".

Theorem 17.2 (printed 94). Let Y be a subspace of X. Then a set A is closed in Y if and only if it equals the intersection of a closed set of X with Y.

Theorem 17.3 (printed 94–95). Let Y be a subspace of X. If A is closed in Y and Y is closed in X, then A is closed in X.

Munkres states Theorem 17.3 and writes "we leave the proof to you" (printed 94). It is Problem 1(d), and it is the exact mirror of top-02's Lemma 16.2, which said the same thing with "open" in both places.
Definition (Munkres, printed 95). Given a subset A of a topological space X, the interior of A is the union of all open sets contained in A, and the closure of A is the intersection of all closed sets containing A. Written Int A and Cl A or Ā. Int A is open, Ā is closed, and Int A ⊂ A ⊂ Ā. If A is open then A = Int A; if A is closed then A = Ā.

Munkres adds a sentence that is worth taking as instruction: "We shall not make much use of the interior of a set, but the closure of a set will be quite important." He also fixes a convention, on the same page — with a subspace in play, Ā always means the closure in X — and Theorem 17.4 says how to recover the other one: for A ⊂ Y ⊂ X, the closure of A in Y equals Ā ∩ Y.

Neither definition is usable as it stands. "The intersection of all closed sets containing A" is a quantification over a collection that is, as Munkres says, "usually much too big to work with". Theorem 17.5 replaces it with a pointwise test.

Theorem 17.5 (printed 96). Let A be a subset of the topological space X.
(a) Then x ∈ Ā if and only if every open set U containing x intersects A.
(b) Supposing the topology of X is given by a basis, then x ∈ Ā if and only if every basis element B containing x intersects A.

Munkres shortens "U is an open set containing x" to "U is a neighborhood of x" (printed 96), and warns on printed 97 that some authors call A a neighbourhood of x when A merely contains an open set containing x. He does not follow that practice and neither will we.
Worked example — proof by contraposition, and why it is easier. Munkres proves Theorem 17.5(a) by replacing both implications with their contrapositives, and says so in as many words. Written out, the equivalent statement is:

x ∉ Ā ⟺ there exists an open set U containing x that does not intersect A.

(⇒) If x ∉ Ā, take U = X − Ā. It is open because Ā is closed, it contains x, and it misses A because A ⊂ Ā.
(⇐) If such a U exists, then X − U is a closed set containing A. By the definition of the closure as the intersection of all such sets, Ā ⊂ X − U. Since x ∈ U, x ∉ X − U, so x ∉ Ā.

Why the contrapositive is the easier form. The original statement, in both directions, quantifies over all open sets containing x. The contrapositive asks in each direction for a single witness set — and in each direction the witness is handed to you: X − Ā one way, X − U the other. A quantifier over all becomes an exhibition of one.

Part (b) is then two lines. Every basis element is open, so "all open sets meet A" gives "all basis elements meet A". Conversely, an open set U containing x contains a basis element containing x, by the definition of the generated topology (top-02), and if that meets A then so does U.

Self-check 1. In ℝ, what is the closure of B = {1/n : n ∈ ℤ₊}?

{0} ∪ B. Every interval (a,b) containing 0 has b > 0, and some 1/n < b, so the interval meets B — hence 0 ∈ B̄ by Theorem 17.5(b). No other new point arrives: a point strictly between 1/(n+1) and 1/n has an interval about it missing B entirely. The first option confuses "every point of B is isolated in B", which is true, with "B is closed", which is false — the missing point is not in B at all.

Limit points, and the closure decomposed

Definition (Munkres, printed 97). If A is a subset of the topological space X and x is a point of X, we say that x is a limit point (or "cluster point", or "point of accumulation") of A if every neighbourhood of x intersects A in some point other than x itself. Said differently, x is a limit point of A if it belongs to the closure of A − {x}. The point x may lie in A or not; for this definition it does not matter.
Theorem 17.6 (printed 97). Let A be a subset of the topological space X, let A′ be the set of all limit points of A. Then Ā = A ∪ A′.

Corollary 17.7 (printed 98). A subset of a topological space is closed if and only if it contains all its limit points.

The three words "other than x itself" are the entire difference between a limit point and a point of the closure, and the picture above shows where they bite. For C = {0} ∪ (1,2) the closure is {0} ∪ [1,2] but the set of limit points is just [1,2]: the point 0 lies in C and is not a limit point of C, because the interval (−1/2, 1/2) meets C only at 0 itself. Such a point is called isolated. Theorem 17.6 is untroubled by it because the theorem takes a union — 0 enters Ā through A, not through A′.

Faded example — Corollary 17.7 in one line, expanded to three. Munkres's whole proof is: "The set A is closed if and only if A = Ā, and the latter holds if and only if A′ ⊂ A."

First equivalence. If A is closed then A is one of the closed sets whose intersection defines Ā, and A ⊂ Ā always, so A = Ā. Conversely if A = Ā then A is closed, because Ā is.

Second equivalence. ⟨your step: use Theorem 17.6 to rewrite A = Ā as A = A ∪ A′, and show that this holds exactly when A′ ⊂ A.⟩

⟨your step: state what the corollary says for C = {0} ∪ (1,2), and check it against the picture — is C closed, and does it contain all of C′?⟩
Guided proof. Prove Theorem 17.6: Ā = A ∪ A′.

Both inclusions. In the harder one, split on whether the point lies in A, and say explicitly which hypothesis lets you upgrade "meets A" to "meets A in a point other than x".
Model answer (A ∪ A′ ⊂ Ā). If x ∈ A′ then every neighbourhood of x intersects A — in a point different from x, but in particular it intersects A — so x ∈ Ā by Theorem 17.5(a). And A ⊂ Ā holds by definition. Hence A ∪ A′ ⊂ Ā.

(Ā ⊂ A ∪ A′). Let x ∈ Ā. If x ∈ A there is nothing to prove. So suppose x ∉ A. Since x ∈ Ā, Theorem 17.5(a) gives that every neighbourhood U of x intersects A. Because x ∉ A, any point of U ∩ A is automatically different from x. So every neighbourhood of x meets A in a point other than x, which is to say x ∈ A′. Hence x ∈ A ∪ A′. ∎

The hypothesis being consumed. The upgrade from "U meets A" to "U meets A in a point other than x" is free only because x ∉ A. That is why the proof splits: in the case x ∈ A the upgrade is not available and is not needed, since x ∈ A ∪ A′ already. A proof that tried to run uniformly would have to show that every point of the closure is a limit point, which is false — the isolated point 0 of C = {0} ∪ (1,2) is the counterexample.

Self-check 2. For C = {0} ∪ (1,2) in ℝ, which is right?

C̄ = {0} ∪ [1,2] and C′ = [1,2]. The point 0 is in C but not in C′ — the interval (−1/2, 1/2) meets C only at 0 — so it is isolated. The third option would need every point of [0,1] to be a limit point of C, but (1/4, 3/4) misses C entirely. The fourth forgets that 1 and 2 are limit points of (1,2) although neither lies in it.
You try. Prove that A′ is closed, for every subset A of every space. Munkres does not state this in §17, so you are on your own for the statement as well as the proof.

Take x ∈ (A′)‾ and a neighbourhood U of x; U meets A′ at some y. Now U is a neighbourhood of y as well, so U meets A in a point other than y. The care is in getting a point other than x: handle y = x and y ≠ x separately, and in the second case shrink U before you use it.

Then decide whether (A′)′ = A′ always holds. Test A = {1/n} in ℝ before committing to an answer, and note that the two questions have different answers — which is the point of asking both.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Hausdorff: ruling out the spaces that mislead

Munkres's warning on printed 98 is that experience with ℝ and ℝ² misleads. In those spaces every one-point set is closed and a sequence has at most one limit, and neither fact holds in an arbitrary space. His counterexample is a three-point space shown in a figure; the figure's topology is not spelled out in the text, so take instead the topology 𝒜 = {∅, X, {b}, {a,b}, {b,c}} on X = {a,b,c}, which Munkres does describe in prose in §12 (printed 76) and which top-01 verified is a topology. It has both pathologies.

Worked example — one non-closed point, three limits for one sequence. Work in (X, 𝒜) with 𝒜 = {∅, X, {b}, {a,b}, {b,c}}.

{b} is not closed. X − {b} = {a,c}, and {a,c} ∉ 𝒜. So the complement of {b} is not open, which by definition means {b} is not closed. (Indeed {b}‾ = X: every non-empty open set contains b, so every neighbourhood of every point meets {b}, and Theorem 17.5(a) puts every point in the closure.)

The constant sequence x_n = b converges to all three points. Munkres's definition, printed 98: x_n → x provided that for each neighbourhood U of x there is N with x_n ∈ U for all n ≥ N.
• Neighbourhoods of a: {a,b} and X. Both contain b, so x_n ∈ U for every n. Take N = 1.
• Neighbourhoods of b: {b}, {a,b}, {b,c}, X. All contain b.
• Neighbourhoods of c: {b,c} and X. Both contain b.
So x_n → a, x_n → b and x_n → c.

What exactly fails. There is no pair of disjoint open sets separating b from a, because every open set containing a also contains b. That is the Hausdorff condition failing, and Theorem 17.10 says it is the only thing that could have gone wrong.
Definition (Munkres, printed 98). A topological space X is called a Hausdorff space if for each pair x₁, x₂ of distinct points of X, there exist neighbourhoods U₁ and U₂ of x₁ and x₂, respectively, that are disjoint.
Theorem 17.8 (printed 99). Every finite point set in a Hausdorff space X is closed.

Theorem 17.9 (printed 99). Let X be a space satisfying the T₁ axiom; let A be a subset of X. Then the point x is a limit point of A if and only if every neighbourhood of x contains infinitely many points of A.

Theorem 17.10 (printed 99). If X is a Hausdorff space, then a sequence of points of X converges to at most one point of X.

Theorem 17.11 (printed 100). Every simply ordered set is a Hausdorff space in the order topology. The product of two Hausdorff spaces is a Hausdorff space. A subspace of a Hausdorff space is a Hausdorff space.

Munkres introduces Theorem 17.11 with "The proof of the following result is left to the exercises" (printed 100). It is stated and not proved anywhere in §17; Problem 5 proves all three clauses.

Between Hausdorff and nothing sits the T₁ axiom: the condition that finite point sets be closed. Munkres notes on printed 99 that it is strictly weaker, and gives the separating example — ℝ in the finite complement topology is T₁ and not Hausdorff. The first half is top-01's Example 3 read through complements: the 𝒯_f-closed sets are exactly ℝ and the finite sets. The second half is a one-liner worth knowing: if U and V are non-empty and 𝒯_f-open, then ℝ − U and ℝ − V are finite, so ℝ − (U ∩ V) is finite, so U ∩ V is non-empty because ℝ is infinite. In that space no two distinct points can be separated at all.

Munkres also tells you how much use T₁ will get: "The T₁ axiom will appear in this book in a few exercises, and in just one theorem" — Theorem 17.9. Its hypothesis is not decorative. In (X, 𝒜) above, take A = {b} and x = a: every neighbourhood of a contains b ≠ a, so a is a limit point of A, and A has exactly one point. Theorem 17.9 would forbid that, and the space is not T₁.

Self-check 3. ℝ with the finite complement topology 𝒯_f. Which holds?

T₁ and not Hausdorff — Munkres's own separating example, printed 99. Finite sets are closed because their complements are 𝒯_f-open by definition. But no two non-empty 𝒯_f-open sets are disjoint, since the complement of their intersection is a union of two finite sets. "Hausdorff but not T₁" is impossible: Theorem 17.8 says Hausdorff implies T₁.

Self-check 4. Which theorem is the reason "the limit" of a convergent sequence is a legitimate phrase?

Theorem 17.10 — and the qualification matters. Munkres only introduces the notation x_n → x and the phrase "x is the limit" on printed 100, immediately after proving uniqueness, and only for Hausdorff spaces. Before that theorem the definite article is not licensed, as the three-point space shows.

Check against this 1. Closed is defined by complement, so Theorem 17.1 is top-01's axioms in a mirror — and De Morgan moves the word "finite" from intersections to unions. Open and closed are not opposites: a set can be both, either, or neither, and in a subspace the answer can differ from the answer in the ambient space (Theorem 17.2, Theorem 17.3, Theorem 17.4).

2. The closure is defined by an unusable intersection and computed by Theorem 17.5, which replaces "intersect all closed sets containing A" with "test every neighbourhood of x", and by 17.5(b) with "test every basis element". Theorem 17.6 then splits Ā into A ∪ A′, and the three words "other than x itself" are exactly what keeps A′ smaller than Ā — the isolated point 0 of {0} ∪ (1,2) sits in one and not the other. Corollary 17.7: closed means containing your own limit points.

3. Without an extra axiom, none of this behaves. A one-point set need not be closed and a constant sequence can converge everywhere. The Hausdorff condition rules both out — Theorem 17.8 and Theorem 17.10 — with T₁ sitting strictly between (Theorem 17.9, and ℝ with the finite complement topology as the separating example). Theorem 17.11 says the condition survives orders, finite products and subspaces, so it costs almost nothing to assume.
