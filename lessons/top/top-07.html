Connectedness and components (top-07)

Can you walk between any two points without leaving the set? Counting those path-pieces is H₀. Except that "walk" and "one piece" are two different questions, and Munkres spends §25 showing they can have different answers. There is a subset of the plane — one curve and one segment — that is a single connected piece and cannot be walked across. Everything in this unit turns on the hypothesis that rules that out.

Predict before we start. A space is connected if it admits no separation into two disjoint non-empty open pieces; it is path connected if any two points are joined by a path. Predict the relation between them.

Path connected implies connected, by a three-line argument Munkres gives on printed 155: a path's image is a continuous image of a connected interval, so it lies wholly on one side of any separation. The converse fails, and he gives two counterexamples — the ordered square (Example 6) and the topologist's sine curve (Example 7), drawn below. Theorem 25.5 names the extra hypothesis that restores the equivalence.

Why this matters for the mission: Path components ARE what zeroth homology counts — for TDA's finite complexes they are the connected pieces, the first and most robust Betti number.

The strip's parenthesis is the load-bearing part, and Theorem 25.5 is why.

Read it again: "path components ARE ... — for TDA's finite complexes — the connected pieces". Strip the parenthesis and the claim is false. Munkres's Theorem 25.5 says only that each path component lies in a component, and that the two coincide when X is locally path connected. Without that hypothesis they come apart, and his own Example 2 is the witness: the topologist's sine curve has one component and two path components. So "path components are the connected pieces" is not a theorem; it is a theorem plus a hypothesis, and the strip's parenthetical clause is where the hypothesis is being supplied.

Note also what supplying it is not. Whether finite simplicial complexes are locally path connected is a question about those complexes, not about §25 — that belongs to at1-07 and lab-02, and nothing in this unit establishes it.

Two further bounds. There is no homology anywhere in §§23–25; the identification of H₀ with path components is at1-07's, and this unit supplies only the objects it counts. And "most robust" is a stability claim — robustness under perturbation is tda1-07's theorem, and nothing here bears on it.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Separations, and the four things connectedness survives

Definition (Munkres, printed 148). Let X be a topological space. A separation of X is a pair U, V of disjoint non-empty open subsets of X whose union is X. The space X is said to be connected if there does not exist a separation of X.

Two remarks before the theorems. First, the definition is about the whole space, so applying it to a subspace Y means applying it to Y's own topology — which is why Lemma 23.1 restates the condition in terms of limit points, letting you check it inside X without ever computing the subspace topology. Second, the pieces are required non-empty, and that is what makes a one-point space and the empty space connected.

Lemma 23.1 (printed 148). If Y is a subspace of X, a separation of Y is a pair of disjoint non-empty sets A and B whose union is Y, neither of which contains a limit point of the other. The space Y is connected if there exists no separation of Y.

Lemma 23.2 (printed 149). If the sets C and D form a separation of X, and if Y is a connected subspace of X, then Y lies entirely within either C or D.

Theorem 23.3 (printed 150). The union of a collection of connected subspaces of X that have a point in common is connected.

Theorem 23.4 (printed 150). Let A be a connected subspace of X. If A ⊂ B ⊂ Ā, then B is also connected.

Theorem 23.5 (printed 150). The image of a connected space under a continuous map is connected.

Theorem 23.6 (printed 150). A finite cartesian product of connected spaces is connected.
Worked example — Theorem 23.3, and why Lemma 23.2 is the whole proof. Let {A_α} be connected subspaces of X with a common point p, and put Y = ⋃A_α. Suppose Y = C ∪ D were a separation.

The point p lies in one of the two pieces; say p ∈ C. Each A_α is a connected subspace of Y, so by Lemma 23.2 it lies entirely in C or entirely in D. Since p ∈ A_α for every α and p ∈ C, every A_α lies in C. Hence Y = ⋃A_α ⊂ C, so D = ∅ — contradicting the requirement that both pieces of a separation be non-empty. ∎

Where the hypothesis is spent. The common point is used exactly once, in the sentence "since p ∈ A_α for every α". Delete it and the theorem is false: two disjoint open intervals of ℝ are each connected and their union is not. Lemma 23.2 is the engine — it converts "connected" from a statement about the piece into a statement about where the piece can sit.

Theorem 23.4 runs the same way, with Lemma 23.1 instead: a separation C, D of B has A wholly inside one, say C; then B ⊂ Ā ⊂ C̄, so any point of D is a limit point of C, which Lemma 23.1 forbids.

Self-check 1. Is ℚ, as a subspace of ℝ, connected?

No. ℚ ∩ (−∞, √2) and ℚ ∩ (√2, ∞) are disjoint, non-empty, open in ℚ, and cover it, since √2 ∉ ℚ. In fact Munkres's Example 1 in §25 (printed 161) says more: every component of ℚ is a single point, and none of them is open in ℚ. Density is no help — the third option is the trap, since connectedness does not pass to dense subspaces, only to closures (Theorem 23.4).

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The real line, the IVT, and paths

Theorem 24.1 (printed 153). If L is a linear continuum in the order topology, then L is connected, and so are intervals and rays in L.

Corollary 24.2 (printed 154). The real line ℝ is connected and so are intervals and rays in ℝ.

Theorem 24.3, the intermediate value theorem (printed 154). Let f : X → Y be a continuous map, where X is a connected space and Y is an ordered set in the order topology. If a and b are two points of X and if r is a point of Y lying between f(a) and f(b), then there exists a point c of X such that f(c) = r.

Theorem 24.3 is the calculus theorem with its hypotheses laid bare, and the proof is two lines once you see what to separate. Suppose no such c exists. Then f(X) ∩ (−∞, r) and f(X) ∩ (r, +∞) are disjoint, open in f(X) — open rays are open in the order topology, from top-02 — non-empty, since one contains f(a) and the other f(b), and together they cover f(X) because r is not a value. That is a separation of f(X), which is connected by Theorem 23.5. Contradiction. The domain's connectedness enters only through Theorem 23.5, and the order on Y only through the rays.

Definition (Munkres, printed 155). Given points x and y of the space X, a path in X from x to y is a continuous map f : [a,b] → X of some closed interval in the real line into X, such that f(a) = x and f(b) = y. A space X is said to be path connected if every pair of points of X can be joined by a path in X.

Path connected implies connected, and Munkres's argument is worth having in one line: if X = A ∪ B were a separation, then any path's image f([a,b]) is a continuous image of a connected set, hence connected, hence lies entirely in A or in B by Lemma 23.2 — so no path joins a point of A to a point of B. "The converse does not hold; a connected space need not be path connected."

Worked example — the topologist's sine curve, in two claims. Munkres's two topologist's-sine-curve examples, printed 156 and 160. Let
S = {x × sin(1/x) : 0 < x ≤ 1}, and let S̄ be its closure in ℝ².

S̄ is connected. S is the image of the connected set (0,1] — connected by Corollary 24.2 — under a continuous map, hence connected by Theorem 23.5. Its closure is then connected by Theorem 23.4, taking B = Ā. So S̄ has exactly one component.

S̄ has two path components. They are S itself and the vertical segment V = 0 × [−1,1], which is the part the closure adds. Munkres records their topological status too, and it is the sharpest illustration of his remark that path components "need be neither open nor closed": S is open in S̄ and not closed; V is closed and not open.

Why this is the example to remember. One component, two path components. So "component" and "path component" are genuinely different notions, and any statement identifying them is carrying a hypothesis. Munkres adds a refinement on the same page: delete from V the points with rational second coordinate and you get a space with one component and uncountably many path components.

Self-check 2. Which is true of the topologist's sine curve S̄?

One component — S̄ is connected, being the closure of a continuous image of an interval — and two path components, S and V = 0 × [−1,1]. The last option is impossible in any space: a path component is path connected, hence connected, so it can never straddle two components (Theorem 25.5, first clause).

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Components, path components, and the hypothesis that fuses them

Definitions (Munkres, printed 159–161). Define x ∼ y if there is a connected subspace of X containing both; the equivalence classes are the components of X. Define x ∼ y if there is a path in X from x to y; the classes are the path components of X.

X is locally connected at x if for every neighbourhood U of x there is a connected neighbourhood V of x contained in U; locally connected if this holds at every point. Similarly for locally path connected.
Theorem 25.1 (printed 159). The components of X are connected disjoint subspaces of X whose union is X, such that each non-empty connected subspace of X intersects only one of them.

Theorem 25.2 (printed 160). The path components of X are path-connected disjoint subspaces of X whose union is X, such that each non-empty path-connected subspace of X intersects only one of them.

Theorem 25.3 (printed 161). A space X is locally connected if and only if for every open set U of X, each component of U is open in X.

Theorem 25.4 (printed 161). A space X is locally path connected if and only if for every open set U of X, each path component of U is open in X.

Theorem 25.5 (printed 161). If X is a topological space, each path component of X lies in a component of X. If X is locally path connected, then the components and the path components of X are the same.

Munkres proves the preceding theorem and says of Theorem 25.2 only that its proof "is similar to that of the theorem preceding" (printed 160) — the path-connectedness of a path component is not written out, and is Problem 4(b).

That the path relation is an equivalence relation takes more work than the connected one, and Munkres does it carefully on printed 160. Reflexivity is the constant path. Symmetry is g(t) = f(1 − t). Transitivity pastes two paths together — after reparametrising, which is legitimate "from the fact that any two closed intervals in ℝ are homeomorphic" — and the pasted map is continuous by Theorem 18.3, the pasting lemma of top-04, whose two closed pieces here are [0,1] and [1,2] meeting at {1}.

Guided proof. Prove the first clause of Theorem 25.5: each path component of X lies in a component of X.

Then say, in one sentence, what has to be added for the two to coincide, and name the space in which the addition fails.
Model answer Let P be a path component of X. By Theorem 25.2, P is path connected. A path connected space is connected — Munkres's argument on printed 155: any separation would have every path's image, being a continuous image of a connected interval (the earlier interval and continuous-image results), lying wholly on one side by the separation lemma, so no path could join the two sides. So P is a non-empty connected subspace of X.

By Theorem 25.1, each non-empty connected subspace of X intersects only one component. Let C be that component. Since P intersects C and P is connected, P ⊂ C: indeed every point of P is related to every other by a connected subspace (namely P itself), so all of P lies in one equivalence class, which is C. ∎

What has to be added. Local path connectedness. Under it, Theorem 25.4 makes every path component of every open set open, so the path components of X are open; a component C is then a disjoint union of open path components, and if there were more than one, any of them together with the union of the rest would separate C — contradicting the connectedness of C given by Theorem 25.1. Hence each component is a single path component.

Where it fails. The topologist's sine curve S̄, which is not locally path connected at any point of V: one component, two path components. That is the whole distance between Theorem 25.5's first clause and its second.
Faded example — a discrete space, which is what a point cloud gives. By top-06's Problem 1(c), the metric topology of any finite metric space is discrete. So take X discrete with n points.

The connected subsets. If A ⊂ X has two distinct points x and y, then {x} and A − {x} are non-empty, disjoint, open in A and cover A — a separation. So the connected subsets of X are ∅ and the singletons.

⟨your step: read the components off that. Then ask what the image of a path [a,b] → X must be — it is connected by Theorem 23.5 — and deduce the path components.⟩

⟨your step: is X locally path connected? Check the definition against the neighbourhood {x}. Then say what Theorem 25.5 gives here, and how many components there are.⟩

The count comes out at exactly n, which is all the discrete topology knows — and is why a point cloud is analysed through a filtration (lab-02, tda1-01) rather than through its own metric topology.

Both directions. Forwards, take an open U, a component C of U, and a point x ∈ C; find a connected neighbourhood inside U and argue it lies in C. Backwards, given a neighbourhood U of x, name the component of U containing x and check the definition against it.

Then do the same for Theorem 25.4 and say which single word changes in the proof — and check that the word really is the only change, rather than assuming it.

Self-check 3. Which hypothesis does Theorem 25.5's second clause require?

Locally path connected. Note that connectedness is no help at all: S̄ is connected and still has two path components. That is exactly why the mission strip needs its parenthetical clause about finite complexes rather than resting on connectedness.

Self-check 4. Munkres says components are always closed, and open when there are finitely many. Why the second clause?

A component's complement is the union of the other components, each closed; with finitely many, that union is closed by Theorem 17.1(3), so the component is open. With infinitely many the union need not be closed — ℚ is the example, where every component is a point and none is open. The last option confuses "finitely many components" with the general case.

Self-check 5. In the topologist's sine curve, which path component is open and which is closed?

S is open and not closed, V is closed and not open — Munkres's Example 2, printed 160, which is his illustration of the remark that path components "need be neither open nor closed". The second option cannot be right for any connected space: two clopen pieces partitioning S̄ would be a separation, and S̄ is connected.
Blank page. Close the file, look away, and reconstruct the argument skeleton. Three claims, and why each forces the next.
Check against this 1. Connectedness is the absence of a separation, and Lemma 23.2 is what makes it usable. It converts "this piece is connected" into "this piece sits wholly on one side", which is the engine of Theorems 23.3 and 23.4 — union with a common point, and anything between a set and its closure. Theorem 23.5 carries connectedness through continuous maps and Theorem 23.6 through finite products.

2. ℝ is connected because it is a linear continuum, and that single fact is what the IVT is. Theorem 24.3 separates f(X) by the two open rays around the missing value r; the domain's connectedness enters only through Theorem 23.5. Paths are then maps out of connected intervals, so path connected ⇒ connected — and the converse fails, with the ordered square and the topologist's sine curve as Munkres's two witnesses.

3. Components and path components are different partitions, and one hypothesis fuses them. Theorem 25.1 and Theorem 25.2 build both; Theorem 25.5 says each path component lies in a component, and that they coincide when X is locally path connected — Theorems 25.3 and 25.4 being the statements that make local connectedness equivalent to openness of the pieces. Without the hypothesis: S̄ has one component and two path components. So any claim that "the path pieces are the connected pieces" is a claim about the space, not about topology.
