Separation axioms and normal spaces (top-11)

Can points be told apart by open sets? Hausdorff and beyond — a ladder of how polite a space is. It is a ladder in strength: normal implies regular implies Hausdorff, once one-point sets are closed. It is emphatically not a ladder in behaviour, and that is the surprise of these two sections. The two lower rungs survive subspaces and products; the top rung does not, and Munkres opens §32 by calling the name "normal" a misnomer for exactly that reason.

Predict before we start. Hausdorff and regularity both pass to subspaces and to arbitrary products. Predict whether normality — the strongest of the three — does too.

No. Immediately after Theorem 31.2 Munkres writes: "There is no analogous theorem for normal spaces, as we shall see shortly, in this section and the next." ℝ_ℓ is normal (Example 2, printed 198) and the Sorgenfrey plane ℝ_ℓ² is not (Example 3). Strength and good behaviour come apart here, which is the point of the diagram below.

Why this matters for the mission: Hausdorffness is the sanity axiom persistence diagrams and their limits silently assume.

The strip is an expectation about an object §31 never constructs.

There are no persistence diagrams in §31 or §32. For "diagram space is Hausdorff" to be a theorem rather than an assumption, someone must first put a topology on that space — and tda1-05, already shipped, established that neither of its own sources defines a metric on diagrams, deferring it to tda1-06. So the strip names a property of a space this module does not build, and the word "silently" is doing real work: the assumption is invisible precisely because the object is introduced later.

What §§31–32 do provide is the route, once the object exists. Theorem 32.2 says metrizable ⇒ normal, and the ladder gives normal ⇒ regular ⇒ Hausdorff. So a metric on diagram space would settle the strip in one line. Until then the strip is a prediction about tda1-06, not a consequence of Munkres.

And the sample, again. A point cloud is discrete (top-06), metrized by its own metric, so distinct points x ≠ y sit in disjoint balls of radius d(x,y)/2: it is Hausdorff. That is the eighth property in this module to come out true of a point cloud by an argument that finiteness settles in advance.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

The ladder, and the clause that makes it one

Definition (Munkres, printed 195). Suppose that one-point sets are closed in X. Then X is said to be regular if for each pair consisting of a point x and a closed set B disjoint from x, there exist disjoint open sets containing x and B, respectively. The space X is said to be normal if for each pair A, B of disjoint closed sets of X, there exist disjoint open sets containing A and B, respectively.

The opening clause is not decoration. Munkres explains it in a parenthesis on the same page: without it, "A two-point space in the indiscrete topology satisfies the other part of the definitions of regularity and normality, even though it is not Hausdorff." Check that against the space {a,b} with topology {∅, X}: the only closed sets are ∅ and X, so the only (point, disjoint closed set) pair has the closed set empty, and ∅ together with X separates them vacuously. With the clause in place, regular ⇒ Hausdorff and normal ⇒ regular; without it, neither implication holds.

He also draws a line back to top-07. These axioms are called separation axioms because they involve separating sets by disjoint open sets — but in the connectedness sense the two open sets had to have union the whole space, and here "the open sets need not satisfy this condition" (printed 196). ℝ is regular and connected, so it separates points from closed sets in one sense and admits no separation at all in the other.

Lemma 31.1 (printed 196). Let X be a topological space. Let one-point sets in X be closed.
(a) X is regular if and only if given a point x of X and a neighbourhood U of x, there is a neighbourhood V of x such that V̄ ⊂ U.
(b) X is normal if and only if given a closed set A and an open set U containing A, there is an open set V containing A such that V̄ ⊂ U.

Munkres proves (a) and disposes of (b) in one sentence: "This proof uses exactly the same argument; one just replaces the point x by the set A throughout." Problem 1(c) makes you do the replacement and check that claim.
Theorem 31.2 (printed 196).
(a) A subspace of a Hausdorff space is Hausdorff; a product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff.
(b) A subspace of a regular space is regular; a product of regular spaces is regular.

And then, in Munkres's own words: "There is no analogous theorem for normal spaces, as we shall see shortly, in this section and the next."
Worked example — where Theorem 31.2(b)'s product proof spends its hypotheses. Let {X_α} be regular and X = ∏X_α. By part (a), X is Hausdorff, so one-point sets are closed and Lemma 31.1(a) is available.

Take x ∈ X and a neighbourhood U. Choose a basis element ∏U_α about x inside U. For each α pick a neighbourhood V_α of x_α with V̄_α ⊂ U_α — regularity, via Lemma 31.1(a) — and then Munkres adds a clause that reads like housekeeping: "if it happens that U_α = X_α, choose V_α = X_α."

That clause is load-bearing. By top-05's Theorem 19.1 a product-topology basis element must have U_α = X_α for all but finitely many α. Without the clause you would be free to pick a proper V_α in infinitely many coordinates, and then V = ∏V_α would not be open in the product topology at all — the construction would leave the topology it is working in.

And then one citation does the rest. "Since V̄ = ∏V̄_α by Theorem 19.5, it follows at once that V̄ ⊂ ∏U_α ⊂ U." That is top-05's identification of the closure of a product with the product of closures — an equality, not merely an inclusion, which is why the coordinatewise choices assemble into a statement about the single set V. Lemma 31.1(a) then returns the verdict: X is regular.

Self-check 1. Why do Munkres's definitions of regular and normal begin "Suppose that one-point sets are closed in X"?

Munkres gives the counterexample himself: the two-point indiscrete space satisfies the separation clauses vacuously — its only closed sets are ∅ and X — and is not Hausdorff. The clause is what makes the ladder a ladder.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Three ways to be normal

Theorem 32.1 (printed 200). Every regular space with a countable basis is normal.

Theorem 32.2 (printed 202). Every metrizable space is normal.

Theorem 32.3 (printed 202). Every compact Hausdorff space is normal.

Theorem 32.4 (printed 203). Every well-ordered set X is normal in the order topology.

Munkres introduces these as "three important sets of hypotheses under which normality of a space is assured", and prefaces §32 with a judgement worth keeping: "In one sense, the term 'normal' is something of a misnomer, for normal spaces are not as well-behaved as one might wish. On the other hand, most of the spaces with which we are familiar do satisfy this axiom." Both halves are true at once, and the four theorems are the second half.

Faded example — Theorem 32.1's trick, and why it needs the counting. Let X be regular with a countable basis, and A, B disjoint closed sets. Each x ∈ A has a neighbourhood missing B; shrink it by Lemma 31.1(a); then take a basis element inside. Since the basis is countable, the resulting cover of A is countable — index it {U_n}, with each Ū_n missing B. Symmetrically get {V_n} covering B with each V̄_n missing A.

The unions ⋃U_n and ⋃V_n need not be disjoint. Munkres's trick:
U′_n = U_n − ⋃_{i ≤ n} V̄_i, V′_n = V_n − ⋃_{i ≤ n} Ū_i.

⟨your step: show each U′_n is open. Which fact about a finite union of closed sets are you using, and where did the finiteness come from?⟩

⟨your step: show {U′_n} still covers A. The reason is short — what is true of every point of A with respect to every V̄_i?⟩

⟨your step: show U′ = ⋃U′_n and V′ = ⋃V′_n are disjoint, by supposing x ∈ U′_j ∩ V′_k and splitting on j ≤ k. Write out both contradictions.⟩

Where the countable basis is spent. Not on the shrinking — that is regularity — but on the indexing. Without a countable cover there is no "⋃_{i ≤ n}", and the sets being subtracted would not be finite unions, so U′_n need not be open. That single step is why second countability appears in the hypothesis of a theorem about separation.
Guided proof. Prove Theorem 32.3: every compact Hausdorff space is normal.

Use top-08's results rather than re-running their proofs, and say at each step which one you are invoking.
Model answer Let X be compact Hausdorff and let A, B be disjoint closed subsets.

One-point sets are closed. X is Hausdorff, so by Theorem 17.8 every finite point set is closed — the hypothesis Munkres's definition of normality requires.

A and B are compact. Each is closed in the compact space X, hence compact by Theorem 26.2.

Separate a point from B. Fix a ∈ A. For each b ∈ B, Hausdorffness gives disjoint open U_b ∋ a and V_b ∋ b. The V_b cover the compact B, so finitely many V_{b₁}, …, V_{b_n} do; put V^a = V_{b₁} ∪ ⋯ ∪ V_{b_n} and U^a = U_{b₁} ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_{b_n}. Then U^a is open — a finite intersection — contains a, and is disjoint from V^a ⊃ B. This is exactly the argument Theorem 26.3 uses to show a compact subspace of a Hausdorff space is closed, run one level up.

Separate A from B. The sets U^a for a ∈ A cover the compact A, so finitely many U^{a₁}, …, U^{a_m} do. Put U = U^{a₁} ∪ ⋯ ∪ U^{a_m} and V = V^{a₁} ∩ ⋯ ∩ V^{a_m}. Then U is open and contains A; V is open — again a finite intersection — and contains B, since each V^{a_i} does; and U ∩ V = ∅, because a point of U lies in some U^{a_i}, which is disjoint from V^{a_i} ⊇ V. ∎

The shape. Compactness is used twice, and each time it is the finiteness of the subcover that makes an intersection of open sets open. That is the same expenditure as in top-08's tube lemma and in Theorem 26.3, and it is worth recognising as one move rather than three.

Self-check 2. Which theorem gives you that every metric space is normal?

Theorem 32.2. The first option's premise is false — a metric space need not be second-countable, as top-10's Example 2 showed for the uniform ℝω — so 32.1 does not cover the general case. Theorem 32.3 needs compactness, which metric spaces need not have.

Self-check 3. In Theorem 32.1's proof, what is the countable basis actually used for?

The indexing. Shrinking is regularity, via Lemma 31.1(a). The countable basis is what makes the covers of A and B countable, and hence what makes ⋃_{i ≤ n} V̄_i a finite union of closed sets — which is what keeps U′_n open.

The examples that separate the rungs

Munkres separates the three axioms with two spaces already in hand from earlier units. ℝ_K is Hausdorff and not regular (Example 1, printed 197): distinct points sit in disjoint open intervals, but the set K = {1/n} is closed in ℝ_K and cannot be separated from the point 0, because every basis element about 0 has the form (a,b) − K and every open set containing K contains intervals about the 1/n that reach into it.

ℝ_ℓ is normal (Example 2, printed 198): for disjoint closed A, B choose [a, x_a) missing B for each a ∈ A and [b, x_b) missing A for each b ∈ B, and take unions. The Sorgenfrey plane ℝ_ℓ² is not normal (Example 3, printed 198). So normality is lost by squaring a normal space, which is what Munkres promised when he denied the analogue of Theorem 31.2.

You try. Verify the ℝ_K half — that K is closed and that 0 and K cannot be separated.

First show ℝ_K − K is open, treating x ≠ 0 and x = 0 separately; the second case is where the K-topology's extra basis elements earn their place. Then suppose U ∋ 0 and V ⊇ K are open and disjoint, take a basis element (a,b) − K ⊂ U, and find a point of V inside it — for large n the point 1/n lies in (a,b), and any basis element of V about 1/n must contain points just below it.

Then answer the structural question: which rung does this example separate from which, and why can the same space not be used to separate normal from regular?

Check against this 1. The ladder needs its clause. Regular ⇒ Hausdorff and normal ⇒ regular hold only because both definitions begin by requiring one-point sets to be closed; the two-point indiscrete space satisfies the separation clauses vacuously and is not Hausdorff. Lemma 31.1 restates both axioms as shrinking conditions — a neighbourhood whose closure fits inside — which is the form every later proof uses, and its own proof is Theorem 17.5(a) applied once, to see that a point with a neighbourhood missing V lies outside V̄.

2. Strength and good behaviour come apart at the top rung. Theorem 31.2 carries Hausdorff and regular through subspaces and arbitrary products, the product argument leaning on Theorem 19.5's equality of closures and on a clause that keeps V inside the product topology. Normality passes through neither: ℝ_ℓ is normal and ℝ_ℓ² is not. Munkres calls the name a misnomer for this reason.

3. Normality is nonetheless common, by four routes. Regular with a countable basis (32.1, where the basis is spent on indexing so that ⋃_{i≤n} is finite), metrizable (32.2), compact Hausdorff (32.3, where compactness is spent twice on making finite intersections open), well-ordered in the order topology (32.4). And ℝ_K separates Hausdorff from regular, so the rungs are genuinely distinct.
