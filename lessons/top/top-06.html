The metric topology (top-06)

Every metric space is a topological space — so all of an-14 was secretly topology already. The bridge is one line: declare the ε-balls a basis, and the topology follows. What is worth noticing is the traffic on it. Distances give you a topology, always. Topologies give you back a metric only sometimes, and Munkres spends most of §20 finding out for which spaces — the answer for ℝ^J turning out to depend on whether the index set is countable and on which of three topologies you meant.

Predict before we start. A point cloud is a finite set with distances on it — a finite metric space. Predict what topology those distances induce on it.

The discrete topology, and the proof is three lines from the definitions in §20 — take ε to be the distance from x to its nearest neighbour, which is positive because the set is finite, and B(x,ε) = {x}. Every singleton is open. So the metric topology of a point cloud records its cardinality and nothing else: two point clouds with the same number of points have literally the same topology. That is not a defect of the bridge; it is why TDA does not cross it directly.

Why this matters for the mission: Point clouds are metric spaces; this is the bridge from their distances to their topology.

The bridge exists and leads nowhere useful, and §20 is enough to prove it.

The first clause of the strip is exactly right: a point cloud is a finite metric space, and §20's definition applies to it verbatim. The second clause is where the trouble is. Run the construction and you get the discrete topology, every time, for every point cloud, whatever the distances are — see the prediction gate above and Problem 1(c), which proves it from the metric axioms and nothing else. All the geometry is discarded in the crossing.

So "the bridge from their distances to their topology" is a bridge whose far side carries no information. That is not a small caveat: it is the reason the whole apparatus of filtrations exists. TDA does not take the metric topology of a point cloud. It builds a family of spaces indexed by a scale parameter — Vietoris–Rips and Čech complexes in lab-02, filtrations in tda1-01 — so that the distances are read off at every scale rather than collapsed at one.

What §20 and §21 do give the mission. Theorem 21.1 makes the ε-δ language available on any metric space, which is the language stability is written in; and Theorem 21.6, the uniform limit theorem, is where sup-norm control first buys a topological conclusion. Neither mentions data. an-14, named in the hook, is a syllabus unit whose lesson does not exist.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

From distances to open sets

Definition (Munkres, printed 119). A metric on a set X is a function d : X × X → ℝ having the following properties:
(1) d(x,y) ≥ 0 for all x, y ∈ X; equality holds if and only if x = y.
(2) d(x,y) = d(y,x) for all x, y ∈ X.
(3) (Triangle inequality) d(x,y) + d(y,z) ≥ d(x,z), for all x, y, z ∈ X.

The ε-ball centred at x is B_d(x,ε) = {y : d(x,y) < ε}. The collection of all ε-balls, for x ∈ X and ε > 0, is a basis for a topology on X, called the metric topology induced by d.

The basis check has one step that gets used constantly afterwards, so it is worth naming rather than passing over: if y lies in B(x,ε), then the ball centred at y of radius δ = ε − d(x,y) is contained in B(x,ε). The proof is the triangle inequality once. Everything else — condition (2) for a basis, Lemma 20.2, the comparison arguments in Theorem 20.3 — is that step applied twice and a minimum taken.

Theorem 20.1 (printed 121). Let X be a metric space with metric d. Define d̄(x,y) = min{d(x,y), 1}. Then d̄ is a metric that induces the same topology as d. It is called the standard bounded metric corresponding to d.

Lemma 20.2 (printed 122). Let d and d′ be two metrics on X inducing 𝒯 and 𝒯′. Then 𝒯′ is finer than 𝒯 if and only if for each x in X and each ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that B_{d′}(x,δ) ⊂ B_d(x,ε).

Theorem 20.3 (printed 123). The topologies on ℝⁿ induced by the euclidean metric d and the square metric ρ are the same as the product topology on ℝⁿ.

Lemma 20.2 is Lemma 13.3 of top-02 with one extra move. Lemma 13.3 hands you a basis element inside; here you need a ball centred at x inside, and the centring step is exactly the one from the basis check. Theorem 20.3 is then two inequalities — ρ ≤ d ≤ √n·ρ — fed through Lemma 20.2 once each. Note that √n is a constant only because n is finite; that is the first hint of what breaks for infinite products.

Worked example — a point cloud's metric topology is discrete. Let (X, d) be a metric space with X finite and at least two points. Fix x ∈ X and put
ε_x = min{ d(x,y) : y ∈ X, y ≠ x }.

The set being minimised over is finite and non-empty, so the minimum is attained — this is where finiteness is spent — and ε_x > 0 by axiom (1), since each such y differs from x. Now
B(x, ε_x) = {y : d(x,y) < ε_x} = {x},
because any y ≠ x has d(x,y) ≥ ε_x by the definition of the minimum, and the ball is open: the nearest neighbour sits exactly on the boundary and is excluded. So {x} is open. Every subset of X is a union of singletons, hence open. The topology is discrete. ∎

The degenerate cases, so the statement holds without exception. If |X| = 1 the minimum above is over the empty set and the argument does not run — but the only topology on a one-point set is already discrete (and indiscrete; top-01 Problem 2(c)). If X = ∅ there is one topology. So: every finite metric space carries the discrete topology.

What follows. The metric topology of a finite metric space does not depend on d at all. Two point clouds of the same size are homeomorphic. The crossing from distances to topology, taken directly, keeps the cardinality and throws away every distance — which is the content of the picture above and the bound on this unit's mission strip.

Self-check 1. Why is ε_x > 0 in the argument above?

A minimum over a finite non-empty set is attained, and each d(x,y) with y ≠ x is strictly positive by metric axiom (1). Both halves matter: drop finiteness and you get an infimum that may be 0 — which is precisely why an infinite metric space need not be discrete. The last option confuses the point: the points are distinct, that is the hypothesis y ≠ x.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

ℝ^J: three topologies, and which of them a metric can reach

Definition (Munkres, printed 124). Given an index set J and points x = (x_α), y = (y_α) of ℝ^J, define
ρ̄(x,y) = sup{ d̄(x_α, y_α) : α ∈ J },
where d̄ is the standard bounded metric on ℝ. This is the uniform metric on ℝ^J, and the topology it induces is the uniform topology.
Theorem 20.4 (printed 124). The uniform topology on ℝ^J is finer than the product topology and coarser than the box topology; these three topologies are all different if J is infinite.

Theorem 20.5 (printed 125). Let d̄(a,b) = min{|a−b|, 1} be the standard bounded metric on ℝ. If x and y are two points of ℝ^ω, define D(x,y) = sup{ d̄(x_i, y_i) / i }. Then D is a metric that induces the product topology on ℝ^ω.

Of the preceding theorem's last clause Munkres writes: "Showing these three topologies are different if J is infinite is a task we leave to the exercises" (printed 125). The strictness is stated here and proved nowhere in §20; Problem 3(b) does one half of it.
Faded example — where the finiteness clause is spent in Theorem 20.4. Take a product-topology basis element ∏U_α about a point x. By top-05's Theorem 19.1 there are only finitely many indices α₁,…,α_n with U_α ≠ ℝ. For each i choose ε_i > 0 with the ε_i-ball about x_{α_i} in the d̄ metric inside U_{α_i}, and put ε = min{ε₁,…,ε_n}.

⟨your step: show that B_ρ̄(x, ε) ⊂ ∏U_α. Take z with ρ̄(x,z) < ε and say what that gives you about d̄(x_α, z_α) for every α — including the α where U_α = ℝ, which need no argument at all.⟩

⟨your step: now say in one sentence why the step "put ε = min{ε₁,…,ε_n}" is the one that needs Theorem 19.1, and what would go wrong with an infimum over infinitely many ε_i.⟩

This is the same shape as top-05's Theorem 19.6 — the product topology's finiteness clause is what lets a construction over finitely many coordinates control all of them at once.

Munkres records on printed 125 what is and is not settled: "the only one of these cases where ℝ^J is metrizable is the case where J is countable and ℝ^J has the product topology. As we shall see." Theorem 20.5 proves the positive half by exhibiting the metric D. The negative half — that ℝ^J is not metrizable in the box topology for infinite J, nor in the product topology for uncountable J — is asserted here and not proved in §20.

Self-check 2. Which topology on ℝ^ω does Theorem 20.5 show to be metrizable?

The product topology. Note the trap in the third option: ρ̄ is a metric, so the uniform topology is metrizable by definition — but that is not what Theorem 20.5 says, and the uniform topology is a different topology from the product one on ℝ^ω by Theorem 20.4. The fourth option contradicts Theorem 20.4's last clause outright.

Self-check 3. Theorem 20.3 uses the bound d ≤ √n·ρ. What fails when n is replaced by an infinite index set?

√n is a constant for each fixed n, so δ = ε/√n serves uniformly. Let n grow and the constant grows without limit, so Lemma 20.2's δ-for-every-ε cannot be produced. This is the arithmetic behind Munkres's statement that ℝ^J is not metrizable in the box topology for infinite J.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Epsilons return, sequences almost suffice, and uniform limits

Theorem 21.1 (printed 129). Let f : X → Y with X and Y metrizable with metrics d_X and d_Y. Then continuity of f is equivalent to the requirement that given x ∈ X and ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that d_X(x,y) < δ ⟹ d_Y(f(x), f(y)) < ε.

Lemma 21.2, the sequence lemma (printed 130). Let X be a topological space and A ⊂ X. If there is a sequence of points of A converging to x, then x ∈ Ā; the converse holds if X is metrizable.

Theorem 21.3 (printed 130). Let f : X → Y. If f is continuous then for every convergent sequence x_n → x in X, the sequence f(x_n) converges to f(x). The converse holds if X is metrizable.

This is the debt top-04 left. There Munkres said the ε-δ definition and the convergent-sequence definition "do not generalize to arbitrary spaces; they will be treated when we study metric spaces" (printed 104). Theorem 21.1 and Theorem 21.3 are the payment, and both come with the hypothesis attached rather than hidden.

The hypotheses are worth reading closely. Lemma 21.2's easy half needs nothing; the converse needs X metrizable, and uses it only to supply the shrinking balls B_d(x, 1/n) to choose points from. Theorem 21.3's converse then inherits the same hypothesis on X, and Munkres notes parenthetically in the proof that "metrizability of Y is not needed" — the sequence lemma is applied in the domain only.

He then draws the moral himself, on printed 130: "in proving Lemma 21.2 and Theorem 21.3 we did not use the full strength of the hypothesis that the space X is metrizable. All we really needed was the countable collection B_d(x, 1/n) of balls about x." That observation becomes the first countability axiom — X has a countable basis at x if there are neighbourhoods {U_n} of x such that any neighbourhood of x contains at least one U_n, and X is first countable if this holds at every point. The repair to Lemma 21.2's proof is to replace B_d(x, 1/n) by B_n = U₁ ∩ U₂ ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_n. The intersection is taken rather than using U_n directly for one reason: the U_n need not shrink, and the B_n are decreasing by construction, which is what makes "for all i ≥ N" work.

Guided proof. Prove Lemma 21.2, both halves, and mark the exact sentence in which metrizability is used.
Model answer Forwards, with no hypothesis on X. Suppose x_n → x with every x_n ∈ A. Let U be a neighbourhood of x. By the definition of convergence there is N with x_n ∈ U for all n ≥ N; in particular U contains a point of A. Since U was an arbitrary neighbourhood, Theorem 17.5(a) gives x ∈ Ā.

Converse, X metrizable. Let d be a metric for the topology of X and let x ∈ Ā. For each positive integer n the ball B_d(x, 1/n) is a neighbourhood of x, so by Theorem 17.5(a) it meets A; choose x_n ∈ B_d(x, 1/n) ∩ A. That sentence is the whole use of metrizability — it is what supplies a countable family of neighbourhoods shrinking to x.

Now x_n → x. Let U be any open set containing x. Since the balls form a basis, U contains some B_d(x, ε) centred at x. Choose N with 1/N < ε. Then for i ≥ N we have x_i ∈ B_d(x, 1/i) ⊂ B_d(x, 1/N) ⊂ B_d(x, ε) ⊂ U. So U contains x_i for all i ≥ N, which is convergence. ∎

What the hypothesis is really doing. Not metrics — countability. Replace B_d(x, 1/n) throughout by B_n = U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ U_n for a countable basis {U_n} at x and the argument is unchanged, which is Munkres's own remark and the reason he defines first countability at this point rather than in Chapter 4. A space with no countable basis at some point can have a point in Ā that is the limit of no sequence from A at all.
Lemma 21.4 (printed 131). The addition, subtraction and multiplication operations are continuous functions from ℝ × ℝ into ℝ; and the quotient operation is a continuous function from ℝ × (ℝ − {0}) into ℝ.

Theorem 21.5 (printed 131). If X is a topological space and f, g : X → ℝ are continuous, then f + g, f − g and f·g are continuous. If g(x) ≠ 0 for all x, then f/g is continuous.

Theorem 21.6, the uniform limit theorem (printed 132). Let f_n : X → Y be a sequence of continuous functions from the topological space X to the metric space Y. If (f_n) converges uniformly to f, then f is continuous.

Munkres does not prove Lemma 21.4: "You have probably seen this lemma proved before; it is a standard 'ε-δ argument.' If not, a proof is outlined in Exercise 12 below" (printed 131). It is Problem 5(a) here. Note also that it is a statement about maps out of a product — the case top-04 warned had no general criterion — which is exactly why it needs a hands-on ε-δ argument rather than a theorem.

Theorem 21.5's proof is four lines and reuses top-04 twice. The map h(x) = f(x) × g(x) into ℝ × ℝ is continuous by Theorem 18.4, since its coordinate functions are f and g; then f + g is the composite of h with the addition map, continuous by Theorem 18.2(c). So the arithmetic of continuous real-valued functions is not a new fact about ℝ at all — it is Lemma 21.4 plus two rules already in hand.

Worked example — Theorem 21.6, and where each ε/3 comes from. Let V be open in Y and x₀ ∈ f⁻¹(V); write y₀ = f(x₀). We find a neighbourhood U of x₀ with f(U) ⊂ V.

Choose ε with B(y₀, ε) ⊂ V. Using uniform convergence, choose N so that d(f_n(x), f(x)) < ε/3 for all n ≥ N and all x ∈ X. Using continuity of f_N, choose a neighbourhood U of x₀ with f_N(U) ⊂ B(f_N(x₀), ε/3).

Then for x ∈ U:
d(f(x), f_N(x)) < ε/3 (by the choice of N — at the point x),
d(f_N(x), f_N(x₀)) < ε/3 (by the choice of U),
d(f_N(x₀), f(x₀)) < ε/3 (by the choice of N — at the point x₀).
The triangle inequality applied twice gives d(f(x), y₀) < ε, so f(U) ⊂ B(y₀, ε) ⊂ V. ∎

Which hypothesis does the work. The single N serves at two different points, x and x₀, and x was not known when N was chosen. That is exactly what uniform convergence supplies and pointwise convergence does not: an N independent of the point. Delete the words "and all x" from the definition and the first and third inequalities can no longer be had together.
You try. Munkres remarks that "uniformity of convergence depends not only on the topology of Y but also on its metric" (printed 131).

Make it precise. By Theorem 20.1, a metric d and its standard bounded companion d̄ = min{d, 1} induce the same topology on Y. Find a sequence f_n and a limit f for which convergence is uniform in one of the two metrics and not in the other. Then say which of d, d̄ can host the failure, and why the other cannot.

Finally, reconcile this with Theorem 21.6: if the hypothesis is metric-dependent, in what sense is the conclusion not? Answer by pointing at the definition of "continuous".

Self-check 4. In Theorem 21.3's converse, whose metrizability is needed?

X's only. The converse applies Lemma 21.2 twice, both times in the domain — once to get a sequence in A converging to x, once to conclude f(x) ∈ f(A)‾ — and the second use is the easy half of the lemma, which needs no hypothesis. Munkres flags it parenthetically: "(Note that metrizability of Y is not needed.)"

Self-check 5. Why is Lemma 21.4 not a corollary of Theorem 18.4?

Theorem 18.4 characterises maps into a product by their coordinate functions. Addition goes ℝ × ℝ → ℝ — out of a product — which top-04 recorded as the case with no useful criterion. So Lemma 21.4 has to be done by hand, and Munkres outsources it to Exercise 12. Addition is continuous in each variable separately, but by top-04's Exercise 12 that would prove nothing.

Check against this 1. Every metric induces a topology, and the induced topology can be much less than the metric. The ε-balls are a basis, by the triangle inequality applied once; Theorem 20.1 shows a metric can be replaced by a bounded one without changing the topology at all, and the finite case shows the collapse at its most extreme — every finite metric space is discrete, so a point cloud's metric topology records only how many points there are.

2. Going the other way is hard, and ℝ^J is where you see it. Theorem 20.3 metrizes ℝⁿ two ways and identifies the result with the product topology, on a bound d ≤ √n·ρ whose constant is finite only because n is. Theorem 20.4 places a third topology, the uniform, strictly between product and box for infinite J; Theorem 20.5 metrizes the product topology on ℝ^ω with D = sup{d̄(x_i,y_i)/i}; and Munkres states, without proving it here, that these are the only metrizable cases.

3. With a metric in the domain, sequences and epsilons come back — almost. Theorem 21.1 restores ε-δ; Lemma 21.2 and Theorem 21.3 restore sequences, but only for metrizable X, and the real hypothesis is countability rather than metrics, which is why first countability is defined at this point. Theorem 21.5 builds continuous functions arithmetically off Lemma 21.4 — a map out of a product, hence proved by hand — and Theorem 21.6 shows that uniform convergence, an ε/3 argument in which one N serves at two points at once, preserves continuity.
