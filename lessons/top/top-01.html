Topological spaces and open sets (top-01)

Forget distance. Keep only which sets are open — and almost all of analysis still works. That is not a slogan anyone arrived at quickly: Munkres opens §12 by recording that Fréchet, Hausdorff and others spent the first decades of the twentieth century proposing rival definitions before the community settled on one, wanting it broad enough to cover euclidean space, infinite-dimensional euclidean space and function spaces all at once, and narrow enough that the standard theorems would still hold. What they landed on is three lines long, and two of those lines are about unions and intersections.

Predict before we start. The third axiom will ask that finite intersections of open sets be open. Is that word "finite" doing real work, or is it there for tidiness — could the axiom just as well have said "arbitrary"?

Real work, and it collapses fast. In segment 2 you will intersect countably many open sets of ℤ — each the complement of a single point — and land on a set that is not open. Countability is not the threshold either: a countably infinite family is already enough to break it.

Why this matters for the mission: A topology is the minimal structure on which continuity, and therefore homology, can be defined.

The strip reaches past this unit, and past this section. Three bounds, all read off §12 itself.

Continuity is not here. §12 defines the structure and stops. Munkres defines a continuous function in §18 (printed 102), which is top-04's subject; nothing in §12 on printed 75–78 mentions continuity except the chapter's opening promise that it is coming.

Homology is much further off. Munkres reaches homology only in Part II and only for surfaces (§75, printed 454). It is at1-07 that builds it in general. Nothing in this unit licenses the word.

"Minimal" is not Munkres's claim. His own account of how the definition was chosen (printed 75–76) is a tradeoff, not a minimality theorem: the mathematicians involved "wanted, of course, a definition that was as broad as possible" so as to include the useful examples, "but they also wanted the definition to be narrow enough that the standard theorems about these familiar spaces would hold". Breadth traded against strength, settled by practice over decades. §12 proves nothing about structures weaker than a topology, and does not claim none would serve.

What this unit does establish is the middle clause of the strip made concrete: what the structure is, and how to tell whether something is one.
Segment 1 · ~25 min

Three axioms, and what they refuse

Definition (Munkres, printed 76). A topology on a set X is a collection 𝒯 of subsets of X having the following properties:
(1) ∅ and X are in 𝒯.
(2) The union of the elements of any subcollection of 𝒯 is in 𝒯.
(3) The intersection of the elements of any finite subcollection of 𝒯 is in 𝒯.

A set X for which a topology 𝒯 has been specified is called a topological space. A subset U of X is an open set of X if U belongs to 𝒯.

Read what that does not say. There is no distance, no metric, no sequence, no limit. "Open" is not a property a set has; it is a label the collection 𝒯 hands out, and a different 𝒯 on the same X hands it out differently. Munkres's own phrasing on printed 76 is worth keeping: a topological space is a set X together with a collection of subsets of X, called open sets, such that ∅ and X are both open and such that arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open.

Two conventions are buried in axioms (2) and (3), and both surface in the problems.

The empty subcollection. Axiom (2) says "any subcollection", and the empty subcollection is one of them. The union over no sets is ∅; the intersection over no sets, taken inside X, is X. So axioms (2) and (3) already deliver what axiom (1) asks for. The axioms overlap; they do not conflict. Axiom (1) is still worth stating separately, because it is the one a candidate collection fails without any set arithmetic at all — see the collection 𝒟 in Problem 1.

Finite means: check two. Axiom (3) quantifies over finite subcollections, but you never have to verify more than pairs.

Lemma (pairs suffice). Let 𝒯 be a collection of subsets of X containing ∅ and X and closed under arbitrary unions. If U ∩ V ∈ 𝒯 whenever U, V ∈ 𝒯, then 𝒯 is a topology on X.

Proof. Axioms (1) and (2) are hypotheses. For axiom (3), induct on the number n of members. For n = 0 the intersection is X ∈ 𝒯, and for n = 1 it is the member itself. Suppose every intersection of n − 1 members lies in 𝒯, and let U₁, …, Uₙ ∈ 𝒯. Then U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ Uₙ = (U₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ Uₙ₋₁) ∩ Uₙ. The bracket lies in 𝒯 by the inductive hypothesis, and the whole is an intersection of two members of 𝒯, hence in 𝒯. ∎

Only the last paragraph needs the pairwise hypothesis; the union hypothesis is carried purely to supply axiom (2), so that the conclusion can be the whole definition rather than one third of it.

This is a sub-step, not one of Munkres's results — §12 numbers nothing at all. It is stated here because Problem 1(c) asks for it and because every verification below quietly uses it.
Worked example — running the checklist, and naming the witness. Let X = {a, b, c} and consider
𝒞 = {∅, X, {a,b}, {b,c}}.

Axiom (1). ∅ ∈ 𝒞 and X ∈ 𝒞. Pass.
Axiom (3), by the Lemma, so pairs only. The pairs involving ∅ give ∅, and those involving X give the other member; both are in 𝒞. The one remaining pair is {a,b} ∩ {b,c} = {b}. And {b} ∉ 𝒞. Fail, witnessed by {a,b} and {b,c}.

Stop there. A collection that fails an axiom is not a topology, and the remaining checks tell you nothing you can use. Note the shape of the answer: not "𝒞 is not a topology" but "𝒞 fails axiom (3), and here are the two sets". The witness is the part that is checkable.

Order of attack. Run (1), then (3), then (2). Axiom (1) is a glance. Axiom (3) needs only pairs and usually breaks first, because a collection built by hand tends to have few members and their overlaps are unplanned. Axiom (2) is the long check, so do it last and only if the others survive.

Self-check 1. Which of these is a topology on X = {a, b, c}?

The second. The first fails (2): {a} ∪ {b} = {a,b} is absent. The third fails (1): ∅ is missing — and note it is closed under both unions and intersections as it stands, which is exactly why axiom (1) must be stated rather than derived. The fourth is 𝒞 above, failing (3). For the winner, the pairwise intersections are {b} ∩ {a,b} = {b}, {b} ∩ {b,c} = {b} and {a,b} ∩ {b,c} = {b}, all present; the unions are {a,b} ∪ {b,c} = X and the rest absorb into X.

Self-check 2. Munkres writes 𝒯′ ⊃ 𝒯 for "𝒯′ is finer than 𝒯". Does that permit 𝒯′ = 𝒯?

Yes. Munkres's ⊂ means "is a subset of", and he reserves a separate phrase for the proper case: "if 𝒯′ properly contains 𝒯, we say that 𝒯′ is strictly finer" (printed 77). Every topology is therefore finer than itself, which is what makes "finer" reflexive in Problem 5(a). This is a live reading trap — many later authors use ⊂ for proper containment.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Four topologies that live on every set

Munkres's Examples 2–4 (printed 77) are not illustrations. They are the supply of counterexamples the rest of the subject runs on, and three of the five problems are set inside them.

Examples 2–4 (Munkres, printed 77).
Discrete topology: for any set X, the collection of all subsets of X.
Indiscrete (or trivial) topology: the collection consisting of X and ∅ only.
Finite complement topology 𝒯_f: all subsets U of X such that X − U either is finite or is all of X.
Countable complement topology 𝒯_c: all subsets U of X such that X − U either is countable or is all of X.

The two-clause shape of 𝒯_f and 𝒯_c is easy to skim past and it is load-bearing. The second clause, "or is all of X", is there for exactly one set: U = ∅, whose complement is X and need not be finite. Without it, ∅ would not be open and axiom (1) would fail on the spot.

Worked example — 𝒯_f is a topology, with De Morgan doing the work. Munkres's argument, printed 77, in full.

Axiom (1). X ∈ 𝒯_f since X − X = ∅ is finite; ∅ ∈ 𝒯_f since X − ∅ = X is all of X, by the second clause.

Axiom (2). Let {U_α} be a family of nonempty members. Then
X − ⋃ U_α = ⋂ (X − U_α),
and this is a subset of any single one of the finite sets X − U_α, hence finite. So ⋃ U_α ∈ 𝒯_f. The fact used is that a subset of a finite set is finite — and it says nothing about how many α there are, which is precisely why axiom (2) may be stated for arbitrary subcollections.

Axiom (3). Let U₁, …, Uₙ be nonempty members. Then
X − ⋂ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Uᵢ = ⋃ᵢ₌₁ⁿ (X − Uᵢ),
a finite union of finite sets, therefore finite. So ⋂ Uᵢ ∈ 𝒯_f. The fact used is that a finite union of finite sets is finite — and this one does depend on n being finite. An infinite union of finite sets can be infinite, and the argument has nothing to fall back on.

Why "nonempty". Munkres states both halves for nonempty members, and the reason is a sentence, not a subtlety: if some U_α = ∅ then X − U_α = X, which is not finite when X is infinite, so "each set X − U_α is finite" is simply false. Dispose of the empty cases by hand. In a union, dropping empty members changes nothing. In an intersection, one empty member makes the whole intersection ∅, which is in 𝒯_f by the second clause. Problem 3(b) asks you to write this out so the proof stands without the restriction.
Faded example — the countably infinite family that breaks axiom (3). Work inside 𝒯_f on X = ℤ.

For each n ≥ 1 put Uₙ = ℤ − {n}. Each Uₙ ∈ 𝒯_f, because ℤ − Uₙ = {n} is finite. Now intersect all of them:
⋂_{n ≥ 1} Uₙ = ℤ − {1, 2, 3, …}, which is {…, −2, −1, 0}.

⟨your step: compute the complement ℤ − ⋂ Uₙ explicitly, then test it against both clauses of the definition of 𝒯_f — is it finite, and is it all of ℤ?⟩

⟨your step: say in one sentence which line of the worked example above fails when n is allowed to run over an infinite index set, and why the union half is untouched by the same substitution.⟩

This is the answer to the prediction gate. The word "finite" in axiom (3) is not tidiness, and a countably infinite family is already enough to break it — no uncountability required.
You try. Munkres gives Example 4 and then says only: "Then 𝒯_c is a topology on X, as you can check." He states it; he does not verify it.

Verify it. Run the same three axioms, following the shape of the 𝒯_f argument above, and identify the one fact about countable sets that has to replace "a finite union of finite sets is finite". Confirm that your replacement is actually true before you lean on it.

Then answer the question Munkres does not ask: is 𝒯_f ⊂ 𝒯_c always? Is the containment ever strict, and for which X? That is Problem 4, and part (c) has a trap in it worth walking into deliberately.

Self-check 3. On a finite set X, what is 𝒯_f?

Discrete. If X is finite then every subset of X is finite, so every X − U is finite, so every subset U is open. The same argument shows 𝒯_c is discrete whenever X is merely countable, which is Problem 4(d).

Self-check 4. Take X = ℤ and D the set of even integers. Where does U = ℤ − D sit?

ℤ − U = D is countably infinite. Countable, so U ∈ 𝒯_c. Infinite and not all of ℤ, so U ∉ 𝒯_f. This is the witness for Problem 4(c), and it is the reason the natural guess there — that 𝒯_f ⊊ 𝒯_c needs X uncountable — is wrong. Any infinite X will do.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Comparing topologies, and a metaphor that only half works

Definition (Munkres, printed 77). Suppose that 𝒯 and 𝒯′ are two topologies on a given set X. If 𝒯′ ⊃ 𝒯, we say that 𝒯′ is finer than 𝒯; if 𝒯′ properly contains 𝒯, we say that 𝒯′ is strictly finer than 𝒯. We also say that 𝒯 is coarser than 𝒯′, or strictly coarser, in these two respective situations. We say 𝒯 is comparable with 𝒯′ if either 𝒯′ ⊃ 𝒯 or 𝒯 ⊃ 𝒯′.

"Finer" is containment of collections. That single observation settles most of Problem 5(a): reflexivity, antisymmetry and transitivity are inherited from ⊆ on sets and need no topology at all. What does not come free is totality — and Munkres denies it in the next paragraph: "Two topologies on X need not be comparable, of course."

The diagram below is the smallest honest picture of that. Five topologies on X = {a, b, c}, ordered upward by fineness; an upward edge means the lower is contained in the upper.

Read the dashed edge, not just the solid ones. The two middle topologies 𝒯₁ = {∅, X, {a}} and 𝒯₂ = {∅, X, {b}} are incomparable: {a} ∉ 𝒯₂ and {b} ∉ 𝒯₁. So there is no answer to "which of these two is finer". They do have a coarsest common refinement — the topology above them — but that is a topology you have to build, and it is strictly larger than the bare union 𝒯₁ ∪ 𝒯₂ = {∅, X, {a}, {b}}, which is not a topology at all: it fails axiom (2), since {a} ∪ {b} = {a,b} is missing. Unions of topologies break; intersections do not. That asymmetry is Problem 5(b) and 5(c).
Guided proof. Let {𝒯ᵢ}ᵢ∈I be a non-empty family of topologies on a set X. Prove that ⋂ᵢ∈I 𝒯ᵢ is a topology on X.

Write out all three axioms. In the axiom (2) step, say explicitly where you use that each 𝒯ᵢ is closed under unions of arbitrary subcollections rather than just finite ones.
Model answer Write 𝒯 = ⋂ᵢ∈I 𝒯ᵢ. Note first that 𝒯 is a collection of subsets of X, since each 𝒯ᵢ is.

Axiom (1). Each 𝒯ᵢ contains ∅ and X, so both lie in the intersection of all of them; hence ∅, X ∈ 𝒯.

Axiom (2). Let {U_α}α∈J be any subcollection of 𝒯. Fix i ∈ I. Every U_α lies in 𝒯, hence in 𝒯ᵢ, so {U_α}α∈J is a subcollection of 𝒯ᵢ — of the same size J, whatever that size is. Because 𝒯ᵢ satisfies axiom (2) for arbitrary subcollections, ⋃α U_α ∈ 𝒯ᵢ. This is the step that needs the full strength of axiom (2): the family we were handed may be of any cardinality, and it must be fed to each 𝒯ᵢ whole. Since i was arbitrary, ⋃α U_α lies in every 𝒯ᵢ and so in 𝒯.

Axiom (3). The same argument with a finite subcollection U₁, …, Uₙ of 𝒯: for each i these all lie in 𝒯ᵢ, so ⋂ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Uᵢ ∈ 𝒯ᵢ by axiom (3) for 𝒯ᵢ, and therefore ⋂ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Uᵢ ∈ 𝒯. (By the Lemma of segment 1 it would have sufficed to do the case n = 2.)

Hence 𝒯 satisfies all three axioms and is a topology on X. ∎

Two remarks. The hypothesis that I is non-empty matters only for bookkeeping: an intersection over no topologies is not defined by this argument, though the convention "everything in the ambient set" would give the discrete topology. And nothing here is special about topologies — the same proof works for any notion defined by closure conditions, which is why intersections behave and unions do not.
Worked example — the two extremes bound everything. Let 𝒯 be any topology on X, let 𝒫 be the discrete topology and 𝒥 the indiscrete.

Every member of 𝒯 is a subset of X, and 𝒫 is by definition the collection of all subsets of X. So 𝒯 ⊂ 𝒫: the discrete topology is finer than every topology on X.
Conversely 𝒥 = {∅, X}, and axiom (1) puts both of those in 𝒯. So 𝒥 ⊂ 𝒯: the indiscrete topology is coarser than every topology on X.

The degenerate case, which Problem 2(c) asks for in full. When do the two extremes coincide? Exactly when X has at most one point. For a one-point set, the subsets are ∅ and X, so both collections are {∅, X}. For X = ∅ the two collections are again equal, and there is a wrinkle worth stating: ∅ and X are then the same set, so the collection {∅, X} = {∅} has one member, not two. Whenever X has at least two points, pick x ∈ X with {x} ≠ X; then {x} is in 𝒫 and not in 𝒥, and the two are distinct.
Faded example — assessing Munkres's gravel. His image, printed 77: a topological space is "something like a truckload full of gravel — the pebbles and all unions of collections of pebbles being the open sets", and smashing the pebbles smaller enlarges the collection of open sets, so the topology "like the gravel, is said to have been made finer".

What it gets right. A topology can be presented by a small generating collection together with all its unions, rather than by listing every open set. That is a real and central idea — it is what §13 will call a basis (top-02) — and the metaphor is placed here to prepare it.

⟨your step: name what the image gets wrong about the shape of the open sets. Gravel pebbles are disjoint; are the generators of a topology? Test the image against 𝒜 = {∅, X, {b}, {a,b}, {b,c}} from Problem 1, whose two two-element members {a,b} and {b,c} overlap in {b} without either containing the other.⟩

⟨your step: name what it gets wrong about the structure of the set of all topologies on X. Two truckloads of gravel can always be ranked by how finely each was smashed. Can two topologies always be ranked? Cite the diagram above, and Munkres's own sentence in the very next paragraph.⟩

Self-check 5. 𝒯₁ = {∅, X, {a}} and 𝒯₂ = {∅, X, {b}} on X = {a,b,c}. Which is true of 𝒯₁ ∩ 𝒯₂ and 𝒯₁ ∪ 𝒯₂?

𝒯₁ ∩ 𝒯₂ = {∅, X}, the indiscrete topology — an instance of the guided proof. 𝒯₁ ∪ 𝒯₂ = {∅, X, {a}, {b}} fails axiom (2), because {a} ∪ {b} = {a,b} is not a member. It is the collection ℬ of Problem 1, met again from a different direction.

One last thing to carry forward, from printed 78. Some authors say "larger"/"smaller" for finer/coarser, which is harmless. Others say "weaker"/"stronger" — and Munkres warns that analysts and topologists use those two words in opposite senses for the same inclusion. He declines to use them at all, and so will we. If you meet "weak topology" in another book, read the surrounding definitions before assuming which containment is meant.

Blank page. Close the file, look away, and reconstruct the argument skeleton from nothing. Three claims, and why each forces the next.
Check against this 1. A topology is three closure conditions on a collection of subsets — contains ∅ and X, closed under arbitrary unions, closed under finite intersections — and "open" means nothing more than "member of that collection". So the same set carries many topologies, and the discrete and indiscrete ones bracket them all.

2. The asymmetry between (2) and (3) is real, and 𝒯_f exhibits it. The union half of the 𝒯_f proof needs only "a subset of a finite set is finite", which is indifferent to how many sets there are; the intersection half needs "a finite union of finite sets is finite", which is not. Intersecting the ℤ − {n} for n ≥ 1 produces a set whose complement is infinite and proper — not open. So axiom (3) could not have said "arbitrary".

3. "Finer" is containment, so the topologies on X form a partial order and not a chain. Reflexivity, antisymmetry and transitivity are free; totality fails, with {∅,X,{a}} and {∅,X,{b}} as witnesses. Intersections of topologies are topologies — the closure conditions are all of the form "if these are in, that is in", and such conditions survive intersection — while unions of topologies generally are not, because a union can supply two sets without supplying what they generate.
