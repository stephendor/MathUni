Continuous functions and homeomorphisms (top-04)

Continuity with no epsilons: preimages of open sets are open. (You proved this shape in an-10.) One line, no metric, no sequences — and Munkres immediately warns what it costs. The ε-δ definition and the convergent-sequence definition "do not generalize to arbitrary spaces; they will be treated when we study metric spaces" (printed 104). So this is not a restatement of the calculus definition dressed up. It is a definition that agrees with it on ℝ, works where it cannot, and is strictly less informative about individual points until you put a metric back.

Predict before we start. Theorem 18.4 will say that a map into a product is continuous exactly when both its coordinate functions are. Predict the dual: is a map out of a product continuous exactly when it is continuous in each variable separately?

No. Munkres's sentence on printed 110 is blunt: "There is no useful criterion for the continuity of a map f : A × B → X whose domain is a product space", and the separate-continuity conjecture "is not true". His Exercise 12 is the witness, and the canvas below draws it. This is the asymmetry at1-01 was relying on when it insisted a homotopy is one jointly continuous map on X × I.

Why this matters for the mission: A homeomorphism is topology's notion of 'same shape' — the equivalence TDA measures everything against.

The strip names the wrong equivalence, and §18 gives the material to say so.

What §18 establishes is precise and worth having. A homeomorphism gives "a bijective correspondence not only between X and Y but between the collections of open sets", so any property "entirely expressed in terms of the topology" of one transfers to the other — Munkres's topological property, printed 105. Homeomorphic spaces are indistinguishable by anything topology can say.

But "the equivalence TDA measures everything against" is not that. The invariants TDA computes are constant on a strictly coarser relation: at1-01, already on the shelf, works with homotopy equivalence. That relation really is coarser, and one line settles it — ℝ deformation retracts onto {0} by f_t(x) = (1 − t)x, so ℝ is homotopy equivalent to a one-point space, while no homeomorphism between them can exist because a homeomorphism is in particular a bijection. Homology cannot distinguish spaces that homotopy equivalence identifies, so it is not measuring against homeomorphism. The implication runs one way only: homeomorphic ⇒ same topological properties ⇒ same homology, and the converse fails in exactly the direction that matters for data.

And there is no TDA in §18. Nothing on printed 102–111 mentions homology, persistence or data. What this unit supplies is the notion of sameness that at1-01 will then deliberately weaken.

Continuity, and how little of it you have to check

Definition (Munkres, printed 102). Let X and Y be topological spaces. A function f : X → Y is said to be continuous if for each open subset V of Y, the set f⁻¹(V) is an open subset of X.

Munkres attaches a caution on the same page: continuity "depends not only upon the function f itself, but also on the topologies specified for its domain and range", and one may say f is continuous relative to specified topologies. That is not pedantry. The identity map on ℝ is continuous in one direction between the standard and lower limit topologies and not in the other, which is his Example 3 (printed 104) and the cleanest instance of the general fact: the identity (X, 𝒯) → (X, 𝒯′) is continuous exactly when 𝒯 is finer than 𝒯′.

Two reductions make the definition workable (printed 103). If Y's topology comes from a basis, it is enough that f⁻¹(B) be open for every basis element, because Lemma 13.1 (top-02) writes V = ⋃B_α, giving f⁻¹(V) = ⋃f⁻¹(B_α). If it comes from a subbasis, it is enough for every subbasis element, because a basis element is a finite intersection S₁ ∩ ⋯ ∩ Sₙ and f⁻¹ of it is f⁻¹(S₁) ∩ ⋯ ∩ f⁻¹(Sₙ). The whole force is that preimage commutes with both unions and intersections — which image does not, and that asymmetry is why the definition is stated with preimages.

Theorem 18.1 (printed 104). Let X and Y be topological spaces; let f : X → Y. Then the following are equivalent:
(1) f is continuous.
(2) For every subset A of X, one has f(Ā) ⊂ f(A)‾.
(3) For every closed set B of Y, the set f⁻¹(B) is closed in X.
(4) For each x ∈ X and each neighbourhood V of f(x), there is a neighbourhood U of x such that f(U) ⊂ V.

If the condition in (4) holds for the point x of X, we say that f is continuous at the point x.
Worked example — (1) ⇒ (2), and why the inclusion is only an inclusion. Assume f continuous and let A ⊂ X. Take x ∈ Ā; we show f(x) ∈ f(A)‾. Let V be a neighbourhood of f(x). Then f⁻¹(V) is open by continuity and contains x, so it is a neighbourhood of x. Since x ∈ Ā, Theorem 17.5(a) says f⁻¹(V) meets A, at some point y. Then f(y) ∈ V ∩ f(A), so V meets f(A). As V was arbitrary, Theorem 17.5(a) gives f(x) ∈ f(A)‾.

Only an inclusion. Take f : ℝ → ℝ, f(x) = arctan x, and A = ℝ. Then Ā = ℝ and f(Ā) = (−π/2, π/2), while f(A)‾ = [−π/2, π/2]. The right-hand side is strictly larger. What went missing is the two endpoints: they are limits of values of f without being values of f, and no amount of continuity supplies them. Equality for every A is a strictly stronger condition — it holds when f is a homeomorphism, by applying (2) to f and to f⁻¹ and combining.

Munkres's proof pattern. He shows (1) ⇒ (2) ⇒ (3) ⇒ (1) and (1) ⇒ (4) ⇒ (1), which is five implications rather than the twelve a naive reading demands. The first cycle makes any of (1), (2), (3) reach any other by composition; the second ties (4) to the cycle in both directions. Choosing the cycle is the whole economy of the proof.

Self-check 1. Which identity map is continuous?

ℝ_ℓ → ℝ. Continuity of the identity (X,𝒯) → (X,𝒯′) means every 𝒯′-open set is 𝒯-open, i.e. 𝒯 is finer — so the finer topology must be on the domain. Munkres's Example 3, printed 104: f⁻¹([a,b)) = [a,b) is not open in ℝ, while g⁻¹((a,b)) = (a,b) is open in ℝ_ℓ. The first option gets the direction exactly backwards, which is the commonest error here.

Self-check 2. Which clause of Theorem 18.1 is the one you would use to prove that the preimage of a closed set is closed?

(3). The whole point of Theorem 18.1 is that these four are interchangeable, so the work is choosing the face that already says what you want. Clause (2) is the awkward one to compute with, because it quantifies over every subset A and both sides are closures.

Homeomorphism: sameness, and three ways it fails

Definition (Munkres, printed 105). Let f : X → Y be a bijection. If both f and f⁻¹ are continuous, f is called a homeomorphism. Equivalently: a bijective correspondence f : X → Y such that f(U) is open if and only if U is open.

A property of X entirely expressed in terms of the topology of X is called a topological property of X. If f : X → Y is injective and continuous and the restriction f : X → f(X) is a homeomorphism onto the image with its subspace topology, then f is a topological imbedding, or simply an imbedding.

Munkres draws the algebraic analogy explicitly: an isomorphism is a bijective correspondence preserving algebraic structure, and a homeomorphism is the topological counterpart. The consequence he actually uses is the one in the middle of the definition — the correspondence is between the collections of open sets, so anything sayable in terms of open sets transfers.

Worked example — a continuous bijection that is not a homeomorphism. Munkres's Example 6, printed 106–107. Let S¹ = {(x,y) : x² + y² = 1} as a subspace of ℝ², and let F : [0,1) → S¹ be F(t) = (cos 2πt, sin 2πt). It is bijective and continuous.

Take U = [0, 1/4). It is open in [0,1): U = [0,1) ∩ (−1/4, 1/4), an intersection of an open set of ℝ with the subspace, so it is open by the definition of the subspace topology (top-02).

Its image F(U) is the quarter-arc from p = F(0) = (1,0) anticlockwise, including p. It is not open in S¹. To see it, note that openness in S¹ means being V ∩ S¹ for some V open in ℝ², so we must show that no open V of ℝ² containing p has V ∩ S¹ ⊂ F(U). But any such V contains a disc about p, and that disc meets S¹ on both sides of p — including at points F(t) with t just below 1, which are not in F(U). So F(U) is not open, F does not carry open sets to open sets, and F is not a homeomorphism.

What the failure is. F is a continuous bijection that has "glued" the two ends of the interval; the inverse has to tear them apart, and tearing is not continuous. Note that nothing is wrong with F — it is the standard way to wrap an interval on a circle, and at1-04 will use it. What is wrong is expecting a continuous bijection to be a homeomorphism.
Faded example — the same map, a different codomain, and Example 7. Munkres expands the range: g : [0,1) → ℝ² is F followed by the inclusion S¹ ↪ ℝ². He calls g "a continuous injective map that is not an imbedding".

g is still continuous, by Theorem 18.2(e) — expanding the range preserves continuity. It is still injective. So what fails?

Self-check 3. Which is a topological property of a subset of ℝ?

Hausdorff — it is stated entirely in terms of open sets, so a homeomorphism transports it. Boundedness is not: (−1,1) and ℝ are homeomorphic by Example 5's F(x) = x/(1 − x²), and exactly one of them is bounded. Length and membership are not even expressible in terms of the topology.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Building continuous maps — and the one place the machinery stops

Theorem 18.2, rules for constructing continuous functions (printed 107–108). Let X, Y, Z be topological spaces.
(a) Constant function. If f maps all of X to a single point y₀, f is continuous.
(b) Inclusion. If A is a subspace of X, the inclusion j : A → X is continuous.
(c) Composites. If f : X → Y and g : Y → Z are continuous, so is g ∘ f.
(d) Restricting the domain. If f : X → Y is continuous and A is a subspace of X, then f|A is continuous.
(e) Restricting or expanding the range. If f(X) ⊂ Z ⊂ Y then X → Z is continuous; if Z has Y as a subspace then X → Z is continuous.
(f) Local formulation. f : X → Y is continuous if X can be written as a union of open sets U_α such that f|U_α is continuous for each α.

Theorem 18.3, the pasting lemma (printed 108). Let X = A ∪ B where A and B are closed in X. Let f : A → Y and g : B → Y be continuous. If f(x) = g(x) for every x ∈ A ∩ B, then f and g combine to give a continuous h : X → Y with h = f on A and h = g on B.

The pasting lemma is proved on closed sets, using Theorem 18.1(3) to work with preimages of closed sets throughout, and top-03's Theorem 17.3 to promote "closed in A" to "closed in X", finishing with Theorem 17.1(3), which makes the union of the two promoted preimages closed. Munkres then notes that the lemma "also holds if A and B are open sets in X; this is just a special case of the local formulation of continuity". The reduction runs one way only: an open two-set cover is literally an instance of 18.2(f)'s hypothesis, whereas a closed cover is not, which is why 18.3 needs its own proof.

His Example 8 (printed 109) is three functions in a row, and the third is the one to remember. h(x) = x for x ≤ 0 and x/2 for x ≥ 0 is continuous: closed pieces, agreeing on the overlap {0}. k(x) = x − 2 for x ≤ 0 and x + 2 for x ≥ 0 is not a function at all — at 0 the two formulas give −2 and 2. And l(x) = x − 2 for x < 0, x + 2 for x ≥ 0 is a function with two continuous pieces, and is not continuous: l⁻¹((1,3)) = [0,1), which is not open. The pieces of l are (−∞,0) and [0,∞) — one open, one closed — so neither version of the lemma applies, and the conclusion duly fails.

Theorem 18.4, maps into products (printed 110). Let f : A → X × Y be given by f(a) = (f₁(a), f₂(a)). Then f is continuous if and only if f₁ : A → X and f₂ : A → Y are continuous. The maps f₁ and f₂ are the coordinate functions of f.
And then the machinery stops. Munkres, immediately after Theorem 18.4, printed 110, in full:

"There is no useful criterion for the continuity of a map f : A × B → X whose domain is a product space. One might conjecture that f is continuous if it is continuous 'in each variable separately,' but this conjecture is not true. (See Exercise 12.)"

Note the asymmetry precisely. Into a product: a clean biconditional, because the basis elements of the product are rectangles and f⁻¹(U × V) splits as f₁⁻¹(U) ∩ f₂⁻¹(V). Out of a product: nothing. The obvious guess is false, and Munkres does not offer a replacement.
Worked example — Exercise 12, the witness. Munkres sets this and does not work it (printed 112). Define F : ℝ × ℝ → ℝ by F(x,y) = xy/(x² + y²) for (x,y) ≠ (0,0), and F(0,0) = 0.

Separately continuous. Fix y₀ ≠ 0. Then x ↦ xy₀/(x² + y₀²) is a quotient of polynomials whose denominator never vanishes, hence continuous. Fix y₀ = 0: then F(x, 0) = 0 for every x, including x = 0, a constant map, continuous by Theorem 18.2(a). By symmetry the same holds in the other variable. So F is continuous in each variable separately, everywhere.

Not continuous. Compute along the diagonal: g(x) = F(x,x) = x²/(x² + x²) = 1/2 for x ≠ 0, while g(0) = F(0,0) = 0. So g is discontinuous at 0. But the diagonal map d(x) = (x,x) is continuous by Theorem 18.4 — its coordinate functions are both the identity — and g = F ∘ d. If F were continuous, g would be continuous by Theorem 18.2(c). It is not. Hence F is not continuous, and the failure is at the origin.

Read the picture. Along either axis F is identically 0. Along the diagonal it is identically 1/2 away from the origin. Every neighbourhood of the origin contains points of both kinds, so F takes both values arbitrarily close to (0,0) and no single value can be its limit there. Separate continuity only ever looks along the two green lines.
Guided proof. Prove that continuity implies separate continuity — so that the failure above is genuinely one-directional.

Precisely: let F : X × Y → Z be continuous and fix y₀ ∈ Y. Show that x ↦ F(x, y₀) is continuous X → Z. Build the map you need out of Theorem 18.4 and Theorem 18.2 rather than checking preimages by hand.
Model answer Define ι : X → X × Y by ι(x) = (x, y₀). Its coordinate functions are the identity 𝟙_X : X → X and the constant map c : X → Y with value y₀. The identity is continuous — it is the inclusion of X in itself, Theorem 18.2(b), or directly from the definition — and the constant map is continuous by Theorem 18.2(a). By Theorem 18.4, ι is therefore continuous.

Now F(·, y₀) = F ∘ ι, a composite of continuous maps, hence continuous by Theorem 18.2(c). The argument for F(x₀, ·) is identical with the roles of the factors exchanged. ∎

Where the converse would have to go, and cannot. To run this backwards you would need to build F itself out of its slices, and there is no rule that does that: Theorem 18.4 constructs maps into a product from their components, and nothing constructs a map out of one from its restrictions. The two green lines in the picture are exactly the slices this proof uses, and Exercise 12's F is continuous on every one of them while being discontinuous at the point they all pass through.
Why at1-01 phrased its definition the way it did. at1-01 quotes Hatcher's deformation retraction as a family of maps f_t : X → X with the continuity clause "the associated map X × I → X, (x,t) ↦ f_t(x), is continuous" — and then says, in its own voice: "It is not the statement that each f_t is continuous. It is the stronger statement that the single map on the product X × I is continuous — jointly in the point and the time. Every verification you do in this unit will turn on that distinction (top-04)."

This is that distinction, and the guided proof above settles the logic of it. "Each f_t is continuous" is separate continuity in the t-slices. Joint continuity implies it and is not implied by it, and no theorem in §18 can close the gap, because the gap is exactly where Munkres says there is no useful criterion. So a family satisfying the weak condition need not assemble into a homotopy at all, and every continuity check in at1-01 has to be run on the product.

Self-check 4. Why does Munkres's Exercise 12 function F fail to be continuous?

The composite with the diagonal is discontinuous, and d is continuous by Theorem 18.4, so Theorem 18.2(c) forces F to be discontinuous. The formula is not undefined at the origin — the second clause defines it to be 0, which is the whole point. F is bounded: |xy| ≤ (x²+y²)/2 gives |F| ≤ 1/2. And F is continuous in the first variable, which is the trap.

Check against this 1. Continuity is stated with preimages because preimage commutes with both unions and intersections, which is what makes the basis and subbasis reductions work. Theorem 18.1 then gives four interchangeable faces — open preimages, closures, closed preimages, and the pointwise neighbourhood condition — and the art is picking the face that already says what you want. Continuity is relative to the topologies, and the identity map on a set is continuous exactly when the domain carries the finer one.

2. A homeomorphism is a bijection between the open-set collections, so it preserves exactly the topological properties. A continuous bijection need not be one — F : [0,1) → S¹ glues two ends, and the inverse would have to tear. Expanding the codomain does not change this (Example 7 is Example 6 again), because being an imbedding is a statement about the image with its subspace topology.

3. The construction kit is generous into a product and empty out of one. Theorem 18.2 and the pasting lemma build maps freely; Theorem 18.4 makes maps into a product a matter of their coordinates. But Munkres states plainly that there is no useful criterion for maps out of a product, and Exercise 12's F — separately continuous everywhere, discontinuous at the origin, with F(x,x) ≡ 1/2 — is the witness. That one-way implication is why at1-01's homotopies are single maps on X × I and not families of maps.
