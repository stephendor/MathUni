Induction, strong induction, well-ordering (pw-02)

Dominoes, but the row is infinite and you get to shove the first one.

Line up dominoes so that every domino, when it falls, knocks over the next one. Push the first domino, and — this is the whole idea — you never have to touch another one. Domino 2 falls because domino 1 hit it. Domino 3 falls because domino 2 hit it. Domino 4, domino 5, domino 4,001,997 — all of them fall, forever, from a single push, because the "knocks over the next one" property never runs out.

Cummings' book gives you this picture right at the top of its induction chapter, and it earns its keep: it's not just a metaphor, it's a template you can read a formal induction proof through. Two things to establish — the first domino falls, and each fallen domino knocks over the next — and an infinite chain of results falls out for free.

Now suppose someone hands you a row of dominoes where you've only checked that domino 1 falls and that domino k knocks over domino k+1 for every k — but this time, imagine domino k+1 sometimes needs a shove from several dominoes back, not just the one right before it (think of chocolate bars snapping into two smaller pieces rather than one).

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. If knocking over domino k+1 sometimes requires several earlier dominoes to have already fallen (not just domino k), does the "push domino 1, everything falls" guarantee still work — and if so, does it need a different induction principle, or does ordinary induction (Principle 4.1) already cover it? Commit to an answer before Segment 1; the lesson resolves it explicitly.

Why this matters for the mission: Simplicial complex arguments induct on dimension constantly.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Induction mechanics: one push, infinitely many falls

Cummings sets up the pattern with a genuinely pretty observation. Add up the first few odd numbers:

1 = 1²,   1+3 = 4 = 2²,   1+3+5 = 9 = 3²,   1+3+5+7 = 16 = 4²

It sure looks like the sum of the first n odd numbers is always n². But "looks like, for the numbers we happened to check" is not a proof — there are infinitely many n left untested. Induction is the tool that closes that gap in one stroke.

Principle 4.1 (Induction). Consider a sequence of mathematical statements S₁, S₂, S₃, … Suppose S₁ is true, and suppose, for each k ∈ ℕ, if Sk is true then Sk+1 is true. Then Sn is true for every n ∈ ℕ. — Cummings §4.1, Principle 4.1, p. 108

Map this straight onto the dominoes: "S₁ is true" is the first domino falling. "If Sk is true then Sk+1 is true" is domino k knocking over domino k+1 — for every k, simultaneously, as a single universal guarantee rather than a case-by-case check. Cummings is upfront that this Principle, like the pigeonhole principle, is accepted without proof — it's one of the load-bearing axioms about ℕ itself. — Cummings §4.1, footnote 2, p. 108

Every induction proof you write should carry four visible labels — Cummings insists on this structure, and the closing sentence is not decoration:

Base Case. Show S₁ directly. Inductive Hypothesis. Assume Sk is true for some fixed but arbitrary k. Induction Step. Prove Sk ⟹ Sk+1. Conclusion. "Therefore, by induction, Sn holds for all n ∈ ℕ." — Cummings §4.1, p. 108

That last sentence is not a formality you can skip. Cummings is blunt: "For induction... it really is required. You can prove that the first domino will fall, and you can prove that each domino... will knock over the next domino, but why does this mean they all fall? Because induction says so!" — Cummings §4.2, p. 115 Until you write it, you've shown two separate facts, not the infinite chain between them.

Worked example — Proposition 4.2 (fully worked). For any n ∈ ℕ, 1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯ + n = n(n+1)/2.

Proof. We proceed by induction.

Base Case. n = 1: the left side is 1, the right side is 1(1+1)/2 = 1. Equal, as desired.

Inductive Hypothesis. Let k ∈ ℕ, and assume 1 + 2 + ⋯ + k = k(k+1)/2.

Induction Step. We show 1 + 2 + ⋯ + (k+1) = (k+1)(k+2)/2. Split off the last term:

 1 + 2 + ⋯ + k + (k+1) = [k(k+1)/2] + (k+1)  (by the inductive hypothesis)

 = (k²+k)/2 + (2k+2)/2 = (k²+3k+2)/2 = (k+1)(k+2)/2, as desired.

Conclusion. Therefore, by induction, 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n = n(n+1)/2 for all n ∈ ℕ. ∎

— Cummings §4.2, Proposition 4.2, pp. 110–112

Notice exactly where the inductive hypothesis got used: the sum "1 + 2 + ⋯ + k" was assumed already known, and the whole proof is just tacking on one more term and doing algebra. Cummings' own framing: if you know 1+2+⋯+60 = 1830, you don't recompute from scratch to get 1+2+⋯+61 — you just add 61. That's the inductive hypothesis earning its keep, every time. — Cummings §4.2, p. 111

Self-check 1. In an induction proof, the Inductive Hypothesis assumes Sk for "some fixed k". Why is it valid to also conclude the result for every n ∈ ℕ, if k was just one fixed value?

This is the "fixed but arbitrary" idea Cummings flags explicitly (§4.2, footnote 4, p. 111): k is a single value, but since nothing about the proof depended on which value, the implication Sk ⟹ Sk+1 holds for every k. Chain that with S1 true, and every Sn follows — domino 1 falls, and "any domino falls the next" is now guaranteed for all of them.

Self-check 2. Why does Cummings insist the phrase "therefore, by induction..." must appear at the end of every induction proof, rather than being optional?

Cummings' own words: you can prove domino 1 falls, and prove each domino knocks over the next, "but why does this mean they all fall? Because induction says so!" (§4.2, p. 115). Without stating the principle, the proof has demonstrated two true facts but never actually asserted the universal conclusion they license.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Strong induction, and well-ordering: the same guarantee, seen twice

Sometimes knowing domino k fell isn't enough to guarantee domino k+1 falls — you need to know that all of dominoes 1 through k have fallen, not just the k-th. Cummings' example: breaking apart an (m×n)-square chocolate bar into k+1 pieces typically produces two smaller bars, neither of which has exactly k squares — so regular induction, which only lets you assume the immediately preceding case, has nothing to grab onto. — Cummings §4.3, p. 129 This resolves the prediction gate from before Segment 1: yes, the guarantee still holds, but it needs a strengthened hypothesis — ordinary induction alone does not license reaching back more than one step.

Principle 4.7 (Strong Induction). Consider a sequence of mathematical statements S₁, S₂, S₃, … Suppose S₁ is true, and suppose, for any k ∈ ℕ, if S₁, S₂, …, Sk are all true, then Sk+1 is true. Then Sn is true for every n ∈ ℕ. — Cummings §4.3, Principle 4.7, p. 124

Same domino picture, sharper tool: "by the time you're about to knock over domino 100, you already know dominoes 1 through 99 have all fallen, not just domino 99." — Cummings §4.3, p. 124 Regular induction only lets you lean on the very last domino; strong induction lets you lean on the entire fallen pile behind it.

Worked example — Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (fully worked, strong induction). Every integer n ≥ 2 is prime or a product of primes.

Proof. We proceed by strong induction.

Base Case. n = 2 is prime, so the claim holds immediately.

Inductive Hypothesis. Let k ≥ 2, and assume every integer 2, 3, …, k is prime or a product of primes.

Induction Step. Consider k+1. Being an integer larger than 1, it is prime or composite. If prime, we are done immediately. If composite, write k+1 = st with 1 < s, t < k+1 — so both s and t lie in the range 2, …, k covered by the inductive hypothesis. Hence each of s and t is prime or a product of primes; concatenating their factorisations gives a prime factorisation of k+1 = st.

Conclusion. Therefore, by strong induction, every integer n ≥ 2 is prime or a product of primes. ∎

— Cummings §4.3, Theorem 4.8, pp. 125–127

Cummings is explicit about why regular induction stalls here: knowing that k is prime or a product of primes tells you almost nothing useful about k+1 — the two numbers share no obvious factorisation link. — Cummings §4.3, p. 126 But s and t, the two pieces k+1 splits into, are both smaller than k+1 — so strong induction's "reach back to any earlier case" is exactly what's needed, precisely because you don't know in advance which earlier case will turn out to matter.

Well-ordering: where the guarantee actually comes from

Here's a question worth asking honestly: why does induction work at all? Why should proving "domino 1 falls" and "each domino knocks over the next" actually force every domino down, rather than, say, dominoes eventually petering out at some enormous but finite point? The answer traces back to a structural fact about ℕ itself.

Well-ordering principle. Every non-empty set of natural numbers contains a smallest element. — Cummings, footnote on the "minimal counterexample" technique, §7, p. 233 (Cummings does not box this as a numbered Principle inside Chapter 4 itself — he invokes it there only in passing, as the fact licensing "there must be a smallest uninteresting/failing case")

This sounds almost too obvious to be worth stating — of course a set of natural numbers has a smallest element, ℕ starts at 1 (or 0) and climbs. But the honest reason it deserves its own name is that it's false for other number systems you already know: the set of positive rationals has no smallest element (halve any candidate and you get a smaller one still positive), and neither does the set of all integers (there's no smallest integer, full stop). Well-ordering is a genuine structural fact about ℕ specifically, not a free property of "numbers" in general.

Here is the honest sketch — not hand-waved, but a sketch, since a fully airtight equivalence proof is more machinery than this lesson needs — of why induction, strong induction, and well-ordering all certify exactly the same thing. These three implications are the intermediate lemmas this unit relies on but which Cummings does not bundle into a single numbered box:

Lemma A (Well-ordering ⟹ induction).

Suppose S₁ holds and Sk ⟹ Sk+1 for every k, but some Sn fails. Let F = {n ∈ ℕ : Sn is false} — assumed non-empty. By well-ordering, F has a smallest element m. Since S₁ is true, m ≠ 1, so m − 1 ∈ ℕ and (by minimality of m) Sm−1 is true. But then the inductive step forces Sm true — contradicting m ∈ F. So F is empty: induction holds. ∎

Lemma B (Induction ⟹ strong induction).

Given the strong-induction hypotheses, define Tn to mean "S₁, S₂, …, Sn are all true." Then T₁ is exactly S₁, and Tk ⟹ Tk+1 follows directly from the strong inductive step. Ordinary induction on the Tn now delivers Tn for all n — and Tn includes Sn as one of its conjuncts. ∎

Lemma C (Strong induction ⟹ well-ordering).

Suppose some non-empty F ⊆ ℕ has no smallest element. Let Sn be the statement "n ∉ F." S₁ holds (else 1 would be F's smallest element). If S₁, …, Sk all hold, then none of 1, …, k is in F; were k+1 ∈ F, it would automatically be F's smallest element (nothing smaller qualifies), contradiction — so Sk+1 holds too. By strong induction every Sn holds, i.e. F is empty — contradicting non-emptiness. So every non-empty F has a smallest element. ∎

The three chase each other in a circle — each implies the next — so all three are logically equivalent ways of expressing the same underlying fact about how ℕ is built. (This is exactly the content of Problem 6 in the problem set, phrased there as a single standalone direction: Lemma B above, written out as its own free-standing proof.)

Faded example — a strong-induction proof with two base cases (your turn to fill the gaps). Every integer n ≥ 11 can be written as 2a + 5b for some natural numbers a, b.

Proof. We proceed by strong induction.

Base Cases. The induction step below will reach back two steps, so we need two base cases: n = 11 = 2·3 + 5·1, and n = 12 = ⟨your step: find a, b ∈ N with 2a + 5b = 12⟩.

Inductive Hypothesis. Assume for some k ≥ 12 that the result holds for every n = 11, 12, …, k.

Induction Step. We show the result for k+1. By the inductive hypothesis applied to k−1 (which is ≥ 11, hence covered), write k−1 = 2a + 5b for some a, b ∈ ℕ. Adding 2 to both sides: k+1 = ⟨your step: rewrite 2a + 5b + 2 in the form 2(something) + 5b⟩, and since ⟨your step: confirm the new coefficient of 2 is still a natural number⟩, this is a valid representation of k+1.

Conclusion. ⟨your step: write the closing sentence⟩

— Cummings §4.3, Proposition 4.10, pp. 131–133

Self-check 3. Why did the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic proof need strong induction rather than regular induction?

Cummings names this exact failure mode (§4.3, p. 126): regular induction's k-to-k+1 link only helps when k+1's truth follows from k's truth specifically. Here it follows from two possibly much smaller numbers s and t, so the proof needs "any prior case might be the one I need," which is strong induction's signature move.

Self-check 4. Why is well-ordering true for ℕ but false for the set of positive rationals?

This is exactly the density property that made Q "leak" back in an-01: between any positive rational and 0 sits another positive rational (e.g. half of it), so no positive rational can be the smallest. N has no such halving trick available inside itself — 1 really is a floor, which is precisely why well-ordering holds there and nowhere near it in Q.

Self-check 5. In the faded two-base-case proof of Proposition 4.10 above, why does the induction step reach back to case k−1 rather than case k?

Strong induction lets you reach back to *any* earlier case, not a fixed distance — here the natural construction (add a domino of value 2) happens to connect k+1 to k−1, so the base cases must cover both "starting parities" (11 and 12) before that step can bite for every k ≥ 12.

Reveal a model answer

Proof. Assume the strong-induction hypotheses: S₁ is true, and for every k ∈ ℕ, if S₁, …, Sk are all true then Sk+1 is true. We show Sn holds for every n, using only ordinary induction (Principle 4.1).

Definition. For each n ∈ ℕ, let Tn be the statement "S₁, S₂, …, Sn are all true."

Base Case (for T). T₁ is just the statement "S₁ is true," which holds by hypothesis.

Inductive Hypothesis (for T). Assume Tk holds for some fixed but arbitrary k, i.e. S₁, …, Sk are all true.

Induction Step (for T). Since S₁, …, Sk are all true (that's exactly Tk), the strong-induction hypothesis fires and gives Sk+1 true. Combining with Tk, we get that S₁, …, Sk, Sk+1 are all true — which is exactly Tk+1. So Tk ⟹ Tk+1.

Conclusion. Therefore, by ordinary induction, Tn holds for every n ∈ ℕ. Since Tn includes Sn as one of its conjuncts, Sn holds for every n — which is exactly the conclusion strong induction promises. Strong induction is thus a consequence of ordinary induction, not an independent axiom. ∎

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. Induction (Principle 4.1) proves a statement for every n ∈ ℕ from just two finite facts: S₁ true, and Sk ⟹ Sk+1 for every k — the domino picture. It needs the explicit closing sentence "therefore, by induction," because the two facts alone are not the same claim as "true for all n."
  2. Sometimes Sk ⟹ Sk+1 needs more than Sk alone — it needs several earlier cases at once (the chocolate-bar/prime-factorisation problem). That forces strong induction (Principle 4.7): assume S₁,…,Sk all true, conclude Sk+1.
  3. Both principles trace back to the same structural fact about ℕ: the well-ordering principle (every non-empty subset of ℕ has a smallest element). The three are logically equivalent — well-ordering ⟹ induction ⟹ strong induction ⟹ well-ordering — because each proof of one from another only ever uses facts about ℕ having a floor with no infinite descent below it. ℚ⁺ lacks this floor (halving trick), which is exactly why well-ordering fails there.
