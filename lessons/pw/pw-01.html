Direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction (pw-01)

Three ways to corner a truth — and how mathematicians pick locks.

Picture a locked room holding a theorem. Three burglars, three methods. The first walks straight in the front door: assume the hypothesis, follow the corridor of definitions and algebra, arrive at the conclusion. The second refuses the front door — instead, they start from "the conclusion is false" and work backwards until they've shown the hypothesis must be false too, which (by a sleight of logic) proves the same thing. The third is the strangest of all: they break in, plant a small bomb of absurdity somewhere inside — a false statement, guaranteed by their own assumptions — and let the explosion prove the room was locked the way they said all along.

Same theorem, same room, three different lock-picks. The rest of this lesson is about learning which pick fits which lock — and, for one theorem, watching all three keys jam in different ways before one turns.

Prediction Gate: Commit before Segment 1. Here is the theorem this lesson keeps returning to: if n² is even, then n is even. Try the front door in your head — assume n² = 2k and push toward "n = 2j". Predict what happens.

It stalls, and the stall is informative rather than fatal. √(2k) is not integer algebra, so the direct route has nowhere to go. Segment 1 walks you into that wall on purpose; Segment 2 shows the contrapositive turning the same theorem into "n odd ⇒ n² odd", where the algebra runs the way you need it to. (The statement is certainly true — 6² = 36 is even and 6 is even, no counterexample there.)

Why this matters for the mission: Every TDA paper you'll verify is built from these moves.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Direct proof: walking in the front door

A direct proof of "if P, then Q" does exactly what it sounds like: assume P is true, then chain definitions, algebra and prior results until Q falls out the other end. Cummings gives the shape of it plainly:

"A direct proof is a way to prove a 'P ⇒ Q' proposition by starting with P and working your way to Q." — Cummings §2 ("The Structure of Direct Proofs"), p. 41

The definitions doing the heavy lifting throughout this lesson are these — memorise them cold, because every proof below leans on them:

Definition 2.2. An integer n is even if n = 2k for some integer k; n is odd if n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. — Cummings §2.1, p. 37

Case study: "if n² is even, then n is even"

Let's throw the direct approach at this one and watch where it strains. Assume n² is even, so n² = 2k for some integer k. Now what? You want to conclude something about n itself — but the definition of even only unlocks facts about squares, and there's no clean algebraic move that peels a square root off "2k" and hands you "n = 2j" for some integer j. You can wave your hands ("well, n = √(2k)…") but that's not integer algebra, it's a stall. This is the tell-tale sign a direct proof is fighting you: the hypothesis talks about n², the conclusion wants n, and direct proof has no way to "un-square" cleanly. Hold that thought — Segment 2 cracks it wide open with a different key.

Direct proof does shine when the algebra runs the same direction as the implication. Here are two fully worked warm-ups before the fade.

Worked example — Proposition 2.4 (fully worked). The sum of two even integers is even.

Proof. Assume n and m are even integers. By Definition 2.2, n = 2a and m = 2b for some integers a, b. Then

 n + m = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b).

Since a + b is an integer, we've written n + m = 2k with k = a + b an integer. Therefore, by Definition 2.2, n + m is even. ∎

— Cummings §2.1, Proposition 2.4, pp. 38

Faded example — Proposition 2.6 (your turn to fill the gaps). If n is an odd integer, then n² is an odd integer.

And since ⟨your step: name the integer inside the parentheses and justify it's an integer, citing Fact 2.1⟩, we have shown n² = 2k + 1 where k is an integer. Therefore, by Definition 2.2, ⟨your step: state the conclusion⟩. ∎

Self-check 1. A direct proof of "P ⇒ Q" begins by assuming…

Direct proof runs the implication forwards: assume the hypothesis P, unfold definitions and algebra, land on the conclusion Q. The other three options describe contrapositive and contradiction — different doors into the same room.

Self-check 2. Which proof technique is this skeleton using? "Assume n is an integer. Then n is either even or odd. Case 1: n even… [algebra] …conclusion holds. Case 2: n odd… [algebra] …conclusion holds. Therefore the conclusion holds for all n."

No assumption is being negated and no absurdity is being chased — this is still a direct march from hypothesis to conclusion, just split into exhaustive cases (Cummings §2.2, Proposition 2.7). Proof by cases is a direct-proof variant, not a fourth technique.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Contrapositive and contradiction: two other keys

Recall the snag from Segment 1: "n² even ⇒ n even" resisted a direct attack because the algebra only wants to run one way. Contrapositive fixes this by trading the implication for its logical twin:

Theorem 5.14. An implication is logically equivalent to its contrapositive: (P ⇒ Q) ⇔ (¬Q ⇒ ¬P). — Cummings §5, Theorem 5.14, p. 198

Watch what this buys us. The contrapositive of "n² even ⇒ n even" is "n not even ⇒ n² not even" — and since n is a natural number, "not even" just means "odd". So we need only prove: if n is odd, then n² is odd — which is exactly Proposition 2.6, the proof you filled in above! The direction of the algebra now matches the direction of the implication perfectly.

Worked example — Proposition 6.2, cracked cleanly. Suppose n ∈ ℕ. If n² is odd, then n is odd.

Proof. We will use the contrapositive. Assume n is not odd, i.e. n is even, so n = 2a for some integer a. Then

 n² = (2a)² = 4a² = 2(2a²).

Since 2a² is an integer, n² is even by Definition 2.2 — that is, n² is not odd. We have shown ¬Q ⇒ ¬P; thus by the contrapositive, P ⇒ Q. ∎

— Cummings §6.2, Proposition 6.2, pp. 201–202

Notice the closing sentence is not optional. Cummings is blunt about this: a contrapositive proof, taken at face value, proves a different statement than the one you were asked for — you must explicitly invoke the contrapositive at the end to connect the two. — Cummings §6.2, footnote 6, p. 203

Contradiction: the show-piece

The third key doesn't flip the implication — it assumes the whole thing fails (P true, Q false simultaneously) and hunts for an absurdity, a statement that is unconditionally false. Since correct logic can never turn a true assumption into a false one, finding that absurdity proves the assumption itself was rotten.

"If your assumptions imply something false, then something you assumed had to be false as well." — Cummings §7, p. 215

The classic demonstration — the second most famous proof in mathematics, per Cummings, after Euclid's infinitude of primes (Theorem 7.5) — is Hippasus' proof that √2 is irrational. Legend has it his fellow Pythagoreans drowned him for it. — Cummings §7.4, p. 224 Here it is, faded for you to complete the second half.

Faded example — Theorem 7.6. The number √2 is irrational.

Proof. Assume for a contradiction that √2 is rational. Then √2 = p/q for some non-zero integers p, q, written in lowest terms (no common divisors). Then √2·q = p, and squaring both sides,

 2q² = p².

Since q² is an integer, this means 2 | p², and hence ⟨your step: what does 2 | p² tell you about p itself, and by which earlier lemma?⟩. So p = 2k for some integer k. Substituting back:

 2q² = (2k)² = 4k²,  so  q² = ⟨your step: finish the algebra⟩.

Therefore 2 | q², and hence ⟨your step: same lemma as before, applied to q this time⟩. But this is a contradiction: ⟨your step: name the exact absurdity — what did we assume about p and q that this now violates?⟩. Therefore √2 cannot be rational, meaning it is irrational. ∎

— Cummings §7.4, Theorem 7.6, p. 225

Three doors, same room

Here is the whole lesson in one picture: three different logical paths, all converging on the same theorem.

When to use which — a rough compass.
  • Direct when the hypothesis and conclusion talk about the same kind of object, and algebra can run straight from one to the other (sums, products of evens/odds).
  • Contrapositive when the conclusion is awkward to attack directly but negating it hands you something concrete to assume — especially when P and Q get "flipped" in a useful order (as with n² ⇒ n), or when the conclusion is itself a negative statement ("n is not…").
  • Contradiction when the conclusion asserts something cannot exist or cannot happen (no largest integer, no smallest positive rational, √2 irrational) — assuming it exists usually hands you a concrete object to abuse until it breaks.

— synthesised from Cummings §6.2, pp. 202–203 and §7, pp. 213–215

Self-check 3. Which technique fits best: "Prove there is no largest even integer"?

The claim asserts non-existence ("there is no…"), the classic contradiction trigger: assume a largest even integer N exists, then N + 2 is a larger even integer — absurd. Same shape as Cummings' "no largest natural number", Proposition 7.1.

Self-check 4. To prove "if 3n + 5 is even, then n is odd", Cummings' Proposition 6.3 assumes n is not odd and derives 3n + 5 is not even. Which technique is this?

Assuming ¬Q and deriving ¬P, then invoking the contrapositive at the end, is precisely the contrapositive shape — not contradiction, which would assume P ∧ ¬Q together and chase an absurdity rather than cleanly deriving ¬P. (Cummings §6.2, Proposition 6.3 part 2, p. 202)

Guided Proof Practice

Prompt: Prove the following, and — before you write a line of algebra — say in one sentence which of the three techniques you are using and what made you pick it.

Let n be an integer. If n² + 3 is odd, then n is even.

Hint: run the compass above over the statement. The conclusion is hard to attack head-on (what does "n is even" give you to work with?), while its negation hands you a concrete algebraic form to substitute. Write the full proof, including the closing sentence that connects what you proved to what you were asked.

Click to reveal model answer

Technique, and why. Contrapositive. Assuming the hypothesis "n² + 3 is odd" gives n² + 3 = 2k + 1, which is a fact about n² — and as Segment 1 established, there is no way to un-square that into a fact about n. Negating the conclusion instead hands us "n is odd", i.e. n = 2a + 1, a concrete form we can substitute and expand. The algebra then runs the same direction as the implication.

Proof. We prove the contrapositive: if n is not even, then n² + 3 is not odd.

Assume n is not even, so n is odd, and by Definition 2.2, n = 2a + 1 for some integer a. Then

 n² + 3 = (2a + 1)² + 3 = 4a² + 4a + 1 + 3 = 4a² + 4a + 4 = 2(2a² + 2a + 2).

Since 2a² + 2a + 2 is an integer, we have written n² + 3 = 2k with k = 2a² + 2a + 2 an integer, so n² + 3 is even by Definition 2.2 — that is, n² + 3 is not odd.

We have shown ¬Q ⇒ ¬P. By Theorem 5.14, an implication is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, so P ⇒ Q: if n² + 3 is odd, then n is even. ∎

Two things to check in your own write-up. First, "not even" was converted to "odd" explicitly — for integers those coincide, but the proof should say so rather than silently swap them. Second, the closing sentence is present. Cummings is blunt that without it you have proved a different statement from the one you were asked for (§6.2, footnote 6, p. 203); a grader is entitled to mark its absence.
