Proof style: writing and critiquing (pw-05)

Below is a proof of a true theorem. Every line of it is the kind of line you have seen in a textbook, it reaches the right conclusion, and it would be marked correct by a reader who was skimming. It contains five planted errors, and by the end of this lesson you will be able to name all five and say what each one costs.

Theorem. For every integer n, if n² is even then n is even.

"Proof." Assume for contradiction that n² is even and that n is not even. Then n is odd, so n = 2k + 1 for some k. Squaring,

n² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k + 1) + 1,

which is odd. Clearly this contradicts our assumption that n² is even. For example, n = 3 gives n² = 9, which is odd, as required. Therefore n is even. ∎

Notice what the exercise is not. You are not being asked whether the theorem is true — it is — nor whether the general idea is right — it is. Critiquing a proof is a different skill from finding one, and it is the skill that gets used every time you referee, mark, review a preprint, or read your own work from last week.

Prediction Gate: Commit before reading on. Which of these is the most serious defect in the argument above?

Multiply out the right-hand side: 4k² + 4k + 2 + 1 = 4k² + 4k + 3. The two sides differ by 2, and the correct grouping is 2(2k² + 2k) + 1. This is the only planted error that makes a written line false rather than unjustified, unclear, or misattributed — and it is the one most readers skate over, because the line looks like the manipulation they were expecting. Segment 2 grades all five.

Why this matters for the mission: The capstone is written mathematics; style is substance.

That is not a slogan about neatness. A proof is the only artifact a mathematical claim ever has: there is no separate object the writing describes. When a step is unjustified the reader cannot tell whether the author had a reason and omitted it or never had one — and neither, six months later, can the author.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

What a proof owes its reader

Four obligations, each one the fix for a class of defect you will meet in Segment 2.

1. Introduce every variable, with its type and its quantifier, at the moment it first appears. "Let n be an integer" and "for some integer k" are not throat-clearing: they are what makes the following lines mean anything. Cummings's own definition of even and odd carries the type explicitly — "n = 2k for some integer k" — and he spends a paragraph on why dropping it breaks the definition, since n = 2k + 1.001 with k real makes every number odd.

Cummings, Proofs, Definition 2.2 and the paragraph following it, p. 36.

2. Name the fact behind every step that is not arithmetic. Not "clearly", not "obviously", not "it follows" — the name of the definition, lemma or theorem being used. The test: could a reader who accepts your named facts and nothing else reconstruct the step?

3. Write more than feels necessary. The empirical claim here is Cummings's, and it runs against most students' instinct: "students are far more likely to write too little than they are to write too much."

Cummings, Proofs, Chapter 1 Pro-Tips, p. 27.

4. End where you claimed to end. If the theorem is universal, the proof must discharge the universal — an argument about one fixed object proves a statement about one fixed object. If the proof establishes the contrapositive, say so and say why that suffices.

Worked example — the same step, three ways. Suppose we have n = 2k + 1 and want n² odd.

Too little: "Then n² is clearly odd."

Enough: "Then n² = (2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k) + 1. Since k is an integer, so is 2k² + 2k (Fact 2.1, p. 36), so n² has the form 2m + 1 with m an integer, and is odd by Definition 2.2."

Too much: the same, plus a recap of what squaring means and a paragraph on why 4 = 2·2. The middle version is the target: every step named once, nothing named twice.

The middle version is also the one where the hook's arithmetic error could not have hidden, because the grouping is written out and can be checked against the line above it.

You try. Rewrite "Since a divides b and b divides c, obviously a divides c" at the "enough" level. You will need to introduce two variables that the original sentence hides.

Self-check 1. What is wrong with "so n = 2k + 1 for some k"?

Context is exactly what a proof is not allowed to rely on for its content. With k unrestricted, 4.002 = 2(1.501) + 1 makes 4.002 odd — which is why Cummings makes the point in the paragraph immediately after Definition 2.2 rather than leaving it to the reader.

Self-check 2. A proof of a universal statement ends "Therefore n is even." Is that a defect?

This is the standard and correct move: fix an arbitrary n, argue about it using nothing specific to it, conclude for all n. What makes it valid is the word "arbitrary" at the top, which is precisely what the hook's proof omits — it never introduces n at all, so there is no arbitrary object for the conclusion to generalise from.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Grading the flawed proof

Go back to the hook and mark it yourself before reading on. Then compare. The five are listed in the order they appear, not in order of severity.

Error 1 — n is never introduced. The proof opens "Assume for contradiction that n² is even". There is no "let n be an arbitrary integer", so the symbol n enters the argument undeclared and the closing "Therefore n is even" has nothing to generalise over. Obligation 1, and it is what makes Error 5 possible.

Error 2 — k is not declared an integer. "n = 2k + 1 for some k" is not Definition 2.2; it is a weaker statement satisfied by every real number. Obligation 1 again, one level down, and the reason self-check 1 exists.

Error 3 — the algebra is false. 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k + 1) + 1 is wrong: the right side is 4k² + 4k + 3. The correct grouping is 2(2k² + 2k) + 1. This is the only line in the proof that is false, and note how little it disturbs the surface — the conclusion drawn from it ("which is odd") is still true, so the error leaves no trace downstream.

Error 4 — "Clearly this contradicts" names no fact. The step needs "no integer is both even and odd", and that is not what Fact 2.1 says. Fact 2.1 says every integer is even or odd — that there is at least one parity, not at most one. The exclusivity comes from the uniqueness clause of the division algorithm: for m = 2 the remainder r with 0 ≤ r < 2 is unique, so no integer admits both forms.

Cummings, Proofs, Fact 2.1 p. 36 (existence of a parity); Theorem 2.11, the division algorithm with its uniqueness clause, p. 46 (exclusivity).

Error 5 — an example is offered as evidence. "For example, n = 3 gives n² = 9, which is odd, as required." One instance of a universal statement is an illustration, never a step. Worse, it invites the reader to accept the general claim on the strength of a case, which is the failure mode that the whole apparatus of proof exists to rule out. Delete it, or label it explicitly as an illustration standing outside the argument.

There is a sixth thing to say about that proof, and it is deliberately not on the list, because it is a style fault rather than an error: the argument is not really a proof by contradiction. Segment 3 is about why that distinction is worth caring about.

Faded example — grade this one. "Claim. The sum of two odd integers is even. Proof. Let a and b be odd. Then a = 2k + 1 and b = 2k + 1 for integers k. So a + b = 4k + 2 = 2(2k + 1), which is even. ∎"

Defect one: ⟨your step — look hard at the two uses of k⟩.

Defect two: ⟨your step — what has the proof actually established, and about which pairs (a, b)?⟩.

Repair: ⟨your step — write the corrected proof; you will need a second letter⟩.

Self-check 3. Which of the five planted errors would a reader who checks only the logic, and not the arithmetic, still catch?

Four of the five are failures to declare, name, or discharge something, and are visible without doing any sums. That ratio is typical, and it is why "read the proof for its obligations first, then check its computations" is a better order than the reverse.

Self-check 4. Error 3 makes a written line false, yet the proof's conclusion is still true. What follows?

The two questions "is this claim true?" and "does this argument establish it?" are independent, and critiquing is entirely the second. Marking the hook's proof correct because you know the theorem is a real error — it is the marker's version of assuming the conclusion.

Self-check 5. The faded example writes a = 2k + 1 and b = 2k + 1. What has it proved?

A reused bound variable is a hypothesis nobody wrote down. The proof is valid for the pairs it actually covers and the claim is about all pairs; the repair is b = 2m + 1 for an integer m, giving a + b = 2(k + m + 1). This is the single most common defect in a first course, and it is invisible unless you read variables as bound rather than as names.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Why contradiction hides your errors

The hook's proof announces "assume for contradiction", assumes n² even and n odd, derives that n² is odd, and stops. But deriving "n odd ⟹ n² odd" is a self-contained contrapositive proof; the assumption "n² is even" was never used to get there. Cummings states the diagnostic directly: if you assume P and ¬Q and your argument proves Q outright, you were writing a direct proof and dressing it as a contradiction.

The asymmetry that makes this matter. In a direct proof of P ⟹ Q there is exactly one target. Make a mistake on the way and you will usually fail to arrive at Q, and the failure is visible: "it would take a second equal-but-opposite mistake to arrive at Q." The same holds for a contrapositive proof, which is a direct proof from ¬Q to ¬P, and for the induction step of an induction.

In a proof by contradiction the target is anything false at all. There is no single thing to hit or miss, so an error along the way can manufacture a contradiction of its own — and the proof then ends in exactly the way a correct one would. Cummings offers no fix for this and says so: proof by contradiction "requires more care than other proofs" because "it is easier to deceive yourself into accepting a flawed proof."

Cummings, Proofs, Chapter 7 Pro-Tips, pp. 237–238.

So the style rule has a real justification behind it, rather than being a matter of taste: use contradiction when you need it, and not when a direct or contrapositive proof is what you actually have, because the technique you choose determines whether your own errors are self-announcing or self-concealing. That is also why a critique should always ask "which technique is this, really?" before checking any step — the answer changes how hard you have to look.

Cummings does name the case where contradiction earns its risk: a "for all" theorem. Assuming it false hands you a particular counterexample to work with, and if counterexamples exist you may take a smallest one and use its minimality. That is the well-ordering move from pw-02, and it is unavailable to a direct proof.

Worked example — the same theorem, honestly labelled.

Theorem. For every integer n, if n² is even then n is even.

Proof. Let n be an arbitrary integer. We prove the contrapositive: if n is odd then n² is odd.

Suppose n is odd. By Definition 2.2 (p. 36) there is an integer k with n = 2k + 1. Then

n² = (2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k) + 1.

By Fact 2.1 (p. 36) the quantity 2k² + 2k is an integer, so n² has the form 2m + 1 with m an integer, and is therefore odd by Definition 2.2.

This proves the contrapositive. Since a conditional and its contrapositive are equivalent, the theorem follows; and as n was arbitrary, it holds for every integer n. ∎

Same mathematics, five defects gone, and one line longer than the flawed version. The cost of the four obligations is almost always this small.

Self-check 6. Why is "assume P and ¬Q, derive Q, contradiction" a style fault rather than a logical error?

Nothing invalid happens. What is lost is information: the reader can no longer see that the proof has a single target, and neither can the author, which is precisely the property that makes errors visible. Interchangeable in truth-value, not in what they reveal.

Self-check 7. When does proof by contradiction earn its extra risk?

The negation of "every X has property P" is "some X lacks P", which converts a statement about everything into an object you can hold. Adding minimality on top of that is the well-ordering / minimal-counterexample technique from pw-02. "Longer" is not a reason for anything.

Guided proof. Take the faded example's claim — the sum of two odd integers is even — and write a proof that satisfies all four obligations. Then write a one-paragraph critique of your own proof as if you were marking a stranger's: name any step where you asked the reader to supply a fact.

Reveal model answer

Proof. Let a and b be arbitrary odd integers. By Definition 2.2 (p. 36) there are integers k and m with a = 2k + 1 and b = 2m + 1 — two letters, because a and b need not be equal. Then

a + b = (2k + 1) + (2m + 1) = 2k + 2m + 2 = 2(k + m + 1).

By Fact 2.1 (p. 36), k + m + 1 is an integer, so a + b = 2j for an integer j and is even by Definition 2.2. As a and b were arbitrary, the claim holds for every pair of odd integers. ∎

Critique of the above. The step from 2k + 2m + 2 to 2(k + m + 1) is arithmetic and needs no name. The claim that k + m + 1 is an integer does need one and gets it. The one place a reader is asked to supply something is the phrase "two letters, because a and b need not be equal" — that is a remark about the proof rather than a step in it, and a stricter style would move it to a footnote. Note that this is a direct proof, with one target, so an arithmetic slip would have left me unable to reach the form 2j.

One target or many

The picture below is the whole of Segment 3. A direct proof walks from P to a single named destination; if it strays, it lands somewhere that is visibly not Q. A proof by contradiction walks from P ∧ ¬Q towards any falsehood at all, and the whole outer region counts as arrival — which is why a wrong turn can still look like success.
