Cauchy sequences and completeness (an-05)

Convergence without knowing the destination.

Every convergence proof so far has needed a limit in hand before it could start. an-04 got around that twice — the Monotone Convergence Theorem manufactured a limit out of a supremum, and Bolzano–Weierstrass manufactured one out of nested intervals — but both bought their limit with a hypothesis: order in the first case, boundedness plus a sacrifice of most of the sequence in the second.

This unit removes the limit from the statement altogether. The Cauchy condition is a property of a sequence that mentions only its own terms, and in ℝ it is equivalent to convergence. That equivalence is not a logical triviality: it is false in ℚ, and what makes it true in ℝ is completeness. By the end of the unit you will have seen the same fact wearing five different faces.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Two conditions on a sequence (sn) of reals. (i) Consecutive terms get arbitrarily close: for every ε > 0 there is N with |sn+1 − sn| < ε for all n ≥ N. (ii) All later terms get arbitrarily close to each other: for every ε > 0 there is N with |sn − sm| < ε for all m, n ≥ N. Which of these forces (sn) to converge?

Only (ii). Condition (i) is satisfied by the partial sums of the harmonic series, since |sn+1 − sn| = 1/(n+1) → 0, and an-04 proved those partial sums diverge. The two conditions differ by a quantifier — one index free or two — and that difference is the entire unit. Abbott calls (i) the pseudo-Cauchy property and asks you to separate them in Exercise 2.6.3.

Why this matters for the mission: The space of persistence diagrams is complete — same criterion.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

A convergence condition with no limit in it

Definition 2.6.1. A sequence (an) is called a Cauchy sequence if, for every ε > 0, there exists an N ∈ ℕ such that whenever m, n ≥ N it follows that |an − am| < ε. — Abbott §2.6, Definition 2.6.1, p. 58

Set that beside an-03's Definition 2.2.3 and the resemblance is deliberate: same quantifier order, same ε, same threshold N. Exactly one symbol has changed — the fixed limit a has become a second free index m. Abbott prints the two definitions on facing halves of the same page so the substitution is impossible to miss. — Abbott §2.6, p. 58

The consequence is the whole point of the definition: it can be checked without knowing the limit. Every statement in it is about terms of the sequence. Compare the Monotone Convergence Theorem, which also proves convergence without a named limit but demands that the sequence never turn back; the Cauchy condition asks for nothing but the terms crowding together.

Theorem 2.6.2. Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence. — Abbott §2.6, Theorem 2.6.2, p. 59

This is the easy direction and Abbott leaves it as Exercise 2.6.2; it is the Guided Proof below. The idea is one triangle inequality: if every term past some point is within ε/2 of a, then any two such terms are within ε of each other, because the limit sits between them as a relay point.

Worked example — Cauchy sequences are bounded (faded). — Abbott §2.6, Lemma 2.6.3, p. 59

The Cauchy condition holds for every ε, so it holds for one convenient value. Take ε = ⟨your step: which ε makes the arithmetic trivial?⟩. There is then an N with |xm − xn| < 1 for all m, n ≥ N.

Fix m = N and let n ≥ N vary. Then |xn| = |xn − xN + xN| ≤ |xn − xN| + |xN| < |xN| + 1, so the tail is bounded. The initial segment x₁, …, xN−1 is a finite list, and a finite list of numbers has a largest absolute value. Take M = max{|x₁|, |x₂|, …, |xN−1|, |xN| + 1}. ∎

Gap solution: ε = 1. Any fixed positive number works; 1 is chosen because it makes the bound readable.

Compare an-03's Theorem 2.3.2, that convergent sequences are bounded: same statement, same proof shape, and Abbott says so explicitly when he introduces this lemma. The difference is that the proof above never mentions a limit, which is the only reason it is available at this point in the argument — the whole aim of the section is to reach convergence, so a proof that assumed it would be circular. — Abbott §2.6, p. 59

Self-check 1. Why can Lemma 2.6.3 not simply be deduced from an-03's Theorem 2.3.2, that every convergent sequence is bounded?

The first option is true as a statement and useless as an argument: it assumes Theorem 2.6.4, which is what Lemma 2.6.3 is used to prove. Watching where a proof sits in the dependency order is as important as watching whether each step is valid.

Self-check 2. The sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, … of decimal truncations of √2 is a Cauchy sequence of rational numbers. Does it converge in ℚ?

Being Cauchy is a condition on the terms, and the terms are rational, so the sequence is Cauchy in ℚ on any reading. What fails in ℚ is the converse theorem. This is the precise sense in which ℚ is incomplete, and the third option gets it backwards: the gap is not in the hypothesis but in the number field.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The Cauchy Criterion, and five faces of one fact

Theorem 2.6.4 (Cauchy Criterion). A sequence converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence. — Abbott §2.6, Theorem 2.6.4, p. 59

The forward direction is Theorem 2.6.2. For the converse, the difficulty is the familiar one: to prove convergence we need a candidate limit, and the hypothesis names none. The strategy is to extract one. A Cauchy sequence is bounded by Lemma 2.6.3, so an-04's Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem supplies a convergent subsequence (xnk) → x, and that x is the candidate. — Abbott §2.5, Theorem 2.5.5, p. 56

The rest is an ε/2 split with the subsequence as the relay. Given ε > 0, choose N with |xn − xm| < ε/2 for m, n ≥ N, then choose a subsequence term xnK with nK ≥ N and |xnK − x| < ε/2. For n ≥ nK, insert that term: |xn − x| ≤ |xn − xnK| + |xnK − x| < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε. — Abbott §2.6, p. 60

Read what that did. A subsequence converging is ordinarily much weaker than the sequence converging — an-04's self-check 4 turned on exactly that gap. The Cauchy hypothesis closes the gap: it says the terms cannot spread out, so once some subsequence settles, the rest has nowhere else to be.

Interactive visual: crowding together versus stepping smaller

Two sequences, both with consecutive gaps shrinking to zero. The blue one is Cauchy; the amber one is the partial sums of the harmonic series, which is pseudo-Cauchy and divergent. The bars measure the spread of the terms from index N out to index e·N — a window that widens as the threshold rises, because Definition 2.6.1 lets both indices run free and a fixed-width window would not test it. Watch the blue spread collapse and the amber spread sit at about ln e = 1 however far out you push N.

The picture is Exercise 2.6.3 in motion. Both sequences have |sn+1 − sn| → 0; only one has the tail collapsing to a point. A single free index controls neighbours; two free indices control the whole tail at once, and only the second is strong enough. — Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.3, p. 61

Five statements, one axiom

Abbott closes the section by tracing the dependency chain the course has actually built: the Axiom of Completeness gives the Nested Interval Property and the Monotone Convergence Theorem; the Nested Interval Property gives Bolzano–Weierstrass; and Bolzano–Weierstrass gives the Cauchy Criterion. That is the order in which they were proved. — Abbott §2.6, p. 61

AoC ⇒ NIP ⇒ BW ⇒ CC,   and AoC ⇒ MCT

The surprise is that the arrows all reverse. Any one of the five can be taken as the axiom and the other four derived from it, so they are five descriptions of a single property of ℝ rather than five separate facts. Abbott sets the missing implications as Exercise 2.6.6, and this unit's problem set works two of them. — Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.6, p. 62

Two consequences are worth carrying forward. First, none of the five can be proved from the field and order axioms alone, because ℚ satisfies those and satisfies none of the five. Completeness is genuinely extra. Second — and this is why the unit exists for the mission — the Cauchy Criterion is the only one of the five that survives verbatim when ℝ is replaced by a general metric space, since it alone mentions neither order nor suprema. an-14 takes that step, and it is why "complete metric space" is the phrase that appears in the stability theory of persistence diagrams rather than "ordered field with the least upper bound property".

Self-check 3. A sequence has a convergent subsequence. Must the sequence be Cauchy?

Take ε = 1 and any N: the terms aN and aN+1 differ by 2. So (−1)n is bounded, has two convergent subsequences, and is not Cauchy — which also disposes of the third option. The implication runs one way only: Cauchy is strictly stronger than having a convergent subsequence, and Theorem 2.6.4's proof is exactly the work of upgrading the weaker to the stronger.

Self-check 4. Which of these is the reason the Cauchy Criterion, rather than the Monotone Convergence Theorem, is the completeness statement that generalises to metric spaces?

"Increasing", "bounded above" and "supremum" all presuppose that the elements can be compared. A metric space has a distance and no order at all, so Definition 2.6.1 transfers by replacing |xn − xm| with d(xn, xm) and the other four do not transfer at all.

Self-check 5. Is every bounded sequence Cauchy?

Lemma 2.6.3 says Cauchy implies bounded. Reading an implication backwards is the most common single error in a first analysis course, and it is worth naming: the converse of a true statement is a different statement, needing its own proof or its own counterexample.

You try (solo). Give an example of each of the following, or argue that the request is impossible: (a) a Cauchy sequence that is not monotone; (b) a monotone sequence that is not Cauchy; (c) a Cauchy sequence with a divergent subsequence; (d) an unbounded sequence containing a subsequence that is Cauchy. — Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.1, p. 61

Hint: for each request, decide first whether it contradicts one of the three results above. Exactly one of the four is impossible, and naming which theorem forbids it is the answer. The other three all have examples, and the one most often given up on is (d) — an unbounded sequence may still have a constant subsequence.

Guided proof practice

Prompt: Prove Theorem 2.6.2: every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence. Hint: the definition of convergence gives you closeness to a, and you need closeness between two terms. You have one point of contact with a and two terms to relate, so ask what quantity you should insert and remove — and what that forces you to demand of each term separately. — Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.2, p. 61

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Assume (xn) → x and let ε > 0. Applying the definition of convergence with the positive number ε/2, there is N ∈ ℕ such that |xn − x| < ε/2 whenever n ≥ N.

Now take any m, n ≥ N. Insert and remove x:

|xn − xm| = |(xn − x) + (x − xm)| ≤ |xn − x| + |x − xm| < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε.

Since ε > 0 was arbitrary, (xn) satisfies Definition 2.6.1. ∎

Why ε/2 and not ε. The estimate consumes the convergence hypothesis twice, once for each index, and the two errors add. Asking for ε each time would deliver 2ε, which is not what the definition demands. Applying the hypothesis at ε/2 is legitimate because convergence was assumed for every positive number, not merely for the one you were handed — the same manoeuvre that let Lemma 2.6.3 pick ε = 1.
