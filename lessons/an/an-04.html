Monotone convergence and Bolzano–Weierstrass (an-04)

Bounded sequences can't escape: something must accumulate.

an-03 gave you a definition of convergence that can be checked — but only once someone hands you the limit. The ε-N game needs a target before it can be played, and for most sequences that arise in practice nobody hands you one. This unit supplies two ways to prove a limit exists without ever naming it.

The first buys convergence with an ordering hypothesis: a sequence that never turns back and never escapes must arrive somewhere, and the Axiom of Completeness says where. The second gives up on the whole sequence and asks only for a piece of it — and that weaker demand is always met.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let (an) be a bounded sequence of real numbers. Answer both halves separately, and notice they are not the same question. (i) Must (an) converge? (ii) Must (an) have some subsequence that converges?

No to (i): (−1)n is bounded by 1 and converges to nothing, as an-03's opening self-check established. Yes to (ii): that sequence has the constant subsequence 1, 1, 1, … and the constant subsequence −1, −1, −1, …, both convergent. The general statement is the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem in Segment 2, and the gap between the two halves is the whole content of this unit.

Why this matters for the mission: Compactness arguments in stability proofs start here.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Monotone sequences, and the series they pay for

A sequence can fail to converge in exactly two ways: it can run away, or it can oscillate. Ruling out both at once is what the next definition and theorem do together — boundedness kills the first, monotonicity kills the second.

Definition 2.4.1. A sequence (an) is increasing if an ≤ an+1 for all n ∈ ℕ, and decreasing if an ≥ an+1 for all n ∈ ℕ. A sequence is monotone if it is either increasing or decreasing. — Abbott §2.4, Definition 2.4.1, p. 51

Theorem 2.4.2 (Monotone Convergence Theorem). If a sequence is monotone and bounded, then it converges. — Abbott §2.4, Theorem 2.4.2, p. 51

The proof is worth reading as a piece of engineering rather than as a list of steps, because it answers the question the ε-N definition cannot answer by itself: where does the candidate limit come from? For an increasing sequence, take the set of its terms {an : n ∈ ℕ}. It is bounded, so the Axiom of Completeness hands over s = sup{an : n ∈ ℕ}, and that supremum is the candidate. — Abbott §2.4, p. 51

Then an-01's characterisation of the supremum does the rest. Given ε > 0, the number s − ε is not an upper bound, so some term aN satisfies aN > s − ε; monotonicity carries that inequality to every later term, and s being an upper bound caps them all above. So s − ε < an ≤ s < s + ε for all n ≥ N. — Abbott §1.3, Lemma 1.3.7, p. 16

Read that again with an eye on what was consumed. The only property of ℝ used is completeness. This theorem is false in ℚ — the increasing bounded sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … of decimal truncations of √2 has no rational limit — so an-01's axiom is not a technicality here, it is the entire mechanism.

Definition 2.4.3. Given a sequence (bn), the infinite series ∑bn is defined to be the limit of the sequence of partial sums sm = b₁ + b₂ + ⋯ + bm, when that limit exists. — Abbott §2.4, Definition 2.4.3, p. 51

This is the definition that makes an infinite sum a legitimate object rather than a hopeful notation, and it is why series belong in a chapter about sequences at all: a series is a sequence, namely (sm). For non-negative terms the partial sums increase, so Theorem 2.4.2 applies at once and convergence reduces to boundedness. That single observation is worth the whole section. — Abbott §2.4, Example 2.4.4, p. 52

The harmonic series, and the trick that generalises

Worked example — the harmonic series diverges (fully worked). Consider ∑1/n, whose terms tend to 0. Do not look at the whole sequence of partial sums; look only at the terms s2k. — Abbott §2.4, Example 2.4.5, p. 52

s2k = 1 + 12 + (13 + 14) + (15 + ⋯ + 18) + ⋯ + (12k−1+1 + ⋯ + 12k)

Replace every term inside a bracket by the smallest term in that bracket. The bracket with 2k−1 terms, each at least 1/2k, is therefore at least 1/2. There are k such brackets, so s2k ≥ 1 + k(1/2), which is unbounded. Since an-03's Theorem 2.3.2 says convergent sequences are bounded, (sm) cannot converge. — Abbott §2.4, p. 53

What made it work. Not the estimate — the choice of which terms to look at. Powers of two are exactly the indices at which the grouping closes, and the argument reads a piece of the sequence rather than the sequence. Segment 2 gives that move a name.

Theorem 2.4.6 (Cauchy Condensation Test). Suppose (bn) is decreasing and satisfies bn ≥ 0 for all n ∈ ℕ. Then the series ∑bn converges if and only if the series ∑2nb2n converges. — Abbott §2.4, Theorem 2.4.6, p. 53

The convergent half of the proof bounds the partial sums sm of ∑bn by the partial sums tk of the condensed series and then invokes the Monotone Convergence Theorem on (sm). The divergent half is Abbott's Exercise 2.4.1, and it is the harmonic argument above with 1/n replaced by bn. — Abbott §2.4, p. 54

Corollary 2.4.7. The series ∑1/np converges if and only if p > 1. — Abbott §2.4, Corollary 2.4.7, p. 54

Condensing 1/np gives ∑2n(1/2np) = ∑(21−p)n, a geometric series with ratio 21−p, which converges exactly when that ratio is below 1 — that is, when p > 1. Abbott defers the geometric-series facts to §2.7, so the rigorous write-up is an-06's business; what this unit owns is the condensation step that turns a hard question into an easy one. — Abbott §2.4, p. 54

Self-check 1. A sequence is increasing and unbounded above. What does the Monotone Convergence Theorem tell you about it?

The theorem is an implication with hypotheses "monotone" and "bounded". Dropping one leaves you with no conclusion from this theorem. (It happens to be true that such a sequence diverges, but that needs its own argument — from an-03's Theorem 2.3.2, since a convergent sequence would have to be bounded.)

Self-check 2. The terms of the harmonic series tend to 0. Why is that not enough to make the series converge?

Definition 2.4.3 defines ∑bn as lim(sm). Terms tending to zero is a necessary condition (an-06 proves it) and never a sufficient one. The harmonic series is the standard witness that the converse fails.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Subsequences, and Bolzano–Weierstrass

Definition 2.5.1. Let (an) be a sequence, and let n₁ < n₂ < n₃ < ⋯ be an increasing sequence of natural numbers. Then the sequence an₁, an₂, an₃, … is called a subsequence of (an) and is denoted (ank). — Abbott §2.5, Definition 2.5.1, p. 55

Two features of that definition carry all the weight. The indices must be strictly increasing, which forces nk ≥ k — the fact every proof about subsequences eventually reaches for. And the order of the original sequence is preserved: you may delete terms, never rearrange them. A subsequence is a sequence in its own right, so every result from an-03 applies to it unchanged.

Theorem 2.5.2. Subsequences of a convergent sequence converge to the same limit as the original sequence. — Abbott §2.5, Theorem 2.5.2, p. 55

Abbott leaves this one to the reader as Exercise 2.5.1, and the Guided Proof below is that exercise. Its usefulness is mostly contrapositive: if two subsequences of (an) converge to different limits, then (an) converges to nothing at all. — Abbott §2.5, Example 2.5.4, p. 56

Worked example — computing a limit with a subsequence (faded). Let 0 < b < 1 and show (bn) → 0. — Abbott §2.5, Example 2.5.3, p. 56

Because b > b² > b³ > ⋯ > 0, the sequence is decreasing and bounded below, so by ⟨your step: which theorem?⟩ it converges to some l with b > l ≥ 0.

Now (b2n) is a subsequence of (bn), so it converges to l as well, by Theorem 2.5.2. But (b2n) = (bn)(bn), so an-03's Algebraic Limit Theorem gives (b2n) → l·l = l². Limits are unique, so l² = l, and since l < 1 this forces l = 0. ∎

Gap solution: the Monotone Convergence Theorem, Theorem 2.4.2.

Notice the shape of that argument, because it recurs for the rest of the course: assume the limit exists, then use structure to pin down its value. Establishing existence and computing the value are separate jobs, and the Monotone Convergence Theorem does only the first.

Theorem 2.5.5 (Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem). Every bounded sequence contains a convergent subsequence. — Abbott §2.5, Theorem 2.5.5, p. 56

The proof is a bisection. The sequence lives inside some interval [−M, M]. Cut it in half; at least one half contains infinitely many terms of the sequence, so choose such a half and call it I₁. Cut I₁ in half and repeat. Now build the subsequence by picking any term of the sequence lying in I₁, then a later term lying in I₂, then a later term still in I₃, and so on — always possible precisely because each Ik holds infinitely many terms, so no finite number of already-used indices can exhaust it. The lengths M/2k−1 shrink to zero, which pins the chosen terms together. — Abbott §2.5, p. 57

Interactive visual: the bisection that produces the subsequence

The dots are terms of a bounded sequence. The amber band is the interval kept at the current stage — always a half containing infinitely many terms. The circled dots are the subsequence being extracted, one from each stage, each with a larger index than the last.

Two hypotheses are doing visible work in that picture. Bounded gives the starting interval; without it there is nothing to bisect. Infinitely many terms in the kept half is what lets the index always advance — if a half held only finitely many terms you could run out and the construction would stall. Nothing at all is assumed about order, which is why Bolzano–Weierstrass applies where the Monotone Convergence Theorem cannot.

Self-check 3. Is 1, 3, 5, 7, … a subsequence of 1, 2, 3, 4, …?

Definition 2.5.1 asks only for indices n₁ < n₂ < ⋯, strictly increasing. Deleting terms is allowed; reordering them is not. So 1, 3, 5, 7, … is a subsequence, while 2, 1, 4, 3, … is not.

Self-check 4. A sequence has one subsequence converging to 2 and another converging to −2. What follows?

This is the contrapositive of Theorem 2.5.2. If (an) converged to a, then every subsequence would converge to a; two different subsequential limits make that impossible. Abbott packages the move as the Divergence Criterion, Example 2.5.4.

Self-check 5. Does Bolzano–Weierstrass hold for sequences of rational numbers, with a rational limit?

Take the decimal truncations of √2: bounded, rational, and every subsequence converges to √2, which is not rational. The nested intervals in the proof pin down a real number, and the existence of that number is the Axiom of Completeness again. Bolzano–Weierstrass is a theorem about ℝ, not about ordered fields in general.

You try (solo). Show that (bn) → 0 whenever −1 < b < 1, generalising the faded example above. — Abbott §2.5, Exercise 2.5.5, p. 58

Hint: the sequence is no longer monotone when b is negative, so the faded argument does not transfer directly. Consider |bn| = |b|n instead, and then relate convergence of |an| → 0 to convergence of an → 0.

Guided proof practice

Prompt: Prove Theorem 2.5.2: if (an) → a and (ank) is any subsequence of (an), then (ank) → a. Hint: the definition of convergence gives you a threshold N for the original sequence. You need a threshold K for the subsequence — so ask what the strict inequalities n₁ < n₂ < n₃ < ⋯ force about the size of nk relative to k. — Abbott §2.5, Exercise 2.5.1, p. 57

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. First establish the index fact: nk ≥ k for every k ∈ ℕ. Induct. Since n₁ is a natural number, n₁ ≥ 1. If nk ≥ k, then nk+1 > nk ≥ k, and a natural number strictly greater than k is at least k + 1.

Now let ε > 0. Because (an) → a, there is N ∈ ℕ with |an − a| < ε whenever n ≥ N. Put K = N. If k ≥ K then nk ≥ k ≥ N, so ank is one of the terms the threshold already governs, and |ank − a| < ε. Since ε was arbitrary, (ank) → a. ∎

Where the hypothesis lives. Everything turned on nk ≥ k, which is exactly what strict increase of the indices buys. Drop strictness — allow n₁ = n₂ = n₃ = 1, say — and the "subsequence" 1, 1, 1, … of an arbitrary sequence would converge whatever the parent did, and the theorem would be false in the useful direction.
