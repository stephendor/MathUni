Infinite series (an-06)

Rearrange a series, change its sum: conditional convergence is wild.

Finite addition is commutative, and nobody has ever had to say so. Infinite addition is not — and the failure is not exotic. Take the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, a convergent series of perfectly ordinary terms, permute its terms so that two positives are followed by one negative, and the sum increases by half.

The whole unit is an answer to one question: what is the extra hypothesis that makes infinite addition behave? It is not convergence. It is absolute convergence, and the distinction between the two is the most consequential thing in this chapter.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let ∑ak be a convergent series with sum A, and let ∑bk be a rearrangement of it — the same terms, each used exactly once, in a different order. (i) Must ∑bk converge to A? (ii) Must it, if in addition ∑|ak| converges?

No to (i): the canvas in Segment 2 shows the alternating harmonic series and one of its rearrangements converging to visibly different numbers. Yes to (ii): that is Theorem 2.7.10. The gap between the two halves is Definition 2.7.8's distinction between absolute and conditional convergence, and it is the reason the word "absolutely" appears in so many later hypotheses.

Why this matters for the mission: Rigour training; series estimates appear in vectorisation methods.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Series are sequences, so the sequence theorems come free

an-04 defined ∑ak as the limit of the partial sums sn = a₁ + ⋯ + an, and that definition is not a formality to be recited and forgotten. It means every theorem about sequences is already a theorem about series, applied to (sn). The first two results of this section are exactly that observation, cashed twice.

Theorem 2.7.1 (Algebraic Limit Theorem for Series). If ∑ak = A and ∑bk = B, then ∑cak = cA for all c ∈ ℝ, and ∑(ak + bk) = A + B. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.1, p. 62

The proof of the first part is one line: the partial sums of ∑cak are c times the partial sums of ∑ak, so an-03's Theorem 2.3.3 applies. Abbott leaves the second part as Exercise 2.7.8. Notice what is absent from the statement: nothing at all about products of series, because a product requires a choice of ordering for the cross terms, and ordering is the thing this unit is about. — Abbott §2.7, p. 63

Theorem 2.7.2 (Cauchy Criterion for Series). The series ∑ak converges if and only if, given ε > 0, there exists N ∈ ℕ such that whenever n > m ≥ N it follows that |am+1 + am+2 + ⋯ + an| < ε. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.2, p. 63

The proof is one observation: |sn − sm| is exactly the block sum am+1 + ⋯ + an, so an-05's Cauchy Criterion for sequences, applied to (sn), is this theorem. It is worth pausing on how much that buys: a convergence test for series that never names the sum, stated entirely in terms of the terms. Everything else in Segment 1 is a corollary of it. — Abbott §2.6, Theorem 2.6.4, p. 59

Theorem 2.7.3. If the series ∑ak converges, then (ak) → 0. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.3, p. 63

Take n = m + 1 in Theorem 2.7.2 and the block sum is the single term am+1. That is the entire proof, and it is the Guided Proof below. Abbott attaches a warning to every statement of it, and so does this lesson: the converse is false, and an-04's harmonic series is the standing witness. Terms tending to zero is necessary and never sufficient. — Abbott §2.4, Example 2.4.5, p. 52

Theorem 2.7.4 (Comparison Test). Assume 0 ≤ ak ≤ bk for all k ∈ ℕ. If ∑bk converges then ∑ak converges; if ∑ak diverges then ∑bk diverges. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.4, p. 63

Both halves follow from Theorem 2.7.2 and the observation that the block sums are ordered the same way as the terms. Two remarks Abbott makes are worth keeping. The hypothesis need only hold eventually, since convergence is immune to changing finitely many terms. And a comparison test is useless without a stock of series to compare against — which is what the next example, together with an-04's Corollary 2.4.7, is for. — Abbott §2.7, p. 64

Worked example — the geometric series (faded). A series is geometric if it has the form ∑ark = a + ar + ar² + ⋯. — Abbott §2.7, Example 2.7.5, p. 64

If r = 1 and a ≠ 0 the partial sums are ma and the series diverges. For r ≠ 1, the algebraic identity (1 − r)(1 + r + r² + ⋯ + rm−1) = 1 − rm rewrites the partial sum in closed form:

sm = a + ar + ⋯ + arm−1 = a(1 − rm)1 − r

A closed form turns a question about a series into a question about a single sequence, namely (rm). That sequence tends to 0 exactly when ⟨your step: what condition on r?⟩, which an-04 established, and fails to converge otherwise. Hence ∑ark = a/(1 − r) if and only if that condition holds. ∎

Gap solution: |r| < 1 — an-04's Example 2.5.3 and its extension, Exercise 2.5.5.

With geometric series in hand, an-04's Corollary 2.4.7 can finally be proved rather than asserted: condensing ∑1/np produces ∑(21−p)n, whose ratio is below 1 exactly when p > 1. Abbott sets this as Exercise 2.7.7 and this unit's problem set works it. — Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.7, p. 68

Self-check 1. The terms of ∑1/n tend to 0, and the series diverges. Does that contradict Theorem 2.7.3?

Theorem 2.7.3 says convergence implies terms → 0. The harmonic series has terms → 0 and no convergence, which contradicts the converse and confirms it is false. The third option is the misconception the theorem exists to prevent: an-04 proved the partial sums exceed 1 + k/2, so they exceed every real number.

Self-check 2. The Comparison Test requires 0 ≤ ak ≤ bk. Why must the terms be non-negative?

The proof compares |am+1 + ⋯ + an| with |bm+1 + ⋯ + bn|, and that comparison needs the block sums to be sums of non-negative numbers. Allow signs and cancellation can make the larger terms produce the smaller block. Segment 2's Absolute Convergence Test is how the test is recovered for general signs.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Absolute convergence, and what order does to a sum

Theorem 2.7.6 (Absolute Convergence Test). If ∑|an| converges, then ∑an converges. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.6, p. 65

The proof uses both directions of Theorem 2.7.2 in one breath, which is worth watching. Convergence of ∑|an| gives, by necessity, small blocks of absolute values; the triangle inequality turns those into small blocks of the terms themselves; and sufficiency then returns convergence of ∑an. A criterion that runs both ways is doing genuine work here, not decoration.

Theorem 2.7.7 (Alternating Series Test). If (an) satisfies a₁ ≥ a₂ ≥ a₃ ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0 and (an) → 0, then the alternating series ∑(−1)n+1an converges. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.7, p. 65

The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ satisfies both hypotheses and therefore converges, while the series of its absolute values is the harmonic series and does not. So the converse of Theorem 2.7.6 is false, and the gap between the two behaviours needs a name. — Abbott §2.7, p. 65

Definition 2.7.8. If ∑|an| converges, then ∑an is said to converge absolutely. If ∑an converges but ∑|an| does not, then ∑an converges conditionally. — Abbott §2.7, Definition 2.7.8, p. 65

Read the word "conditionally" as a warning label rather than a description. It marks a series whose convergence depends on cancellation between positive and negative terms — and cancellation depends on the order in which the terms arrive.

Definition 2.7.9. ∑bk is a rearrangement of ∑ak if there is a one-to-one and onto function f : ℕ → ℕ with bf(k) = ak for every k ∈ ℕ. — Abbott §2.7, Definition 2.7.9, p. 66

The bijection is what makes this an honest permutation: every original term appears, none is repeated, none is lost. Abbott is careful to show two series that look like reorderings of the alternating harmonic series and are not, because they change which terms appear rather than only their order — and the distinction matters, since a series that quietly drops or duplicates terms can be made to do anything at all. — Abbott §2.7, p. 66

Interactive visual: the same terms, two orders, two sums

Blue plots the partial sums of the alternating harmonic series in its given order. Amber plots the partial sums of the rearrangement that takes two positive terms for each negative one: 1 + 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 − 1/4 + ⋯. Identical terms, each used exactly once in each. The dashed lines are the two limits.

Nothing was added and nothing was removed; the sum went up by half. That is only possible because the alternating harmonic series converges conditionally: it holds a reservoir of positive terms summing to infinity and a reservoir of negative terms summing to negative infinity, and taking more from one reservoir before the other shifts where the partial sums settle. An absolutely convergent series has no such reservoirs, and the next theorem is that observation made precise.

Theorem 2.7.10. If ∑ak converges absolutely, then any rearrangement of it converges to the same limit. — Abbott §2.7, Theorem 2.7.10, p. 66

The proof is an ε/2 argument with one genuinely new move. Choose N so the original partial sum sN is within ε/2 of A and so the absolute tail past N is under ε/2. The first N terms all appear somewhere in the rearranged series, so let M = max{f(k) : 1 ≤ k ≤ N} be the point by which all of them have been used. For m ≥ M, the difference tm − sN consists only of terms from beyond index N, so the absolute tail bounds it. — Abbott §2.7, p. 67

That maximum over a finite set is where the bijection is spent. It exists because only finitely many terms need to be located, and it is why "rearrangement" had to be defined with a bijection rather than loosely as "the terms in some other order".

Self-check 3. Does ∑(−1)n+1/n² converge absolutely, conditionally, or not at all?

an-04's Corollary 2.4.7 gives convergence of ∑1/np for p > 1, so the series of absolute values is ∑1/n², which converges, and Definition 2.7.8 makes this absolute. The alternation is doing nothing here — which is exactly the difference from 1/n, where it does everything.

Self-check 4. A series has all terms positive and converges. Can a rearrangement of it converge to a different value?

If an ≥ 0 then |an| = an, so convergence and absolute convergence are the same statement. Abbott makes the point directly: any convergent series whose terms are positive from some point onwards converges absolutely. Conditional convergence needs infinitely many terms of each sign.

Self-check 5. Where in Theorem 2.7.10's proof is absolute convergence used, as opposed to plain convergence?

The first choice needs only convergence of the original series. The bijection is part of the definition of a rearrangement. The single step that fails without absolute convergence is the middle one: tm − sN is some finite collection of terms beyond index N, in unknown positions and unknown quantity, and the only way to bound such a collection is by the sum of the absolute values of everything past N.

You try (solo). Show that if ∑xn converges absolutely and (yn) is bounded, then ∑xnyn converges. Then find a counterexample showing the claim fails when ∑xn converges only conditionally. — Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.6, p. 68

Hint: for the first half, bound |xnyn| and use Theorem 2.7.4 followed by Theorem 2.7.6. For the counterexample, take xn = (−1)n+1/n and choose (yn) to cancel the signs.

Guided proof practice

Prompt: Prove Theorem 2.7.3: if ∑ak converges then (ak) → 0. Hint: Theorem 2.7.2 controls the block am+1 + ⋯ + an for every pair n > m ≥ N. You are allowed to choose the pair. Which choice makes the block a single term? Then write down what the resulting statement says, and compare it letter by letter with an-03's Definition 2.2.3.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Let ε > 0. Since ∑ak converges, Theorem 2.7.2 supplies N ∈ ℕ such that |am+1 + ⋯ + an| < ε whenever n > m ≥ N.

Take n = m + 1. The block collapses to the single term am+1, so |am+1| < ε for every m ≥ N. Equivalently, |ak − 0| < ε for every k ≥ N + 1. Since ε was arbitrary, (ak) → 0 by Definition 2.2.3. ∎

Two things to notice. The proof used only one instance of a criterion that speaks about all pairs, which is why it is three lines; the strength of Theorem 2.7.2 is that you may choose the pair to suit the goal. And the index bookkeeping at the end is not pedantry — the criterion delivers a bound on terms indexed m + 1 with m ≥ N, which is the terms from N + 1 onwards, and a proof that writes N where it means N + 1 has not quite proved the statement.
