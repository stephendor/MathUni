Reals, completeness, sup and inf (an-01)

√2 punched a hole in the rationals; completeness is the patch.

Around 500 BC, someone in Pythagoras' school proved that the diagonal of a unit square has no rational length — no fraction, however finely tuned, squares to exactly 2. Legend says the discoverer was drowned for it. The reason the murder (apocryphal or not) makes sense is that the result was genuinely wounding: the Pythagoreans had built a whole cosmology on the idea that any two lengths share a common rational measure. The diagonal of a square broke that promise using nothing but the Pythagorean theorem itself — their own tool, turned against them.

You can approximate √2 with rationals as closely as you like — 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, each squaring a little nearer to 2 — and the approximations never stop getting better. But there is nowhere on the rational number line where √2 actually sits. It is a hole. This lesson is about the axiom that patches every hole like it at once, and the two numbers — supremum and infimum — that axiom hands you as tools.

Prediction Gate: Commit to an answer before Segment 1. Live strictly inside ℚ for a moment — pretend ℝ has not been invented. Let A = {r ∈ ℚ : r > 0 and r² < 2}. It is certainly bounded above (2 is an upper bound). Does A have a least rational upper bound?

The candidate least upper bound would have to square to exactly 2, and Theorem 1.1.1 says no rational does. So each rational upper bound admits a strictly smaller one, forever. That failure is the precise deficiency the Axiom of Completeness repairs — Segment 2 states the axiom, and the faded example runs this same search for r² < 3 so you can watch it fail with your own hands.

Why this matters for the mission: Persistence values are reals; inf/sup define birth and death.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The √2 wound, and why ℚ leaks

Abbott opens his book the same way — by handing us Hardy's opinion that this theorem belongs among the handful of truly beautiful results in mathematics, "simple both in idea and execution," yet unfaded by two thousand years. — Abbott §1.1, p. 1 Here is the proof, in the classic contradiction shape.

Theorem 1.1.1. There is no rational number whose square is 2. — Abbott §1.1, p. 2

Proof sketch. Suppose, for contradiction, that p/q is a fraction in lowest terms with (p/q)² = 2. Then p² = 2q², so p² is even, so p itself is even — write p = 2r. Substituting gives 2r² = q², so q² is even too, so q is even. But then p and q are both even, contradicting "lowest terms." No such fraction exists. ∎

Abbott's point is not just that this theorem is true — it's what the theorem costs. Before this discovery, it was taken for granted that any two line segments share a common rational sub-unit: that the length of one is always a rational multiple of the other. Looking at the diagonal of a unit square, that assumption collapses. Because the Pythagoreans equated "number" with "rational number", they were forced into an uncomfortable conclusion: number is a strictly weaker notion than length. — Abbott §1.1, p. 3

There are two ways to respond to a crack like this. The Greeks essentially abandoned arithmetic in favour of geometry — length became primary, number secondary. Abbott (and this course) takes the opposite route: fix the number system instead. Watch the chain of forced extensions:

  • ℕ = {1, 2, 3, …} — closed under addition, but no zero, no negatives.
  • ℤ = {…, −1, 0, 1, …} — add the additive identity and inverses, so subtraction works.
  • ℚ = {p/q : p, q ∈ ℤ, q ≠ 0} — add multiplicative inverses, so division works.

At each stage the motive is the same: some operation was missing, so the number system grew to supply it. ℚ is a genuine field — addition and multiplication are commutative, associative, distributive, with identities and inverses throughout — and it comes with a natural order, densely packed: between any two rationals r < s sits another rational, (r+s)/2. — Abbott §1.1, pp. 3–4

Densely packed is not the same as complete. Theorem 1.1.1 already told us √2 isn't in ℚ. But the failure is worse than "square roots sometimes don't exist" — it's structural. You can approximate √2 with rationals to any precision you like (1.414² = 1.999396, and adding decimal places closes the gap further), yet however far you push it, there is no rational number that is the limit of that approximation. Abbott's own words for it:

"Even so, we are now well aware that there is a 'hole' in the rational number line where √2 ought to be." — Abbott §1.1, p. 4

And there isn't just one hole — √3, √5, and infinitely many others puncture ℚ the same way. The real numbers ℝ are defined, informally for now, as ℚ with every one of these holes filled: wherever a gap sits, a new irrational number is inserted into the existing order. — Abbott §1.1, p. 4 Segment 2 makes "filled" precise.

Self-check 1. Why can't the Pythagorean-style rational-approximation game ever land exactly on √2?

Rationals actually are dense in ℝ (that's Segment 2's Theorem 1.4.3) — density is not the issue. The issue is Theorem 1.1.1: no rational number, however you write it, squares to exactly 2. Density gets you arbitrarily close; it never gets you there.

Self-check 2. What exactly did the Pythagoreans lose when √2 turned out to be irrational?

The diagonal of a unit square undeniably exists and has a definite length — the crisis was purely conceptual: rational number turned out to be strictly weaker than geometric length, shattering the assumed correspondence between the two. (Abbott §1.1, p. 3)

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The Axiom of Completeness, and computing sup / inf

Here, finally, is the assumption that plugs every hole in ℚ at once. Abbott calls it "the final, and most distinctive" assumption about the reals, and is deliberately fussy about its wording:

Axiom of Completeness. Every nonempty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a least upper bound. — Abbott §1.3, p. 14

Unpacking "least upper bound" needs two definitions, stated with a fussiness that will pay off the moment you try to use them in a proof.

Definition 1.3.1. A set A ⊆ ℝ is bounded above if there exists b ∈ ℝ with a ≤ b for all a ∈ A; b is called an upper bound for A. Bounded below and lower bound are defined the same way, flipped. — Abbott §1.3, p. 14

Definition 1.3.2. A real number s is the least upper bound, or supremum (written sup A), for a set A ⊆ ℝ if: (i) s is an upper bound for A, and (ii) if b is any upper bound for A, then s ≤ b. The infimum (inf A) is defined the same way for lower bounds. — Abbott §1.3, p. 14

Both parts of the definition matter, and proofs about sup routinely use both — part (i) to say "sup A doesn't undershoot", part (ii) to say "nothing smaller works as an upper bound." A quick but important warning: sup A need not be an element of A at all. Compare (0, 2), which has sup 2 but no maximum (2 ∉ (0,2)), against [0, 2], which has sup 2 and max 2. — Abbott §1.3, Example 1.3.5, p. 15 Supremum can exist without being a maximum; when a maximum does exist, though, it is automatically the supremum.

Time to compute some. Here's a fully worked example before the fade.

Worked example — Example 1.3.3 (fully worked). Let A = {1/n : n ∈ ℕ} = {1, 1/2, 1/3, …}. Show sup A = 1.

Proof. We check both parts of Definition 1.3.2.

(i) s = 1 is an upper bound: for every n ∈ ℕ, 1/n ≤ 1, since n ≥ 1.

(ii) s = 1 is the least upper bound: let b be any upper bound for A. Since 1 ∈ A (take n = 1) and b is an upper bound, we must have 1 ≤ b. This is exactly what (ii) demands.

Both parts hold, so sup A = 1. ∎ (Note: 1 ∈ A, so 1 is actually the maximum here too — but inf A = 0, and 0 ∉ A, so the infimum is a genuine non-member; proving that rigorously needs a tool — the Archimedean Property — that comes later in the chapter.)

— Abbott §1.3, Example 1.3.3, p. 15

Which of the two — sup or inf — is actually attained as a maximum or minimum of the set, and which is only a limiting value the set never reaches? — cf. Abbott §1.3, Exercise 1.3.6

One more worked idea worth having in your pocket: the Axiom of Completeness doesn't just sit there as an abstract guarantee — it's the engine behind concrete existence proofs. Abbott uses exactly this axiom to prove a real number α with α² = 2 actually exists (set T = {t ∈ ℝ : t² < 2}, let α = sup T, then rule out α² < 2 and α² > 2 in turn, using both halves of the sup definition each time). — Abbott §1.4, Theorem 1.4.5, pp. 21–22 That's the wound from Segment 1, formally stitched shut.

Self-check 3. For A = {1/n : n ∈ ℕ}, why is checking "1 ≤ b for any upper bound b" enough to prove sup A = 1, without checking every other candidate upper bound individually?

When the proposed supremum is itself a member of the set, condition (ii) collapses to a one-line argument: any upper bound must dominate every element of A, including this one. That shortcut disappears the moment your candidate sup is not in the set (as with inf A = 0 here).

Self-check 4. The Axiom of Completeness explicitly asserts the existence of least upper bounds for sets bounded above. Why doesn't it need to separately assert the existence of greatest lower bounds for sets bounded below?

Abbott notes this explicitly (§1.3, p. 16, and Exercise 1.3.3): given A bounded below, the set of its lower bounds is bounded above, and its supremum turns out to equal inf A. One axiom, both directions, for free.

Self-check 5. True or false: if a nonempty set A ⊆ ℝ is bounded above, then sup A must be an element of A.

This is precisely the distinction between supremum and maximum. A maximum must belong to the set by definition; a supremum need not. When it does belong, it's also the maximum — but that's a bonus, not a requirement. (Abbott §1.3, Definition 1.3.4 and Example 1.3.5, p. 15)

Guided Proof Practice

Prompt: Prove the working characterisation of the supremum that every later proof in this course actually uses. Let A ⊆ ℝ be nonempty and bounded above, and let s = sup A. Show that for every ε > 0 there exists an element a ∈ A satisfying a > s − ε. (In words: you cannot back off from the supremum by any positive amount, however small, without leaving some element of A above you.) Hint: suppose no such a exists, and ask what that makes s − ε. Then use part (ii) of Definition 1.3.2 — the half that says nothing smaller than s can be an upper bound.

Click to reveal model answer

Model Answer: Let ε > 0 be arbitrary, and suppose for contradiction that no element of A satisfies a > s − ε. Then every a ∈ A satisfies a ≤ s − ε, which is precisely the statement that s − ε is an upper bound for A (Definition 1.3.1).

But s = sup A, so by part (ii) of Definition 1.3.2, s is less than or equal to every upper bound of A. Applying this to the upper bound s − ε gives s ≤ s − ε, hence ε ≤ 0 — contradicting ε > 0.

So the supposition fails, and some a ∈ A does satisfy a > s − ε. Since ε > 0 was arbitrary, this holds for every ε > 0. ∎ — Abbott §1.3, Lemma 1.3.8, p. 18

Why this is the load-bearing form. Definition 1.3.2 part (ii) is a statement about all upper bounds, which is awkward to use: upper bounds are not objects you have in your hands. This lemma converts it into a statement that hands you an actual element of A for each ε you name — exactly the shape an ε-argument can consume. When Abbott proves α² = 2 for α = sup T (§1.4, Theorem 1.4.5), this is the tool doing the work.
