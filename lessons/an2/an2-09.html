Differentiation in Rn (an2-09)

Lindström's definition of the derivative has one word in it that no calculus course mentions: the derivative of F at a is a bounded, linear map. In ℝⁿ the word costs nothing — every linear map on ℝⁿ is bounded — and he warns you anyway: "the assumption that F′(a) is bounded turns out to be essential." an2-07 is why. A linear map that is not bounded is not continuous (Theorem 5.4.5), and a "linear approximation" that is not continuous approximates nothing. The whole of the previous unit is standing inside one adjective.

Predict before we start. F : ℝ² → ℝ has a directional derivative at 0 in every direction. Which follows?

Nothing at all. F(x, y) = x²y/(x⁴ + y²), with F(0, 0) = 0, has F(tr)/t → 0 along every line, so every directional derivative at the origin exists and equals 0 — and the map r ↦ 0 is perfectly linear and bounded, killing the second option. But along the parabola y = x² the function is constantly ½, so F is not continuous at 0, which kills the fourth; and by Proposition 6.1.4 differentiability would force continuity. Proposition 6.1.11 runs one way only: it says what F′(a) must be if it exists, which is a method for producing a candidate and never a proof that the candidate works.

Why this matters for the mission: Morse theory and gradient-based filtrations need the multivariable derivative treated honestly.

The strip is right about what is needed, and the honest treatment turns out to constrain the mission rather than serve it.

The gradient is not the derivative. §6.2 at printed 183 gives, for a scalar f on ℝⁿ, f′(a)(r) = ∇f(a) · r. Read that right to left: ∇f(a) is the vector that represents the derivative through the dot product. The derivative is the linear functional; the gradient is its image under an identification that needs an inner product to exist. That is an2-07's Definition 5.3.1, at printed 142, which an2-07 recorded as sitting one section outside its range and which no S2 unit develops. On ℝⁿ it is free. On C([0,1], ℝ) with the supremum norm there is no such vector at all — the derivative is a bounded linear functional and nothing identifies it with an element of the space.

And an2-05's constraint still binds. Sup-norm closeness of two functions transfers continuity, boundedness and the value of an integral, and transfers neither differentiability nor the value of a derivative. an2-07 supplied the mechanism: differentiation is an unbounded operator. Nothing in this unit repeals it. So a filtration argument that perturbs a filter function uniformly and then reasons about ∇f has changed hypothesis without saying so.

What "treated honestly" therefore has to mean. Three things, in order: the derivative is the linear map (Definition 6.1.1); the gradient is a representation available only where an inner product is; and a bound on ‖f − g‖∞ licenses nothing about ∇f − ∇g. This unit establishes the first two and an2-05 established the third. No syllabus action on the strip — but the constraint belongs in any downstream unit that builds a filtration from a gradient and then perturbs the function. Everything about Morse theory and persistence here is reported from tda1-07 and tda2-09; this unit opens neither.

Separately, the unit's cited source is the wrong subject. The syllabus gives "Oxford M2 notes (multivariable analysis)"; that file is vector calculus — multiple integrals, polar Jacobians, div, curl, the divergence theorem — with zero occurrences of "linear map", which is what the hook promises. Authored from Lindström Chapter 6 instead; recorded for the syllabus pass.

The derivative is a map, and it is unique

Definition 6.1.1 (printed 174). For X, Y normed, O ⊆ X open and F : O → Y, a derivative of F at a is a bounded, linear map A : X → Y such that σ(r) = F(a + r) − F(a) − A(r) satisfies

limr→0 ‖σ(r)‖ / ‖r‖ = 0.
Lindström's framing, from the chapter opening at printed 173: the point of a linear approximation "doesn't just mean that the quantities get closer and closer — that is a triviality as they both approach 0. The real point is that they get closer and closer even compared to the size of r."

Definition 6.1.3 (printed 175). If such an A exists, F is differentiable at a and A is written F′(a).

Left: f(x) = sin x near a = 0.5, with the tangent (slope cos 0.5 = 0.8776) and a wrong line through the same point (slope + 0.6). Both pass through (a, f(a)); only one is the derivative. Right: the quantity Definition 6.1.1 actually tests, |f(a+r) − f(a) − A·r| / |r|, for each. The tangent's ratio falls to 0; the wrong line's levels off at 0.6, the slope error. Every line through the point makes the numerator small — it is the division by |r| that picks out one.

Lemma 6.1.2 (printed 174–175). F has at most one derivative at a point. Proved. The proof puts r = tx for a fixed x and lets t → 0, using linearity twice to pull t outside A and B.

Proposition 6.1.4 (printed 176). Differentiable at a implies continuous at a. Proved.

Proposition 6.1.5 (printed 176). A bounded linear F is differentiable everywhere with F′(a) = F. Proposition 6.1.6 (printed 176). A constant map has derivative 0. 6.1.5 proved; 6.1.6 set as Exercise 1 to §6.1.

Proposition 6.1.7 (printed 176). (F + G)′(a) = F′(a) + G′(a). Proposition 6.1.8 (printed 177). The product rule for αF with α scalar-valued. Both proved.

Proposition 6.1.4's proof is where the adjective earns its keep. Write F(a + r) − F(a) = F′(a)(r) + σ(r). The second term vanishes because ‖σ(r)‖/‖r‖ → 0. The first vanishes because ‖F′(a)(r)‖ ≤ ‖F′(a)‖ ‖r‖ — and that inequality is an2-07's operator-norm bound, available only for a bounded map. Drop the word "bounded" from Definition 6.1.1 and by Theorem 5.4.5 the candidate need not be continuous at 0, so there are r → 0 with ‖F′(a)(r)‖ = 1, and the first term does not vanish. The approximation would fail to approximate.

Theorem 6.1.9 — the Chain Rule (printed 178). If G is differentiable at a and F at b = G(a), then F ∘ G is differentiable at a with

(F ∘ G)′(a) = F′(G(a)) ∘ G′(a).
Proved. Note the ∘: a composition of linear maps, where the one-variable rule has a product of numbers.
Worked — why the two chain rules are one chain rule.

Take X = Y = Z = ℝ. By Lindström's Exercise 6 to §6.1, every linear map ℝ → ℝ is multiplication by a number, and the correspondence A ↦ A(1) is a norm-preserving bijection ℒ(ℝ, ℝ) → ℝ. Under it, f′(a) as a map corresponds to f′(a) as a number, and composition of "multiply by u" with "multiply by v" is "multiply by uv".

So (F ∘ G)′(a) = F′(G(a)) ∘ G′(a) reads, under the correspondence, as (f ∘ g)′(a) = f′(g(a)) · g′(a). Same theorem, and the general form is the one that survives when ℝ is replaced by a space with no multiplication in it.

Two immediate consequences, both Lindström's exercises. Exercise 2: an affine map F(x) = A(x) + c with A bounded linear has F′(a) = A at every a — by Proposition 6.1.7 the derivative of a sum is the sum, by Proposition 6.1.5 the first piece is A, by Proposition 6.1.6 the constant contributes 0. The derivative forgets the translation, which is the content of "the derivative is the linear part". Exercise 4: if A is bounded linear and G differentiable then (A ∘ G)′ = A ∘ G′ — a special case of the chain rule, using Proposition 6.1.5 to supply A′.

Self-check 1. Why does Definition 6.1.1 require the derivative to be bounded as well as linear?

The operator-norm bound. It is the step that makes F′(a)(r) → 0 as r → 0 in Proposition 6.1.4's proof, and it appears again inside the chain rule when the error term of the outer function is estimated. The first option is wrong: the ratio ‖σ(r)‖/‖r‖ is a well-defined nonnegative number for any candidate. The third is wrong: Lemma 6.1.2's proof uses linearity and the two limit hypotheses and never the operator norm. The fourth is the one worth dwelling on — it is true in ℝⁿ, which is why calculus never mentions it, and false in general, with an2-07's differentiation operator D(f) = f′ on C([0,1], ℝ) as the standing counterexample.

Producing a candidate, and what the Jacobian actually is

Definition 6.1.10 (printed 180). The directional derivative of F at a in the direction r is F′(a; r) = limt→0 (F(a + tr) − F(a)) / t, when it exists.

Proposition 6.1.11 (printed 180). If F is differentiable at a then F′(a; r) exists for every r and equals F′(a)(r). Proved.

Lindström is explicit about what that proposition is for, and it is not a theorem about directional derivatives: "In many cases, the directional derivative is a good tool for producing a candidate for the derivative... Once we have computed the directional derivative F′(a; r), we know from the previous section that if there is a derivative, it has to be given by F′(a)(r) = F′(a; r). But we have to be careful." The implication runs one way. The prediction gate above is the direction that fails.

Worked — where the Jacobian comes from, and what it is. Lindström's Example 1, printed 183.

Let F : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ be differentiable at a. F′(a) is a linear map ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ, so it has an m × n matrix A in the standard bases, and the j-th column of A is F′(a)(ej). By Proposition 6.1.11 that equals the directional derivative F′(a; ej), which is

limt→0 (F(a + tej) − F(a)) / t = (∂F₁/∂xj(a), …, ∂Fm/∂xj(a))T.
So A is the Jacobian JF(a), and F′(a)(r) = JF(a) r. For a scalar f this reduces to f′(a)(r) = ∇f(a) · r.

What has been proved, exactly. That the Jacobian is the matrix of the derivative with respect to the standard bases — not that the derivative is the Jacobian. The derivative is basis-free, which is why the chain rule is composition rather than matrix multiplication in disguise, and why the same Definition 6.1.1 works on C([0,1], ℝ) where no basis exists. And Lindström adds his own warning immediately: "That the Jacobian matrix exists does not mean that the function is differentiable — we saw in Exercise 11 of the previous section that F may fail to be differentiable even if all directional derivatives exist."
Proposition 6.2.1 (printed 184). If all partials ∂Fi/∂xj exist in O ⊆ ℝⁿ and are continuous at a, then F is differentiable at a. States, with the proof deferred. Lindström: "As we shall prove a more general version of this result in Section 6.6, I skip the proof here." §6.6 is in this unit, and Segment 3 collects the debt.
Faded — a derivative in infinite dimensions. Lindström's Example 2, printed 184–185.

Let X = Y = C([0,1], ℝ) with the supremum norm — complete, by Theorem 4.6.4, an2-08's page — and F(y)(x) = ∫₀ˣ y(s)² ds. The directional derivative:

F′(y; r)(x) = limt→0 (1/t) ∫₀ˣ [(y + tr)² − y²] ds = limt→0 ∫₀ˣ [2y r + t r²] ds = ∫₀ˣ 2y(s) r(s) ds.
So the only candidate is A(r)(x) = ∫₀ˣ 2y(s) r(s) ds.

⟨your step: candidate in hand, verify it against Definition 6.1.1, which Proposition 6.1.11 has not done for you. Three checks. (i) A is linear in r. (ii) A is bounded, with ‖A‖ ≤ 2‖y‖ — the clause that would be invisible in ℝⁿ. (iii) σ(r)(x) = ∫₀ˣ r(s)² ds satisfies ‖σ(r)‖/‖r‖ → 0. Do all three, and name the two properties of the supremum norm the bound in (iii) uses.⟩

Self-check 2. "F : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ has a Jacobian matrix at a, therefore F is differentiable at a." What is wrong?

The entries of JF(a) are n·m particular directional derivatives, and Proposition 6.1.11 says that if F is differentiable they are the matrix of F′(a). The converse is false, and Lindström says so on the same page: F may fail to be differentiable even when all directional derivatives exist, let alone the n axis-directional ones. The first option confuses existence of partials with continuity of them — that stronger hypothesis is Proposition 6.2.1, and it does suffice. Invertibility is irrelevant until Theorem 6.7.1, and the shape m × n is irrelevant throughout.

Partial derivatives pay a debt, and Chapter 3 collects one

Definition 6.6.1 (printed 202). For X = X₁ × ⋯ × Xn, the i-th partial derivative ∂F/∂xi(a) is the derivative of the partial map Fia obtained by freezing every coordinate but the i-th. Note what it is: a bounded linear map Xi → Y, not a number.

Proposition 6.6.2 (printed 202). Differentiable at a implies all partials exist there, with ∂F/∂xi(a) = F′(a) restricted to the i-th factor. Proved, and the proof is one line of bookkeeping rather than an appeal to anything: with r̂i the vector that is ri in slot i and 0 elsewhere, the error term of Fia at ai is the error term of F at a evaluated at r̂i. Definition 6.1.1 does the rest.

Theorem 6.6.3 (printed 203). If F is continuous at a and the partials exist in U and are continuous at a, then F is differentiable at a and F′(a)(r) = Σi ∂F/∂xi(a)(ri). Proved — the general version promised at printed 184. The proof telescopes F(y) − F(a) into n differences each moving one coordinate, and shows every line is o(‖r‖); Lindström demonstrates on the last line and leaves the pattern.

Proposition 6.6.4 (printed 204). A map into a product is differentiable if and only if every component is. Proposition 6.6.5 (printed 205). The two decompositions combined. Both proved.

Compare the quantifier shapes of 6.6.2 and 6.6.4 and the asymmetry is the point. Proposition 6.6.4 is an if and only if because a map into a product is determined coordinatewise, and each coordinate is F composed with a bounded linear projection — Exercise 4 to §6.1. Proposition 6.6.2 runs one way only because a map out of a product is not determined by its restrictions to the factors: knowing F along each axis through a says nothing about F off the axes, and the prediction gate's F(x, y) = x²y/(x⁴ + y²) is exactly that failure. Theorem 6.6.3 is what it costs to reverse it — continuity of F, plus continuity of the partials.

Reveal the model answer

The assembly. Take Xi = ℝ for i = 1, …, n and Yj = ℝ for j = 1, …, m. Then X₁ × ⋯ × Xn = ℝⁿ, the partial derivatives ∂Fi/∂xj are the ordinary ones, and Proposition 6.6.5 — the product-domain and product-codomain statement together — says precisely that F is differentiable at a. The circularity check. Walk §6.6's dependencies. Proposition 6.6.2 argues directly from Definition 6.1.1, by identifying its error term with F's own error term at r̂i. Theorem 6.6.3 argues directly from Definition 6.1.1 by telescoping. Proposition 6.6.4 uses the bounded linear projections and Proposition 6.1.7. Proposition 6.6.5 uses Theorem 6.6.3 and Proposition 6.6.4. None of them cites Proposition 6.2.1, directly or transitively. The deferral is genuine.

The hypothesis. Free in ℝⁿ. If all partials exist on an open set and are continuous at a, they are bounded on a neighbourhood of a, and telescoping one coordinate at a time with the one-variable Mean Value Theorem gives |F(y) − F(a)| ≤ C‖y − a‖ near a — so F is continuous at a automatically. Theorem 6.6.3 states continuity as a hypothesis because in a general normed space that Mean Value argument is not available: there is no one-variable interval to apply it on. So Proposition 6.2.1 assumes nothing extra, and Theorem 6.6.3 is genuinely more general at the price of one hypothesis that ℝⁿ supplies for free.

Self-check 3. Where does completeness enter Theorem 6.7.1, given that nothing in §6.1, §6.2 or §6.6 needs it?

Through the Perturbation Lemma. Lindström says so by name: "We shall now use Banach's Fixed Point Theorem 3.4.5 to prove this." Banach's theorem needs a complete metric space, the closed ball inherits completeness from X by an2-02's Proposition 3.4.4, and without it a contraction still shrinks distances and still produces a Cauchy sequence of iterates — with nothing to guarantee a limit. Self-check 4. Proposition 6.6.2 is one-directional and Proposition 6.6.4 is an if-and-only-if. Why the asymmetry?

Codomain versus domain. A map into a product is exactly the tuple of its components, and each component is a bounded linear projection composed with F — so Exercise 4 to §6.1 and Proposition 6.1.7 give both directions. A map out of a product is not recoverable from its restrictions to the factors, and the counterexample is the prediction gate's x²y/(x⁴ + y²): all axis-directional derivatives exist at 0 and the function is not even continuous there. Theorem 6.6.3 is the price of reversing 6.6.2, and its extra hypotheses — F continuous at a, the partials continuous at a — are exactly what pay it. The fourth option has the hypotheses backwards.

Self-check 5. Which is the honest statement of the relationship between ∇f(a) and f′(a)?

A representation, not an identity. Lindström's formula f′(a)(r) = ∇f(a) · r says that the linear functional f′(a) is computed by dotting against a particular vector, and the dot product is an inner product — an2-07's Definition 5.3.1, printed 142, one section outside that unit's range and developed in no S2 unit. On ℝⁿ the representation always exists and is unique. On C([0,1], ℝ) with the supremum norm there is no inner product at all, so a differentiable functional has a derivative and no gradient. The third option inverts the roles; the fourth describes the second derivative.
