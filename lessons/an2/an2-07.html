Normed and Banach spaces (an2-07)

an2-05 left you a fact with no reason attached. Uniform convergence transfers continuity, boundedness and the value of an integral, and it does not transfer the value of a derivative — witness sin(nx)/n → 0 with cos(nx) converging nowhere. That looked like an unlucky example. It is not. Lindström's Example 4 to §5.4 is the same two functions, and it says what is actually wrong: differentiation is an unbounded linear operator, and for a linear map, unbounded and discontinuous are the same word. Every unbounded operator manufactures a counterexample of exactly that shape, on demand.

Predict before we start. A : V → W is linear between normed spaces and continuous at the single point 0. What follows?

All three conditions coincide, and Theorem 5.4.5 is the statement that they do. Linearity is what collapses them: continuity at 0 forces a growth bound (Lemma 5.4.4, proved contrapositively), and a growth bound forces uniform continuity in one line, because A(u) − A(v) = A(u − v) turns a difference of values into the value at a difference — which is exactly the step no non-linear map may take. Neither completeness nor dimension appears anywhere in the theorem.

Why this matters for the mission: Feature spaces for vectorised diagrams are normed spaces; kernels live in complete (Banach) ones.

The first clause is right, the second is true and one structure short — and the downstream unit that uses kernels already says the stronger thing.

Clause one. A vectorisation sends a diagram to an element of a function space carrying a norm, so "feature spaces are normed spaces" is exactly right and this unit is the one that makes it a statement rather than a phrase.

Clause two, and what it leaves out. tda2-09 builds the persistence scale space kernel as kσ(D, E) = ⟨Φσ(D), Φσ(E)⟩ in 𝓛²(Ω) — an inner product of feature vectors — and it opens with its own strip saying that diagram space "is a metric space but not a Hilbert space, which is precisely why lab-06's landscapes, images and kernels exist". An inner product is not part of the data of a Banach space. It is Definition 5.3.1, at printed 142, in §5.3 — one section past this unit's cited range, and stated here for that reason and not developed. Completeness is necessary; it is the inner product that makes a kernel a kernel.

So the strip is not false. Every space named is normed, and the ones in use are complete. "Banach" is the weaker true word where "Hilbert" is the available one, and a reader who takes the strip as a specification of the structure needed will under-build. For the syllabus pass: an2-07 either reaches §5.3, or the strip's second clause should say Hilbert and point at the unit that supplies it.

Everything attributed to tda2-09 here is reported from that lesson and, through it, from Dey and Wang §13.1. This unit opens neither book.

A norm is a metric that knows about scaling

Definition 5.1.1 (printed 133–134). A vector space over 𝕂 = ℝ or ℂ: a set with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying eight axioms.

Definition 5.1.2 (printed 134). A norm on a vector space V is ‖·‖ : V → ℝ with (i) ‖u‖ ≥ 0, with equality iff u = 0; (ii) ‖αu‖ = |α|‖u‖; (iii) ‖u + v‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖v‖. The pair (V, ‖·‖) is a normed space.

Compare an2-01's Definition 3.1.1, whose three clauses were positivity, symmetry and the triangle inequality. Clause (ii) here has no metric counterpart at all, and clause (i) of a metric — symmetry — has no norm counterpart, because it is a consequence.
Proposition 5.1.3 (printed 135). If (V, ‖·‖) is a normed space, then d(u, v) = ‖u − v‖ is a metric on V. Proved.

The proof's accounting is the point. Positivity of d is norm clause (i); symmetry of d is norm clause (ii) with α = −1; the triangle inequality of d is norm clause (iii) after inserting w. So homogeneity, the clause with no metric counterpart, is what produces the metric axiom with no norm counterpart — and it has capacity left over, since it applies to every α and only α = −1 was spent.

That leftover capacity is exactly what a metric cannot supply, and Lindström's Exercise 6 to §5.1 makes it precise: on any V ≠ {0}, the discrete metric is generated by no norm. Take u ≠ 0. The discrete metric gives d(u, 0) = d(2u, 0) = 1, while any norm gives ‖2u‖ = 2‖u‖. A metric may be blind to scale; a norm never is.

Worked — the three norms you have already been using. Lindström's Examples 1, 2 and 3, printed 134–135.

Example 1. The euclidean norm ‖x‖ = √(x₁² + ⋯ + xn²) on ℝⁿ, and its complex counterpart with |zi|² on ℂⁿ.

Example 2. For X a compact metric space, ‖f‖ = sup{|f(x)| : x ∈ X} on C(X, ℝ). This is the supremum norm, and by Proposition 5.1.3 the metric it generates is an2-01's d₁ — the same object, now with a name that records why it behaves well under addition.

Example 3. On C([a, b], ℝ), ‖f‖₁ = ∫ₐᵇ|f| and ‖f‖₂ = (∫ₐᵇ|f|²)1/2, the L¹ and L² norms. Also an2-01's, and also now visible as norms rather than as three unrelated metrics.

What has changed. Nothing about the spaces. What has changed is that the three are now comparable, because they live on one vector space and a norm is a thing you can put an inequality between. Segment 2 is that comparison.
Proposition 5.1.4 (printed 136). In a normed space: (i) xn → x implies ‖xn‖ → ‖x‖; (ii) xn → x and yn → y imply xn + yn → x + y; (iii) xn → x and αn → α imply αnxn → αx. (i) and (iii) proved; (ii) left to the reader. The proof of (i) uses the inverse triangle inequality for norms, ‖u‖ − ‖v‖ ≤ ‖u − v‖ — Lindström's (5.1.2), obtained from an2-01's Proposition 3.1.4 by putting x = 0, y = u, z = v.

Note what (iii) quietly consumes: its proof needs {‖xn‖} bounded, and gets it from (i), since a convergent real sequence is bounded. The parts are in dependency order, which is worth noticing because it is the pattern of the whole chapter — the algebraic operations become continuous, and every later proof spends that.

Self-check 1. Every norm gives a metric. Does every metric on a vector space come from a norm?

No, and the first option is the near-miss worth understanding: ‖u‖ := d(u, 0) always satisfies positivity, and it satisfies the triangle inequality when d is translation-invariant, but nothing makes it homogeneous. The discrete metric is the cleanest witness: d(2u, 0) = d(u, 0) = 1 for every u ≠ 0, while homogeneity would force the first to be twice the second. This is Lindström's Exercise 6 to §5.1. Boundedness is a symptom rather than the criterion — the discrete metric happens to be bounded, but so is min(d, 1) for any norm-induced d, and that too is generated by no norm. And Theorem 5.1.7 is about comparing norms, not about which metrics are norms.

Segment 2 · 25 min

Which norm you use: never, and always

Definition 5.1.5 (printed 137). Two norms ‖·‖₁ and ‖·‖₂ on the same V are equivalent if there are positive constants K₁, K₂ with ‖x‖₁ ≤ K₁‖x‖₂ and ‖x‖₂ ≤ K₂‖x‖₁ for all x.

Proposition 5.1.6 (printed 137–138). Equivalent norms have the same convergent sequences, the same open, closed and compact sets, and the same continuous maps into and out of the space. Stated in three parts; "the proofs are left to the reader", and Exercise 8 sets them.

Left, in ℝ²: the three unit balls nest, B₁ ⊆ B₂ ⊆ B∞, and the nesting factors are the equivalence constants of Definition 5.1.5. Right, in C([0, 1], ℝ): Lindström's Example 4, tents of height 1 on a base of width 1/n. Every one has supremum norm 1; their L¹ norms are 1/(2n) and shrink to 0. Same question, opposite answers, and the only difference is the dimension.

Theorem 5.1.7 (printed 138). All norms on ℝⁿ are equivalent. Proved in full.

The proof has two halves and they are not alike. For K₁ = nB with B = maxi|ei|, expand x in the standard basis and apply the triangle inequality and homogeneity of the unknown norm: pure algebra, no limits. For K₂ the argument is analytic and it is where an2-03 is spent. Define f(x) = |x|; the estimate |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ |x − y| ≤ K₁‖x − y‖ shows f is continuous for the euclidean norm — using the K₁ just obtained. The euclidean unit sphere is compact, so f attains a minimum a there by the Extreme Value Theorem Theorem 3.5.10, and a > 0 because no nonzero vector has zero norm. Then K₂ = 1/a.

That is precisely the step that cannot survive into infinite dimensions, and an2-03 already recorded why: closed and bounded implies compact is a fact about ℝⁿ, and C([0, 1], ℝ) is not even locally compact. So there is no minimum to attain, and the right-hand panel of the figure shows what happens instead.

Faded — two tent families with opposite morals. Lindström's Example 4 to §5.1, printed 136–137, against his Example 1 to §4.3, printed 86, which an2-05 used.

§5.1's tents. fn rises linearly from 0 to 1 on [0, 1/2n], falls back to 0 on [1/2n, 1/n], and is 0 thereafter. Then ‖fn‖₁ = ½·(1/n)·1 = 1/(2n) → 0, the area of a triangle; and ‖fn‖∞ = 1 for every n. So fn → 0 in ‖·‖₁ and does not converge to 0 in ‖·‖∞, and by Proposition 5.1.6(i) the two norms are not equivalent on C([0, 1], ℝ).

⟨your step: §4.3's tents have base 1/n and height n. Compute both norms for that family. One of the two families has a constant ‖·‖₁ and the other a constant ‖·‖∞ — say which, and say what each family is a counterexample to. They are not counterexamples to the same thing, and drawing them side by side is how the confusion starts.⟩

Self-check 2. Where exactly does the proof of Theorem 5.1.7 fail for C([0, 1], ℝ) with the two norms of the figure?

At the compact unit sphere. The first half genuinely has no infinite-dimensional analogue either, but it is not where the proof breaks — it is where the proof stops being available, and the difference matters because the failure is exhibitable: the tents have ‖fn‖∞ = 1, so they lie on the supremum-norm unit sphere, and their ‖·‖₁ values 1/(2n) tend to 0 without ever reaching it. The infimum is 0 and is not attained, which is the minimum that Theorem 3.5.10 would have supplied and does not. Completeness is a red herring: C([0, 1], ℝ) is complete in the supremum norm (Theorem 4.6.4, an2-08's), and the norms are still inequivalent.

Banach, and the M-test's real content

Banach space (printed 141). Lindström's whole definition: "A complete normed space is known as a Banach space." Nothing new — Definition 5.1.2 for the norm, Proposition 5.1.3 for the metric it generates, and an2-02's Definition 3.4.3 of completeness applied to that metric. This sentence is in §5.2, printed 141, which is one section outside this unit's cited range. Taken here, with Proposition 5.2.3 below, and nothing else from that section.

Definition 5.2.1 (printed 140). Σk uk in a normed space is the limit of the partial sums, when it exists. Definition 5.2.2 (printed 140). {ek} is a basis if each x is Σ αkek for a unique scalar sequence.

Lindström flags Definition 5.2.1 as "rather treacherous — it seems to be a purely algebraic relationship, but it does, in fact, depend on which norm we are using", and Segment 2 is the proof that the warning has teeth: the same equation u = Σ uk can be true in ‖·‖₁ and false in ‖·‖∞ on one and the same space.

Proposition 5.2.3 (printed 141). A normed space V is complete if and only if every absolutely convergent series converges — where Σ uk converges absolutely means Σ ‖uk‖ converges. Proved, both directions. The converse extracts a subsequence with ‖xni+1 − xni‖ < 2−i, telescopes, and finishes with an2-03's Lemma 3.5.6: a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.
Guided proof — an2-06's M-test was a theorem about completeness, and its proof was a completeness proof in disguise.

an2-06 proved Proposition 4.3.4: if |vn(x)| ≤ Mn for all x ∈ A and Σ Mn converges, then Σ vn converges uniformly on A.

Do three things. First, restate that as a claim about a normed space — name the space, name the norm, and say what the hypothesis Σ Mn < ∞ becomes in it. Second, identify which half of Proposition 5.2.3 the restated claim is, and therefore what it presupposes about that space. Third, go back to an2-06's proof and point to the exact place where the presupposition was discharged — because no completeness theorem was cited there.
Reveal the model answer

The space. B(A, ℝ), the bounded real-valued functions on the set A, with the supremum norm ‖v‖∞ = supx∈A|v(x)|. The hypothesis |vn(x)| ≤ Mn says ‖vn‖∞ ≤ Mn, so Σ ‖vn‖∞ ≤ Σ Mn < ∞: the series is absolutely convergent in B(A, ℝ). And by an2-05's Proposition 4.2.3, uniform convergence on A is convergence in ‖·‖∞. So the M-test reads: an absolutely convergent series in B(A, ℝ) converges.

Which half. The forward half of Proposition 5.2.3 — "complete ⟹ absolutely convergent series converge". It therefore presupposes that B(A, ℝ) is complete. Note the direction: the M-test is not a criterion for completeness, it is a consequence of one.

Where an2-06 paid for it. Look at the two halves of that proof. The first fixes x, observes |sm(x) − sn(x)| ≤ |Sm − Sn|, and invokes completeness of ℝ to get a limit s(x). The second produces the x-free bound |s(x) − sn(x)| ≤ Σk>n Mk < ε. Those two steps together are a proof that B(A, ℝ) is complete, run on the sequence in hand instead of in general: pointwise limit from completeness of the target of the values, then a uniform tail estimate to upgrade pointwise to uniform. Lindström could not have said so at printed 89, because he has no normed spaces until printed 133.

What the reframing is worth. Two things. It tells you the M-test's hypothesis Σ Mn < ∞ is not a technical convenience but absolute convergence, the exact notion Proposition 5.2.3 says characterises completeness. And it tells you where to look when the M-test fails: an2-06's Σ (−1)ⁿxⁿ/n on [0, 1] converges uniformly with Σ‖fn‖∞ = Σ1/n divergent, which is now recognisable as the ordinary fact that convergence does not imply absolute convergence — true in ℝ, and true here for the same reason.

Definition 5.4.1 (printed 150). A : V → W between vector spaces is a linear operator if A(αu) = αA(u) and A(u + v) = A(u) + A(v). When W = 𝕂 it is called a functional.

Definition 5.4.2 (printed 151). A linear operator between normed spaces is bounded if there is M ∈ ℝ with ‖A(u)‖W ≤ M‖u‖V for all u. The least such M is the operator norm ‖A‖ = sup{‖A(u)‖W/‖u‖V : u ≠ 0}, equally sup{‖A(u)‖W : ‖u‖V = 1} — his formula (5.4.3), set as Exercise 4.

Lindström warns in the same breath that "the terminology here is rather treacherous as a bounded operator is not a bounded function in the sense of, e.g., the Extreme Value Theorem 3.5.10", and gives the one-line reason: if A(u) ≠ 0, then ‖A(αu)‖ = |α|‖A(u)‖ grows without limit. A nonzero bounded operator has unbounded range, always. "Bounded" means bounded on the unit ball, which for a linear map is a growth rate.

Lemma 5.4.3 (printed 152). A bounded linear operator is uniformly continuous. Proved: take δ = ε/‖A‖.

Lemma 5.4.4 (printed 152). A linear operator continuous at 0 is bounded. Proved, contrapositively, via vn = un/(Mn‖un‖) with ‖vn‖ = 1/Mn → 0 and ‖A(vn)‖ = 1; the conclusion uses an2-01's Proposition 3.2.5.

Theorem 5.4.5 (printed 152). For a linear A : V → W, these are equivalent: (i) bounded; (ii) uniformly continuous; (iii) continuous at 0. Proved, by assembling the two lemmas.

Lemma 5.4.3's proof is where linearity does its work, in a single equality: ‖A(u) − A(v)‖ = ‖A(u − v)‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖u − v‖. A difference of values has become the value at a difference, and the δ that results mentions neither u nor v. No non-linear function may make that move, which is why for a general continuous map the three conditions are three conditions.

Worked — and now the debt from an2-05 is paid. Lindström's Examples 3 and 4 to §5.4, printed 152.

Bounded. On C([a, b], ℝ) with the supremum norm, B(u)(x) = ∫ₐˣ u satisfies |B(u)(x)| ≤ ∫ₐˣ|u| ≤ ‖u‖(x − a) ≤ ‖u‖(b − a), so ‖B‖ ≤ b − a. Taking u ≡ 1 gives B(u)(x) = x − a and ‖B(u)‖ = b − a, so ‖B‖ = b − a exactly. Integration is a bounded operator.

Unbounded. Let D(f) = f′ on the same norm. Take un = sin(nx): then ‖un‖ = 1 and ‖D(un)‖ = ‖n cos(nx)‖ = n. So no M works and D is unbounded. Lindström adds the sentence this whole unit has been walking towards: that unboundedness "is the source of a lot of trouble, e.g., the rather unsatisfactory conditions we had to enforce in our treatment of differentiation of series in Proposition 4.3.5."

And therefore. By Theorem 5.4.5, D unbounded means D is not continuous at 0: there must be fn → 0 in the supremum norm with D(fn) ↛ 0. Normalise Lindström's own witnesses — fn = un/n = sin(nx)/n has ‖fn‖ = 1/n → 0 and ‖D(fn)‖ = ‖cos(nx)‖ = 1 for every n. That is an2-05's counterexample, and it has just been manufactured rather than found. Any unbounded operator yields one.

Proposition 5.4.7 (printed 153). The operator norm is a norm on ℒ(V, W). Proved.

Self-check 3. "A is a bounded linear operator" means:

A growth bound. Lindström calls the terminology "treacherous" for exactly the reason the first option is wrong: if A(u) ≠ 0 then ‖A(αu)‖ = |α|‖A(u)‖ is unbounded in α, so a nonzero bounded operator never has bounded range. The third option is true in its first clause and false in its second — bounded linear maps do carry bounded sets to bounded sets, but "bounded on every set" would be the first option again. And no dimension hypothesis appears anywhere: Theorem 5.4.5 holds between arbitrary normed spaces, which is what makes the unbounded D of Example 4 possible at all.

Self-check 4. Theorem 5.4.8 says ℒ(V, W) is complete when —

W alone. Trace the proof: the only limit taken is limn An(u), which lives in W, and completeness of W is what makes it exist. V supplies vectors to evaluate at and is asked for nothing else. The consequence worth remembering is that ℒ(V, ℝ) — the dual of V — is a Banach space whatever V is, because ℝ is complete. If the roles were reversed the theorem would be far less useful: one usually knows the codomain and not the domain.

Self-check 5. The strip says kernels live in Banach spaces. What is the most accurate correction?

True, and one structure short. tda2-09 defines kσ(D, E) = ⟨Φσ(D), Φσ(E)⟩ — an inner product of feature vectors in 𝓛²(Ω) — and its own strip says diagram space "is a metric space but not a Hilbert space, which is precisely why lab-06's landscapes, images and kernels exist". Self-check 6. Two tent families, both base 1/n on [0, 1]. §4.3's has height n; §5.1's has height 1. What is each a counterexample to?

Opposite constants, opposite morals. §4.3's tents have ‖fn‖₁ = ½ for every n and ‖fn‖∞ = n → ∞: they go to 0 at every point while the integral never moves, which is the failure of lim ∫ = ∫ lim under pointwise convergence. §5.1's tents have ‖fn‖∞ = 1 for every n and ‖fn‖₁ = 1/(2n) → 0: convergence in ‖·‖₁, no convergence in ‖·‖∞, hence by Proposition 5.1.6(i) the norms are inequivalent. The third option has them exactly swapped, which is the mistake the picture invites.

Check against this 1. A norm is a metric plus homogeneity, and homogeneity is what a metric cannot fake. Proposition 5.1.3 turns a norm into a metric, spending clause (ii) to produce symmetry; the converse fails, and the discrete metric on any nonzero vector space is the witness. What the extra axiom buys is comparability: Definition 5.1.5 can compare two norms, and Proposition 5.1.6 says equivalent norms agree about convergence, openness, compactness and continuity.

2. In finite dimensions the choice of norm never matters, and in infinite dimensions it always might. Theorem 5.1.7 proves all norms on ℝⁿ equivalent, and the half of the proof that is not algebra runs on compactness of the euclidean unit sphere and Theorem 3.5.10. Lindström's Example 4 shows the supremum and L¹ norms on C([0, 1], ℝ) are inequivalent: tents of height 1 and base 1/n have ‖·‖∞ = 1 always and ‖·‖₁ = 1/(2n) → 0.

3. Completeness plus linearity re-reads two earlier units. Proposition 5.2.3 characterises completeness by absolutely convergent series, and an2-06's M-test is its forward half for the supremum norm — which is why an2-06's proof had to establish that completeness by hand. Theorem 5.4.5 collapses bounded, continuous-at-0 and uniformly continuous into one condition for linear maps, and Lindström's Example 4 to §5.4 shows differentiation is unbounded and integration is not. That is the mechanism behind an2-05's transfer table, and it makes the missing row inevitable rather than unlucky.
