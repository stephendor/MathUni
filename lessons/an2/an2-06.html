Series of functions; the Weierstrass M-test and approximation (an2-06)

There is a function on the real line that is infinitely differentiable, is zero only at the origin, and whose Taylor series converges — uniformly, on all of ℝ — to the constant function 0. Cauchy found it in 1821 and it ended a century of confidence. So the hook's promise, "approximate ANY continuous function by polynomials", is not something Taylor can deliver even for the most beautifully behaved functions there are. It is delivered instead by a theorem that assumes less: no derivatives at all, only continuity, and a closed bounded interval.

Predict before we start. Let f be infinitely differentiable on ℝ and let SN be its Taylor partial sums at 0. Which is guaranteed?

Only the value at the origin. For g(x) = e−1/x² (with g(0) = 0) every derivative at 0 vanishes, so every Taylor coefficient is 0 and SN is the zero function for every N. That series converges uniformly on all of ℝ — it is constant — and agrees with g at the single point x = 0. So the third option fails too: the series converges everywhere and the limit is not f anywhere except the origin. What Taylor's formula establishes is a conditional (if a power series for f exists, its coefficients are forced), and the counterexample shows the antecedent can fail for a function with every derivative you could ask for.

Why this matters for the mission: Uniform approximation justifies discretising a continuous signal before you compute its topology.

The strip is true and it names the wrong theorem — the mechanism it needs is real, and it is not the one this unit proves.

What the chain actually is. tda1-07 reports Oudot's Corollary 3.6: db(dgm f, dgm g) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞. an2-05 established that smallness in ‖·‖∞ is exactly uniform closeness in an2-01's sup metric. So if a discretisation replaces the signal f by some g with ‖f − g‖∞ small, the diagrams are close. That much is sound, and it is what "uniform approximation justifies discretising" means.

But Theorem 4.10.1 is not the theorem that supplies the g. Discretising a signal replaces it with a sampled object — a step or piecewise-linear interpolant on a finite grid. Weierstrass' Theorem says nothing about that class: it produces polynomials, and produces them by an existence argument. What actually bounds ‖f − fgrid‖∞ is uniform continuity of f on a compact domain — Proposition 4.1.2, an2-05's page, resting on an2-03's compactness — together with the grid spacing. The strip's mechanism is uniform continuity plus stability; this unit's theorem is a different and stronger statement about which functions can serve as approximants.

And it comes with no rate. Theorem 4.10.1 is pure existence. It names no n as a function of ε, so it cannot tell you how fine a grid to take. Bernstein's Proposition 4.10.3 does give an explicit sequence, and the figure in Segment 3 measures it: on f(x) = |x − ½| the worst error falls from 0.1875 to 0.1367 to 0.0982 as N runs 4, 8, 16 — a factor near 1.4 per doubling, not near 2. A theorem that guarantees convergence guarantees nothing about affording it.

No syllabus action on the strip. The claim is true. The correction is to the reasoning that would be offered for it: cite uniform continuity and stability, not Weierstrass, when the question is how fine to sample.

A series is a sequence, so only one thing here is new

Definition 6.4.1 (Abbott, printed 167). For fn defined on A ⊆ ℝ, the series Σ fn converges pointwise on A to f if the sequence of partial sums sk = f₁ + ⋯ + fk converges pointwise to f; it converges uniformly if (sk) converges uniformly.

Read the definition twice. It defines nothing new about series: it defines a series to be the sequence of its partial sums, in whichever of an2-05's two senses is named. Everything an2-05 proved about sequences therefore applies here, with the only work being to check that a partial sum inherits the property in question — and a partial sum is a finite sum, so it always does.

That is why the first two theorems of §6.4 are one line each.

Theorem 6.4.2 (printed 167). If the fn are continuous on A and Σ fn converges uniformly to f, then f is continuous. Abbott's entire proof: "Apply Theorem 6.2.6 to the partial sums." Proved.

Theorem 6.4.3 (printed 168). If the fn are differentiable on [a, b], Σ fn′ converges uniformly to g, and Σ fn(x₀) converges at some x₀, then Σ fn converges uniformly to a differentiable f with f′ = g. "Apply Theorem 6.3.3 to the partial sums", plus the observation that sk′ = f₁′ + ⋯ + fk′ for a finite sum. Proved.

Notice which sequence carries the uniformity in Theorem 6.4.3. It is the series of derivatives, and the original series is assumed to converge at only one point. That ordering is an2-05's Theorem 6.3.3 verbatim, and it is the reason Segment 2 has to establish convergence of a differentiated series before it may differentiate anything.

So what is genuinely new? Not a transfer theorem — a test. Uniform convergence is a statement about a sequence of functions, and until now the only way to certify it has been to compute a supremum and watch it go to zero. For a series, there is a shortcut that turns the question into one about numbers.

Corollary 6.4.5 — Weierstrass M-test (Abbott, printed 168). Let fn be defined on A ⊆ ℝ and let Mn > 0 satisfy |fn(x)| ≤ Mn for all x ∈ A. If Σ Mn converges, then Σ fn converges uniformly on A. Stated as a corollary of Theorem 6.4.4; proof set as Exercise 6.4.2.

Proposition 4.3.4 — Weierstrass' M-test (Lindström, printed 89). Let vn : A → ℝ be functions on any set A, and suppose there is a convergent series Σ Mn of nonnegative numbers with |vn(x)| ≤ Mn for all n and all x ∈ A. Then Σ vn converges uniformly on A. Proved, in full.

The two statements have the same content and three differences, and only the first two are visible. Abbott's A is a subset of ℝ; Lindström's is an arbitrary set, which is the honest statement, because nothing in the proof touches A at all. Abbott asks Mn > 0 where Lindström asks only Mn ≥ 0. And Abbott sets the proof as an exercise where Lindström gives it — a difference that appears in neither statement and decides which book this lesson cites when it says "and here is why".

The third difference has a consequence worth tracing. Abbott's M-test is a corollary of Theorem 6.4.4, the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence of series, which he states at printed 168 with no proof and no exercise attached; and Theorem 6.4.4 is an2-05's Theorem 6.2.5 applied to partial sums, whose own proof was set as Exercise 6.2.6. Lindström's proof does not pass through any of that. It uses the completeness of ℝ directly, and it is four lines long.

Worked — the M-test, proved. Following Lindström, printed 89.

Let sn(x) = Σk≤n vk(x) and Sn = Σk≤n Mk.

Pointwise first. Σ Mn converges, so (Sn) is Cauchy in ℝ. For m > n and any x ∈ A,

|sm(x) − sn(x)| = |Σk=n+1m vk(x)| ≤ Σk=n+1m |vk(x)| ≤ Σk=n+1m Mk = |Sm − Sn|,
so {sn(x)} is Cauchy in ℝ for each fixed x, and completeness of ℝ gives a limit s(x). That is one limit per point: pointwise convergence and nothing more.

Then uniformly. Write S = Σk=0∞ Mk. Given ε > 0 there is N with S − Sn = Σk>n Mk < ε for all n ≥ N. Then for every x ∈ A and every n ≥ N,

|s(x) − sn(x)| = |Σk>n vk(x)| ≤ Σk>n |vk(x)| ≤ Σk>n Mk < ε.
The N came from a statement about numbers and was never a function of x. That is the whole trick, and it is why the second half is a separate argument rather than a consequence of the first: the first half produces an N for each x, which is precisely what an2-05 spent a unit distinguishing from what is wanted here. ∎
Faded — where the M-test reaches and where it stops. Abbott's Exercises 6.4.3 and 6.4.6, both set and neither worked.

Power series: how far the M-test reaches, and who takes over

A power series Σ anxⁿ is the best case for the M-test, and the gap between "best case" and "everything" is exactly one endpoint.

Theorem 6.5.1 (printed 170). If Σ anxⁿ converges at x₀, it converges absolutely for every x with |x| < |x₀|. Proved: convergence makes (anx₀ⁿ) bounded by some M, then |anxⁿ| ≤ M|x/x₀|ⁿ and geometric comparison finishes it.

Theorem 6.5.2 (printed 170). If the series converges absolutely at x₀ then it converges uniformly on [−c, c], c = |x₀|. Stated; proof is Exercise 6.5.5 — "a straightforward application of the Weierstrass M-Test", with Mn = |an|cⁿ.

Theorem 6.5.1 makes the convergence set an interval about 0, with radius R, and says nothing about its endpoints — the inequality is strict. Theorem 6.5.2 then covers the endpoint too, provided the convergence there is absolute, because then Mn = |an|Rⁿ sums. If the convergence at x = R is merely conditional, Σ|an|Rⁿ diverges, the Mn are gone, and the M-test has nothing to offer. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why §6.5 needs a second, harder tool.

Lemma 6.5.3 — Abel's Lemma (printed 171). If b₁ ≥ b₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0 and the partial sums of Σ an are bounded by A, then |a₁b₁ + ⋯ + anbn| ≤ 2Ab₁ for every n. Stated; proof delegated to Exercises 2.7.12 and 2.7.14(b), two chapters earlier.

Theorem 6.5.4 — Abel's Theorem (printed 172). If g(x) = Σ anxⁿ converges at x = R > 0, the series converges uniformly on [0, R]. Proved — one of only two complete proofs in the section.

Abbott says out loud why the triangle inequality cannot replace Lemma 6.5.3: if A bounded the partial sums of Σ|an| the lemma "would be a simple exercise in the triangle inequality", and conditional convergence is exactly the hypothesis that denies us that. The lemma is applied to the factored form Σ (anRⁿ)(x/R)ⁿ, where (x/R)ⁿ is the monotone decreasing sequence and the tail sums of Σ anRⁿ supply the bound A = ε/2.

Theorem 6.5.5 (printed 172). Pointwise convergence on A implies uniform convergence on every compact K ⊆ A. Stated; proof is Exercise 6.5.6, assembling 6.5.1, 6.5.2 and Abel.

Theorem 6.5.6 (printed 173). If Σ anxⁿ converges on (−R, R), so does the differentiated series Σ n anxn−1. Stated; proof is Exercise 6.5.7.

Theorem 6.5.7 (printed 173). A power series is continuous on its interval of convergence, infinitely differentiable on (−R, R), and every derivative is obtained term by term. Derivative formula and induction proved; continuity "requested in the exercises".

Theorem 6.5.6 is not a convenience — it is a precondition. The hypotheses of Theorem 6.4.3 require the differentiated series to converge uniformly before anything may be differentiated, so a proof of Theorem 6.5.7 that reached for term-by-term differentiation first would be circular. Abbott's own warning is that we must know it "beforehand". And the radius is the right invariant here but the endpoint is not: Σ (−x)ⁿ/n converges at x = 1 while its differentiated series does not.

Taylor converges to the wrong thing; Weierstrass assumes less and gets more

Given f, can we find a power series for it? Taylor's formula answers the easier half. Assume f(x) = Σ anxⁿ on some interval; differentiate term by term N times — legitimate by Theorem 6.5.7 — and set x = 0 to get aN = f(N)(0)/N!. Abbott is emphatic about the shape of what this establishes: "we assumed that f actually had a power series representation." The coefficients are forced if a representation exists. Whether one exists is untouched.

Theorem 6.6.1 — Lagrange's Remainder Theorem (printed 178–179). Let f be infinitely differentiable on (−R, R), put an = f(n)(0)/n! and SN = a₀ + ⋯ + aNxN. For x ≠ 0 there is a c with |c| < |x| such that EN(x) = f(x) − SN(x) = f(N+1)(c)xN+1/(N+1)!. Proof begun at printed 179 via the Generalized Mean Value Theorem and completed by Exercises 6.6.7 and 6.6.8.

Example 6.6.2 (printed 179), and a misprint worth catching. For sin x on [−2, 2] and S₅(x) = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5!, the theorem gives E₅(x) = f⁽⁶⁾(c)x⁶/6!. Differentiate sin six times — cos, −sin, −cos, sin, cos, −sin — and the numerator is −sin(c). The page prints −cos(c). That is Abbott's slip, not the extraction's: printed 179 in the PDF carries "E₅(x) = sin(x) − S₅(x) = (−cos(c)/6!)x⁶" and then "|cos(c)| ≤ 1". Nothing downstream breaks, because the bound he needs holds for either — |sin c| ≤ 1 just as |cos c| ≤ 1 — so |E₅(x)| ≤ 2⁶/6! = 64/720 = 0.0889, his ".089", is correct as printed.

Keep the reason it does not break, because it is the point of the example. The step that makes the estimate work is that the sixth derivative of sin is bounded by 1 whatever c is, and that is not a property of the remainder formula. It is a property of sin — which is why an error in naming the sixth derivative costs nothing here and would cost everything for a function whose derivatives grow. Cite this example for the mechanism, and if you cite the formula, cite it corrected.

The epilogue's criterion (printed 181). If there is a single M with |f(N+1)(c)| ≤ M for all c ∈ (−R, R) and all N, then EN(x) → 0 and SN → f on (−R, R). Stated, with the (N+1)! -beats-xN+1 argument given in one line. Abbott adds that weaker growth conditions also suffice.

And here is the function that ends the story. Let g(x) = e−1/x² for x ≠ 0 and g(0) = 0. Both books present it and neither proves anything about it: Abbott sets every step as Exercises 6.6.9–6.6.12 at printed 180, Lindström as Exercise 3 to §4.10 at printed 122. Abbott attributes it to Cauchy, 1821. The verification, which is yours to do in the problem set, is that g(n)(x) = e−1/x²Pn(x)/xNn for x ≠ 0 and g(n)(0) = 0 for every n. So every Taylor coefficient is zero, SN is the zero function for every N, that sequence converges uniformly on all of ℝ, and its limit agrees with g at exactly one point.

Left: g(x) = e−1/x² and SN ≡ 0, the same picture for every N. Right: f(x) = |x − ½|, which is not differentiable at ½, with Bernstein polynomials p₄, p₈, p₁₆. Two failures of Taylor and one success of Weierstrass, drawn to the same scale of difficulty.

Reveal the model answer

The deduction. Fix R > 0 and suppose such an M existed on (−R, R). By the epilogue's criterion, EN(x) → 0 for every x ∈ (−R, R), that is, SN(x) → g(x). But SN ≡ 0 for every N, so the left side is 0 and we get g(x) = 0 for all x ∈ (−R, R). Taking x = R/2 gives e−4/R² = 0, which is false. Hence no such M exists — and since R was arbitrary, none exists on any interval about 0. ∎

What that argument is. A statement about the entire family {g(N)}N∈ℕ on a whole interval, obtained from one value of g and the fact that its Taylor coefficients vanish. No derivative of g was computed. The work is done by reading the epilogue's sufficient condition backwards: whatever else is true of g, it cannot satisfy that condition, because if it did the conclusion would follow and the conclusion is false.

What the example refutes. That every infinitely differentiable function is the sum of its Taylor series near 0 — the conjecture Abbott says "effectively ended" with Cauchy's discovery in 1821.

What it does not refute. Theorem 6.6.1, which is true of g and remains exact; the epilogue's criterion, which is sufficient and never claimed necessary; or the existence of some polynomial approximation to g. The last of these is important: g is continuous on [−1, 1], so Theorem 4.10.1 applies to it and polynomials converging uniformly to g on [−1, 1] certainly exist. What fails is only that the Taylor polynomials are among them.

That last point is the turn of the unit. The Taylor construction failed, and the reason it failed is that it asks for too much: one series, whose partial sums are nested, whose coefficients are extracted from the behaviour of f at a single point. Weierstrass asks for something weaker in every respect.

Theorem 4.10.1 — Weierstrass' Theorem (Lindström, printed 116). The polynomials are dense in C([a, b], ℝ): for each continuous f : [a, b] → ℝ there is a sequence of polynomials {pn} converging uniformly to f. Proved, twice — probabilistically via Bernstein at printed 117–119, analytically via kernels at printed 119–121, then transported from [0, 1] to [a, b] at printed 121.

Compare the two claims with their quantifiers in order. Taylor: there is one sequence (an), determined by f's derivatives at 0, such that for all x the nested partial sums converge to f(x). Weierstrass: there is a sequence of polynomials pn, subject to no relation whatever between pn and pn+1, converging uniformly to f. And Taylor needs f infinitely differentiable where Weierstrass needs f merely continuous — which is Lindström's stated motive for the theorem: "Most continuous functions are not differentiable at all."

Lemma 4.10.2 — Chebyshev's inequality (printed 118). For a bounded random variable X with mean x, E(𝟙{|x−X|≥δ}) ≤ Var(X)/δ². Proved, in two lines, from the pointwise inequality δ²𝟙{|x−X|≥δ} ≤ (x − X)².

Proposition 4.10.3 (printed 118). For continuous f : [0, 1] → ℝ the Bernstein polynomials pN(x) = Σk=0N f(k/N)·C(N,k)·xk(1−x)N−k converge uniformly to f. Proved, printed 118–119.

Two properties of [0, 1] are consumed by that proof and they are the same property twice: compactness. It gives uniform continuity of f by Proposition 4.1.2, which controls E(𝟙{|x−Xn|<δ}|f(x) − f(Xn)|) ≤ ε/2; and it gives boundedness of f, supplying the M in the Chebyshev half. Lindström names Proposition 4.1.2 explicitly here and asserts the boundedness without a citation — "since f is a continuous function on a compact interval, it must be bounded by a constant M" — where two sections later, in Lemma 4.11.3, he does name the Extreme Value Theorem 3.5.10 for the same step. Both halves would fail on an open or unbounded interval, and Segment 3's last item shows they must.

Lemma 4.10.4 (printed 120). The normalising constants of the kernels Pn(x) = cn(1 − x²)ⁿ satisfy cn < √n. Proved, from (1 − x²)ⁿ ≥ 1 − nx² on [−1, 1].

Proposition 4.10.5 (printed 120–121). The convolutions pn(x) = ∫−11 f(x + t)Pn(t) dt are polynomials converging uniformly to f on [0, 1], for continuous f vanishing at both endpoints. Proved.

Lindström's second proof is the one to keep in mind for the mission strip, because it is constructive in appearance and not in force. The kernels concentrate: ∫−11Pn = 1 for every n while the mass collects nearer and nearer 0, so pn(x) is a weighted average of values of f close to x. The three-way split of the integral — over [−1, −δ], [−δ, δ], [δ, 1] — is exactly that picture: the middle piece is small because f barely moves, the outer pieces are small because the kernel barely weighs.

Definition 4.11.1 (printed 123). A nonempty 𝒜 ⊆ C(X, ℝ) on a compact metric space X is an algebra if cf ∈ 𝒜 for f ∈ 𝒜 and c ∈ ℝ, and f + g, fg ∈ 𝒜 for f, g ∈ 𝒜.

Self-check 4. Cauchy's g has Taylor series identically 0. Which statement is false?

The third is false, and it is the tempting one. g is continuous on the closed bounded interval [−1, 1], so Theorem 4.10.1 applies directly and polynomials converging uniformly to g exist — Bernstein even names a sequence. What Cauchy's example kills is the claim that the Taylor polynomials are such a sequence. The other three are all true: SN ≡ 0 converges uniformly everywhere because it is constant; Theorem 6.6.1 is a theorem and holds for g, yielding an exact but unusable identity because g(N+1)(c) admits no uniform bound; and the vanishing of every derivative at 0 is the content of Exercises 6.6.9–6.6.11.

Self-check 5. What is the essential difference between "the Taylor series of f converges to f" and Theorem 4.10.1?

Two things at once, and both matter. Taylor's partial sums are nested — SN+1 is SN plus one term — and every coefficient is read off f's derivatives at a single point, so the whole sequence is determined by local data. Weierstrass permits pn+1 to have nothing to do with pn, and asks only that f be continuous. The first option is wrong because Taylor convergence can also be uniform, as it is for sin on [−R, R]; the third mistakes a hypothesis for a habitat; the fourth is true of Bernstein but is not the essential point; the fifth is backwards, since Bernstein writes pN down explicitly while Theorem 4.10.1 attaches no rate to it.

Self-check 6. Weierstrass' Theorem fails for 1/x on (0, 1) and for ex on ℝ. What do the two failures have in common?

The domain, not the function. Theorem 4.10.1's only hypothesis on f is continuity; its hypothesis on the domain is that it be a closed bounded interval, which for subsets of ℝ is an2-03's characterisation of compactness. (0, 1) is bounded and not closed; ℝ is closed and not bounded; each fails one clause and one is enough. The first option is a symptom rather than the cause — unboundedness of f on its domain is what the two proofs exploit, but a bounded function can fail too, and sin(1/x) on (0, 1) does. Note also what survives: 1/x is continuous on any [a, b] ⊆ (0, 1), and there Theorem 4.10.1 applies without difficulty.
