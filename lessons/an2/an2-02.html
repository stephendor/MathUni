Completeness and completion (an2-02)

an2-01 ended one sentence short. Every definition there — convergence, continuity, open, closed — transferred from ℝ to an arbitrary metric space by replacing |x − y| with d(x, y), and every one of them worked. One did not transfer, and it is the one on which all of calculus rests: a sequence can huddle together without having anywhere to go. Lindström's verdict on why this matters is blunt — completeness "is probably the most important notion in this book as most of the deep and interesting theorems about metric spaces only hold when the space is complete."

Predict before we start. an2-01 finished with three metrics on ℝ² that are different functions but have exactly the same open sets. Suppose d₁ and d₂ are two metrics on one set X with exactly the same open sets, and (X, d₁) is complete. Must (X, d₂) be complete?

No. The first option gets the first half right and the second half wrong: convergence is determined by the open sets, but completeness is not a statement about convergence alone — it is a statement about which sequences are Cauchy, and Cauchyness compares two running indices with no point named. Segment 2 builds the counterexample explicitly on ℝ, and it is worth noticing now that the failure has to happen out at infinity: on a bounded region the two notions are hard to prise apart. The fourth option is a reasonable instinct pointed at the wrong object — boundedness is itself one of the properties this counterexample shows is invisible to the topology.

Why this matters for the mission: Diagram space is complete; completeness is what lets limits of diagrams exist to be proven stable.

The strip is quoted exactly. Its first clause is true under a restriction it does not state, and its second clause attaches completeness to the wrong theorem. Take them in order, because the second is the more useful mistake.

First clause. The corpus does establish it, and narrowly. tda2-09 reads Dey and Wang at their printed 406: with Definition 13.9 defining 𝔻qp as the diagrams at finite (p, q)-Wasserstein distance from the trivial diagram, the space 𝔻q∞ is Polish — complete and separable. That is a theorem about one class of diagrams under one distance, and an2-01 already showed why the qualification cannot be dropped: on diagrams in general the bottleneck distance is an extended pseudometric, and Segment 3 shows that completeness is not even a well-posed question about a pseudometric.

Second clause. Stability, as tda1-07 has it from Oudot's Corollary 3.6, says that for 𝔮-tame f and g on the same space X, db(dgm(f), dgm(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞. Count the limits in that sentence: there are none. It relates two diagrams you already have. Completeness is not among its hypotheses and is used nowhere in its proof. What completeness does underwrite in the corpus is tda2-09's Theorem 13.10 — that a probability measure on diagram space has a non-empty Fréchet expectation — which is an existence statement in statistics, not in stability.

So read the strip as pointing at a real theorem with the wrong finger. The distinction it blurs is the one this unit is built to install: density lets you transfer a property to a limit you already have; completeness manufactures a limit you do not. Segment 3 makes that the payoff.
Segment 1 · 25 min

A condition with no limit in it

Definition 3.4.1 (printed 59). A sequence {xₙ} in a metric space (X, d) is a Cauchy sequence if for each ε > 0 there is an N ∈ ℕ such that d(xₙ, x_m) < ε whenever n, m ≥ N.

Definition 8.2.3 (Abbott, printed 223). The same condition, with the two indices written m and n.

Set this beside Definition 3.2.1 from an2-01: {xₙ} converges to a if for every ε there is an N with d(xₙ, a) < ε for n ≥ N. The difference is one symbol. Definition 3.2.1 names a point before it starts quantifying over n; Definition 3.4.1 never names one.

That single missing symbol is the whole content of this unit, so it is worth being slow about. Lemma 3.2.2 said that xₙ → a is equivalent to d(xₙ, a) → 0 — one real sequence, and every theorem from an-05 onwards available. Try the same move on Definition 3.4.1 and there is nothing to move: d(xₙ, x_m) has two running indices, so it is not a sequence at all, and no amount of cleverness converts a doubly indexed array into one.

The consequence is that completeness cannot be derived for a space. It has to be assumed about it, or proved for it by a separate argument. Lindström takes the first step of the comparison anyway, because one direction does go through.

Proposition 3.4.2 (printed 59–60). Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence.

Proof. If a is the limit, take N with d(xₙ, a) < ε/2 for n ≥ N. For n, m ≥ N the Triangle Inequality gives d(xₙ, x_m) ≤ d(xₙ, a) + d(a, x_m) < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε. ∎

Proved in the body. Note the ε/2: you are given one budget and must spend it on two legs of a journey, which is why the halving appears and why it will appear again in every proof in this unit.
Definition 3.4.3 (printed 60). A metric space is called complete if all Cauchy sequences converge.

Lindström's Example 1: ℝⁿ is complete and ℚ is not. Example 2 is sharper and is the first hint of Segment 2 — on the set C[a, b] of continuous functions, an2-01's three metrics d₁, d₂, d₃ give three metric spaces of which only d₁ is complete, and he says "we shall prove this in the next chapter". The promise is kept at printed 101 as Theorem 4.6.2, whose proof cites Proposition 3.4.4 below; that is an2-08's page, not this one's.

The converse of Proposition 3.4.2 is exactly what fails, and the standard witness deserves stating carefully because it is easy to blame the wrong object. In ℚ, take yₙ = the decimal expansion of √2 truncated at n places: 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, … — Lindström introduces this sequence at printed 71 and displays it at printed 72. It is Cauchy, since |yₙ − y_m| ≤ 10−min(n,m). It has no limit in ℚ. Nothing about the sequence has changed between ℚ and ℝ; the terms are the same rational numbers and the distances between them are the same. What changed is which points were available to be the limit. Completeness is a statement about the ambient furniture, not about any sequence.

Proposition 3.4.4 (printed 60). Assume (X, d) is complete and A ⊆ X. Then (A, d_A) is complete if and only if A is closed.

Proved in the body, both directions. The forward direction uses Proposition 3.3.7 to place the limit inside A; the reverse builds aₙ ∈ A ∩ B(a; 1/n) at a boundary point a ∉ A.

This proposition is the bridge between two words that behave completely differently, and mixing them up is the standard error. "Closed" is relative. A set is closed in a space, and the same set of points can be closed in one space and not in another — ℚ is closed in ℚ and not closed in ℝ. "Complete" is absolute. It is a property of the metric space (A, d_A) standing alone, with no ambient space anywhere in the definition. Proposition 3.4.4 says that when the ambient space happens to be complete, the two coincide. Take that hypothesis away and only one direction survives, which is Problem 2(b).

Worked — the discrete metric is complete, and what that costs. Lindström's Exercise 1 to §3.4.

Let (X, d) be discrete and {xₙ} Cauchy. Take ε = 1/2. There is an N with d(xₙ, x_m) < 1/2 for all n, m ≥ N. But d takes only the values 0 and 1, so d(xₙ, x_m) < 1/2 forces d(xₙ, x_m) = 0, that is xₙ = x_m. The sequence is constant from N onwards, and a constant sequence converges to its value, which is a point of X. So every Cauchy sequence converges: (X, d) is complete. ∎

Now notice what has just been proved and refuse to be reassured by it. Take X = ℕ with the discrete metric. It is complete. It is closed in itself. It is bounded, since every distance is 0 or 1. And the sequence xₙ = n has no convergent subsequence at all — Lindström's Example 1 to §3.5, printed 64. If you expected closed-and-bounded to rule that out, the theorem you were reaching for is Corollary 3.5.5, which is a statement about ℝⁿ and nowhere else; it is an2-03's, and the hypothesis you dropped was "in ℝⁿ". Completeness was never the relevant condition, and this is the first of several places where a word imported from ℝⁿ arrives with its hypotheses stripped off.
Faded — completeness passes to products. Lindström's Exercise 2 to §3.4. Let (X, d_X) and (Y, d_Y) be complete, and give X × Y the metric d((x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂)) = d_X(x₁, x₂) + d_Y(y₁, y₂).

The whole proof rests on one sandwich. For any pair of points, max{d_X, d_Y} ≤ d ≤ d_X + d_Y, so d is small exactly when both coordinates are small — the same one-line move as an2-01 Problem 2(a)'s norm chain, and it is what makes "Cauchy in the product" and "Cauchy in each coordinate" the same condition.

⟨your step: run the sandwich in both directions. From d(pₙ, p_m) < ε deduce that each coordinate sequence is Cauchy; from both coordinate sequences converging, deduce that the pair converges in d. Then say which of the two directions used which half of the sandwich, and check that neither used completeness — completeness is applied exactly once, and not in either direction.⟩

Self-check 1. Which of these subsets of ℝ, with the inherited metric, is not complete?

{1/n} alone. The sequence xₙ = 1/n is Cauchy in it — the terms are its own members and they huddle — but 0 is not a member, so there is nothing for it to converge to. Adding the single point 0 makes the set closed in ℝ and therefore complete by Proposition 3.4.4, which is why the third option is safe. ℤ is closed and so is [0, 1]. Note what is not the reason: {1/n} is bounded, and boundedness has nothing to do with it.

Self-check 2. A colleague says: "A is closed in X, so (A, d_A) is complete — that is Proposition 3.4.4." When is that reasoning wrong?

Proposition 3.4.4 opens "Assume that (X, d) is a complete metric space", and the closed ⟹ complete direction consumes that hypothesis immediately — it produces a limit in X and only then places it in A. Drop it and the direction dies: ℚ is closed in ℚ, and ℚ is not complete. The other direction, complete ⟹ closed, survives without the hypothesis, and Problem 2(b) asks you to prove exactly that asymmetry. The fourth option is the relativity of "closed" stated correctly but pointed at the wrong gap.

Segment 2 · 25 min

What the topology cannot see

an2-01 finished with the observation that the Manhattan, Euclidean and max metrics on ℝ² are three different functions with one collection of open sets, and drew the moral that a property of a metric space need not be a property of the metric. This segment runs that sentence backwards. Completeness is defined entirely in terms of the metric. Is it visible in the topology?

Lindström asks half of this question, in Exercise 4 to §3.4. He calls two metrics on the same set equivalent when there are constants K and M with d₁ ≤ K d₂ and d₂ ≤ M d₁ everywhere, and asks you to show that completeness transfers between equivalent metrics. It does, and the proof is three lines (Problem 3(a)). But look at what the hypothesis is: a pair of inequalities between numbers, with constants that do not depend on the points.

Generating the same open sets is weaker than that, and the gap is exactly the uniformity. Same topology says: around each point, each kind of ball contains one of the other kind. The radius you get is allowed to depend on the point. A uniform constant is a stronger demand, and the definition of Cauchy — two running indices, no point named — is precisely the kind of statement that needs uniformity to survive a change of metric.

So the prediction gate's answer is no, and here is the witness. On ℝ, put

ρ(x, y) = |arctan x − arctan y|.

Lindström has already put arctan on the table, at printed 57, when he observed that arctan(ℝ) = (−π/2, π/2) is not closed. Here it does different work: it is a bounded, strictly increasing bijection of ℝ onto that interval, continuous with continuous inverse, and ρ is the ordinary distance transported along it.

One set, two metrics, the same open sets. Above, ℝ with the usual distance: the points xₙ = n march off with every gap equal to 1, so the sequence is not Cauchy and nothing is missing. Below, the same points under ρ: their images arctan n crowd towards π/2, the gaps shrink to zero, and the sequence becomes ρ-Cauchy. The open circle is the place it is heading, and there is no point of ℝ there — the interval is open, which is the observation Lindström makes at printed 57 for an unrelated purpose. The dashed lines are the identity map: the same point of ℝ, drawn twice.

Guided proof — ρ is a metric, has the same open sets as d, and is not complete.

Prove all three. Keep the parts separate and, at each one, name the property of arctan you are using: injectivity, continuity, continuity of the inverse, or boundedness. Exactly one property is used in each part and one of them is used twice.

Start: for the first part, notice that you are not being asked to verify anything about arctan itself — ρ is the pullback of a metric you already trust along a map, so two of Definition 3.1.1's three conditions are free.
Reveal the model answer

ρ is a metric. Symmetry and the Triangle Inequality are inherited: ρ(x, y) = |u − v| with u = arctan x, v = arctan y, and |·| already satisfies both. Non-negativity likewise, and the codomain is [0, ∞) — in fact [0, π). The only condition needing anything is the "only if" half of Definition 3.1.1(i): ρ(x, y) = 0 gives arctan x = arctan y, and injectivity of arctan gives x = y. That is the one place a property of the map is consumed, and it is the same half of the same condition that an2-01 found doing all the work.

Same open sets. A ρ-ball is B_ρ(a; r) = {x : |arctan x − arctan a| < r}, which is the preimage under arctan of an interval — an open interval of ℝ containing a, by continuity of the inverse (tan carries the interval back to an interval). Conversely, given an ordinary open interval about a, continuity of arctan at a supplies an r with B_ρ(a; r) inside it. So each kind of ball contains one of the other around the same centre, and by Proposition 3.3.3 a set is ρ-open exactly when it is d-open.

Not complete. Take xₙ = n. Since arctan n → π/2, the real sequence {arctan n} is convergent, hence Cauchy by Proposition 3.4.2, so ρ(xₙ, x_m) = |arctan n − arctan m| is eventually as small as we like and {xₙ} is ρ-Cauchy. But for any a ∈ ℝ, ρ(n, a) → π/2 − arctan a, which is strictly positive because arctan a < π/2 for every real a — this is boundedness of arctan, used as the statement that the supremum is never attained. So {xₙ} converges to no point at all, and (ℝ, ρ) is incomplete. ∎

Which of Lindström's two inequalities survives. ρ ≤ M d holds with M = 1: arctan has derivative 1/(1 + x²) ≤ 1, so the Mean Value Theorem gives |arctan x − arctan y| ≤ |x − y|. The other, d ≤ K ρ, fails for every K — take x = n and y = n + 1, where d = 1 while ρ(n, n+1) → 0, so any proposed K is beaten as soon as ρ(n, n+1) < 1/K. Exercise 4 needs both inequalities, and this is the pair that shows why.

The moral, stated in the form you will reuse. Convergence, continuity, open and closed are topological — they can be said using only the collection of open sets. Cauchy, complete and bounded are not. ρ is bounded by π and d is unbounded on the same topology, which settles the last of those in one line.

Self-check 3. Where precisely does the proof of Exercise 4 — completeness transfers between equivalent metrics — break if the constant K is allowed to depend on the two points?

The Cauchy condition fixes N first and then quantifies over all n, m ≥ N. To turn a d₂-Cauchy sequence into a d₁-Cauchy one you feed ε/K into the d₂ condition and get a single N that works for every subsequent pair — which requires K to be available before the pair is chosen. A pointwise K is indeed a valid inequality at each pair, as the third option says, and that is exactly why it is useless: you cannot extract one N from infinitely many inequalities with infinitely many constants. This is the same uniformity gap that separates continuity from uniform continuity, which is an2-05's opening subject.

Self-check 4. Sort: which of these properties of a metric space can be stated using only its collection of open sets?

Convergence is "every open set containing a eventually contains xₙ", and continuity is Proposition 3.3.10's preimage statement — both mention no distance. Cauchy cannot be said that way, because it compares d(xₙ, x_m) across two running indices and an open set cannot tell you that one pair of points is closer than another. Complete then inherits the failure, since it quantifies over Cauchy sequences. Bounded fails too, and ρ against d is the one-line proof. The fourth option overshoots: convergence is phrased with a sequence and is perfectly topological.

Segment 3 · 30 min

Building the limits, and what the construction does not promise

If a space is missing limits, add them. The difficulty is that the only raw material available is the space itself, so the new points have to be built out of the old ones — and the only things in the old space that point at a missing limit are the Cauchy sequences that fail to converge. Lindström's move is to stop treating those sequences as evidence for a point and start treating them as the point.

Definition 3.7.1 (printed 71). D ⊆ X is dense in X if for each x ∈ X there is a sequence {yₙ} from D converging to x.

Proposition 3.7.2 (printed 72). D is dense in X if and only if for each x ∈ X and each δ > 0 there is a y ∈ D with d(x, y) ≤ δ. Left as Problem 1 to §3.7. The ≤ and the < forms are equivalent — apply one with δ/2 — and Lindström himself restates it with < at printed 107; but the ≤ is what Lemma 3.7.9's proof actually produces, for a reason worth seeing.

Definition 3.7.3 (printed 72). A completion of (X, d_X) is a metric space (X̄, d_X̄) such that (i) X ⊆ X̄ with d_X̄ restricting to d_X on X, and (ii) X is dense in X̄.
Proposition 3.7.4 (printed 72). Any two completions of (X, d_X) are isometric. Proved in the body; it is what licenses the definite article in "the completion".

Theorem 3.7.12 (printed 75). Every metric space has a completion. Proved in the body, by the construction below.

The construction is one long verification and it is worth reading for its shape rather than its details. Let 𝒳 be the set of all Cauchy sequences in X, and declare {xₙ} ∼ {yₙ} when lim d(xₙ, yₙ) = 0. Let X̄ be the set of equivalence classes, and try to define

d̄([xₙ], [yₙ]) = lim d(xₙ, yₙ).

Every word of that formula is a promise that has to be kept, and the lemmas keep them in order.

Lemma 3.7.5 (printed 73). For Cauchy {xₙ} and {yₙ}, the limit lim d(xₙ, yₙ) exists. Proved, via Proposition 3.1.4 — an2-01's Inverse Triangle Inequality — showing {d(xₙ, yₙ)} is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers, and then invoking completeness of ℝ.

Lemma 3.7.6 (printed 73). ∼ is an equivalence relation. Proved: reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, the last from the Triangle Inequality.

Lemma 3.7.7 (printed 74). The limit is unchanged if either sequence is replaced by an equivalent one — so d̄ is well defined on classes. Proved.

Lemma 3.7.8 (printed 74). (X̄, d̄) is a metric space. Proved, in a few lines, one for each condition.

Stop at the disproportion. Lemmas 3.7.5 to 3.7.7 take more than a page and Lemma 3.7.8 — the one that actually says "this is a metric space" — takes about one line per condition. That is not an accident of exposition. Look at what Lemma 3.7.8 has to prove hardest: positivity's "only if" half, that d̄([xₙ], [yₙ]) = 0 forces the two classes to be equal. And d̄([xₙ], [yₙ]) = 0 says lim d(xₙ, yₙ) = 0, which is the definition of ∼, which is [xₙ] = [yₙ]. The equivalence relation was chosen to make the hard axiom true by construction, and Lemmas 3.7.6 and 3.7.7 are the bill for that choice. an2-01 warned that condition (i) is the one most often false; here it has been arranged to be free, and the cost shows up two lemmas earlier.

Lemma 3.7.9 (printed 75). D = {x̄ : x ∈ X}, where x̄ is the class of the constant sequence, is dense in X̄. Proved.

Lemma 3.7.10 (printed 75). Every Cauchy sequence in D converges to an element of X̄. Proved.

Lemma 3.7.11 (printed 75). If D is dense in (Y, d) and every Cauchy sequence from D converges in Y, then (Y, d) is complete. Proved here — and set as Exercise 8 to §3.4, printed 63, twelve pages earlier. The same sentence is an exercise in one section and a lemma in another.

Two details in that block repay attention. In Lemma 3.7.9, Lindström needs a member of D close to a given class [xₙ], and the obvious candidate — the class of the constant sequence at the limit of {xₙ} — is unavailable, because that limit is exactly what may not exist. He takes x = x_N for an N from the Cauchy condition instead: a term of the sequence rather than its destination. That substitution is the entire section in miniature. And note the shape of the estimate it produces: d̄(x̄, [xₙ]) is a limit of quantities each less than ε/2, and a limit of strict inequalities need not be strict, so what comes out is ≤ ε/2. That is the reason Proposition 3.7.2 is stated with ≤ rather than <; Lindström then recovers a strict < ε by having halved in advance.

The last step is not decorative. Definition 3.7.3(i) demands X ⊆ X̄ as a set, and the elements of D are equivalence classes of sequences, not points of X — so Theorem 3.7.12's proof ends by swapping the copy back out for the originals, using that x ↦ x̄ is an embedding in the sense of Definition 3.1.3. Proposition 3.7.4 then says nothing depended on which construction we ran.

Faded — ℝ is the completion of ℚ, without doing the construction. Lindström's Problem 4 to §3.7.

Definition 3.7.3 has two clauses. Clause (i): ℚ ⊆ ℝ and the metric |x − y| restricts correctly — immediate. Clause (ii): ℚ is dense in ℝ, which is Definition 3.7.1 with the decimal expansions of §3.7's opening. So ℝ is a completion of ℚ.

⟨your step: you have verified a definition, not run a construction. Say what Proposition 3.7.4 now lets you conclude about the equivalence-class object built from Cauchy sequences of rationals, and write the one sentence explaining why the answer would be unsatisfying without it — what would "the completion of ℚ" mean if two constructions could disagree?⟩

And now Lindström's own warning, which is the most useful sentence in the section and the one that connects it to the strip — printed 76: the theorem "doesn't solve all problems with incomplete spaces as there may be additional structure we want the completion to reflect. If, e.g., the original space consists of functions, we may want the completion also to consist of functions, but there is nothing in the construction above that guarantees that this is possible."

Read that against what a new point of X̄ actually is: an equivalence class of Cauchy sequences of old points. Not a function. Not a diagram. Not anything of the original kind. So Theorem 3.7.12 gives you, for free and for every space:

(A) this space has a completion

and gives you nothing at all towards

(B) this space is already complete.

(A) is cheap and says nothing about what the new points are. (B) is a theorem about one space with one metric, and it is the one that means the limits you wanted are objects of the kind you started with. Every useful completeness statement in the corpus is of type (B).

You try. The audit, in three steps, and the middle one is where the work is.

The precondition. Definition 3.4.3 says a space is complete if all Cauchy sequences converge. an2-01 established that on diagrams in general the bottleneck distance is an extended pseudometric — distinct diagrams at distance zero, values in [0, ∞]. Show that "complete" is not a well-posed question in that setting, by finding the step of Proposition 3.2.3 that fails and saying what "converge" then fails to mean.

The first clause. tda2-09 reports Dey and Wang's Definition 13.9, which defines 𝔻qp by a finiteness condition, and their result that 𝔻q∞ is Polish. State exactly what that licenses about "diagram space", and say whether it is claim (A) or claim (B) above.

The second clause. Write out Oudot's Corollary 3.6 as tda1-07 has it and count its limits. Then find the theorem completeness really supports — tda2-09 reports Theorem 13.10 — and say what kind of statement it is. If you want the distinction in one line before you start, it is Lindström's own, at printed 62: "in a complete space you don't have to construct the limit object; you just have to check that the approximations form a Cauchy sequence."

Self-check 5. tda1-07 proves converse stability by showing the pointwise finite-dimensional modules are dense in the 𝔮-tame ones — each 𝕍 is the limit of its ε-smoothings 𝕍1/n — and then arguing by continuity. Which property is that argument using?

Density. The module 𝕍 is the thing being approximated, not the thing being produced — the ε-smoothings are built from it. Nothing in that argument needs a limit to exist, because the limit was the starting point; what it needs is that the approximations get arbitrarily close and that the function being evaluated is continuous. That is the exact division this unit installs: completeness manufactures, density transfers. The first option is the error the mission strip makes, transplanted one theorem to the left.

Self-check 6. Which of these does Theorem 3.4.5, Banach's Fixed Point Theorem, use completeness for?

One line, and one only: "Since (X, d) is complete, the sequence must converge to a point a." Everything before it is arithmetic with the contraction factor — the geometric-series bound d(xₙ, x_{n+k}) ≤ sⁿ d(x₀, x₁)/(1 − s) proves the iterates are Cauchy with no completeness anywhere — and everything after it is continuity. Uniqueness comes from d(a, b) ≤ s d(a, b) with s < 1, which needs only Definition 3.1.1. This unit does not prove Theorem 3.4.5; an2-04 does. It is here because it is the cleanest example of Lindström's remark: the fixed point is produced without ever being written down.
Blank page. Close the file and reconstruct three claims and the line between them.
Check against this 1. Cauchy names no limit, and that is the whole problem. Definition 3.4.1 differs from Definition 3.2.1 by one symbol — the point a — and losing it means Lemma 3.2.2 has nothing to convert. Convergent ⟹ Cauchy (Proposition 3.4.2) always; the converse is what a space may or may not have, so completeness is assumed, not derived. Proposition 3.4.4 ties it to closure, and only when the ambient space is complete: "closed" is relative, "complete" is absolute.

2. Completeness is a property of the metric that the topology cannot see. Lindström's Exercise 4 transfers it between metrics related by two uniform inequalities, and uniformity is the point: ρ(x, y) = |arctan x − arctan y| has exactly the same open sets as the usual metric on ℝ and is not complete, because xₙ = n becomes Cauchy without acquiring a limit. Convergent and continuous are topological; Cauchy, complete and bounded are not. This is an2-01's closing moral run backwards.

3. The completion exists for free and promises almost nothing. Points of X̄ are equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences; the equivalence relation is chosen so that the hard metric axiom holds by construction, which is why Lemma 3.7.8 is six lines and Lemmas 3.7.5 to 3.7.7 are a page. Lindström's Remark at printed 76 is the limit of the result: nothing guarantees the new points are objects of the original kind. So "X has a completion" is cheap and "X is complete" is a theorem — and the difference is exactly the difference between density, which transfers a property to a limit you hold, and completeness, which manufactures a limit you do not.
