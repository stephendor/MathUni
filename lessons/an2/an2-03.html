Compactness: sequential, total boundedness, Arzelà–Ascoli (an2-03)

You have known since school that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains its maximum, and you have probably filed "closed and bounded" as what makes that work. It is not. "Closed and bounded" is a fact about ℝⁿ and about nothing else — there is a complete, closed, bounded metric space in this lesson with a sequence that has no convergent subsequence at all. The property that actually does the work has a different name, and this unit is about what has to replace boundedness once you leave ℝⁿ: total boundedness in general, and equicontinuity in a space of functions. Same repair, twice.

Predict before we start. Take ℕ = {1, 2, 3, …} with the discrete metric: every distinct pair is at distance 1. Which of these does it fail?

The third. Every distance is 0 or 1, so the diameter is 1 and the space is bounded; it is the whole space, so it is closed in itself; and an2-02's worked example proved every discrete space complete, because a Cauchy sequence there is eventually constant. Yet xₙ = n has no convergent subsequence, for the same reason: d(n, m) = 1 for n ≠ m, so no subsequence is even Cauchy. The second option gets this backwards — 1, 2, 3, … is not Cauchy here. This is Lindström's Example 1 to §3.5, and it is the counterexample the whole unit is organised around.

Why this matters for the mission: Compactness of diagram and function spaces is the engine behind existence proofs in stability theory.

The strip is quoted exactly. Its central claim is the best-supported of any strip in this module, and the two spaces it names are the wrong two.

What is right. tda1-06 reads Oudot at his printed 51: a compactness argument shows the bottleneck infimum "is always attained when P, Q are locally finite multisets in ℝ² ∖ Δ", which is exactly what upgrades the extended pseudometric of an2-01 into a true distance on that class — and by Oudot's Theorem 1.13 the class contains every 𝔮-tame module's diagram. So compactness really is the engine, and the specific thing it delivers has a name in this unit: an infimum over a compact set is attained, which is Theorem 3.5.10, the Extreme Value Theorem. Segment 1 is about that theorem and Segment 3 sends you back here.

"Function spaces". Lindström's Exercise 7 to §4.8 asks you to show that C([0, 1], ℝ) is not even locally compact. Segment 3 does it. So no function space in this unit is compact; what Theorem 4.8.9 characterises is which subsets of one are — and equicontinuity is the price.

"Diagram space". tda2-09 reports that 𝔻q∞ is Polish, meaning complete and separable. This unit proves compact ⟹ complete (Proposition 3.5.7) and compact ⟹ separable (Proposition 4.8.3), so Polish is strictly weaker, and the prediction gate's space is the counterexample to the converse. The corpus therefore establishes nothing at all about compactness of diagram space, in either direction.

"In stability theory". Both places the corpus reaches for compactness, it reaches past its own pages: tda1-06 records that Oudot attributes the attainment argument to a reference and does not reproduce it, and tda1-07 records that "the compactness argument needed when dgm(𝕌ₓ) is infinite is referred to [72, §4.8] and not carried out". The one place compactness visibly does existence work in the corpus is tda2-09's Theorem 13.10 — a probability measure with compact support has a non-empty Fréchet expectation. Read the strip, then, as naming the right mechanism in the wrong two spaces and the wrong branch of the theory.
Segment 1 · 25 min

What compactness is, and the one theorem it exists for

Definition 3.5.2 (printed 63). A subset K of (X, d) is compact if every sequence in K has a subsequence converging to a point in K. The space (X, d) is compact if X is.

Definition 3.5.3 (printed 63). A ⊆ X is bounded if there is an M ∈ ℝ with d(a, b) ≤ M for all a, b ∈ A.

Proposition 3.5.1 (printed 63). If {xₙ} converges to a, so does every subsequence. Stated, and left as Problem 2 to §3.5 — "I leave the first result as an exercise".

Lindström attaches a Remark to Definition 3.5.2 and it is the whole definition in one sentence: "It is easy to overlook that the limit of the subsequence has to lie in the set K, but this is a crucial part of the definition." Every counterexample in this unit is that clause failing.

Take the Remark at its word straight away. In ℝ, the open interval (0, 1) has the property that every sequence in it has a subsequence converging — in ℝ. It is not compact, because 1/n has every subsequence converging to 0, which is not a member. Compactness is not "sequences behave"; it is "sequences behave and stay inside", and the second half is what makes it a strong condition.

Proposition 3.5.4 (printed 64). Every compact set is closed and bounded. Proved, contrapositively, in two halves.

Corollary 3.5.5 (printed 64). A subset of ℝⁿ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. Proved, from the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem 2.3.3 — an-08's, and quoted by Lindström from his own §2.3.

Read the hypothesis of Corollary 3.5.5 out loud: a subset of ℝⁿ. Lindström's next line is "Unfortunately, the corollary doesn't hold for metric spaces in general", and his Example 1 is the prediction gate's space: ℕ with the discrete metric is complete, closed and bounded, and xₙ = n has no convergent subsequence. Nothing was wrong with the sequence; there is simply no mechanism in a bare metric space forcing infinitely many points into a small region. In ℝⁿ that mechanism is Bolzano–Weierstrass, and it is a theorem about ℝⁿ.

Lemma 3.5.6 (printed 65). A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges, to the same limit. Proved, ε/2.

Proposition 3.5.7 (printed 65). Every compact metric space is complete. Proved, in two lines from the lemma.

Proposition 3.5.8 (printed 65). A closed subset of a compact set is compact. Proved.

Proposition 3.5.9 (printed 65). Continuous images of compact sets are compact. Proved, via Proposition 3.2.5.

Proposition 3.5.7 fixes compactness above completeness in the hierarchy: compact ⟹ complete (Definition 3.4.3), and never the reverse — ℝ is complete and not compact, and so is the prediction gate's ℕ. Proposition 3.5.9 is worth one extra beat, because it goes the opposite way from everything an2-01 taught you. Continuity controls preimages: preimages of open sets are open, preimages of closed sets are closed. Compactness is the one property that travels forward, along the image — and Problem 1(e) asks for the preimage counterexample, which is a constant map.

Theorem 3.5.10 (The Extreme Value Theorem, printed 65–66). If K is a nonempty compact subset of (X, d) and f : K → ℝ is continuous, then f has maximum and minimum points in K: there are c, d ∈ K with f(d) ≤ f(x) ≤ f(c) for all x ∈ K. Proved, the maximum half in the body, the minimum half left as Problem 3. Lindström gives two proofs and prefers the longer.
Worked — the Extreme Value Theorem, Lindström's longer route, and where the infinity goes.

Put M = sup{f(x) : x ∈ K}. We are not yet entitled to assume M is finite, so allow M = ∞ for now — this is the step the short proof hides.

Choose xₙ ∈ K with f(xₙ) → M. Compactness gives a subsequence x_{n_k} → c ∈ K. Then f(x_{n_k}) → M, since it is a subsequence of a sequence tending to M (Proposition 3.5.1); and f(x_{n_k}) → f(c), by Proposition 3.2.9, the version of Proposition 3.2.5 for functions defined on a subset. Limits in ℝ are unique, so f(c) = M.

And that is where M = ∞ died. It was never assumed away; it was excluded by the conclusion, because f(c) is a real number. The theorem proves boundedness of f and attainment of the bound in one stroke, and the sequence extraction is doing both jobs at once. ∎

Lindström's Remark on printed 66 gives the short proof: Proposition 3.5.9 makes f(K) compact, hence closed and bounded by Proposition 3.5.4, hence containing its own sup and inf. He prefers the long one because it "gives a better feeling for what is going on" — and Problem 2(b) asks you to say what that feeling is. Note that the long proof hands you the maximum point as an explicit limit; the short one hands you only the value.
Faded — positive continuous functions on a compact space are bounded away from zero. Lindström's Problem 7 to §3.5.

Let (X, d) be compact and f : X → ℝ continuous with f(x) > 0 for every x. Show there is a real a > 0 with f(x) > a for all x.

The whole proof is one application of Theorem 3.5.10 and one arithmetic step.

⟨your step: apply the Extreme Value Theorem to f, name the point where the minimum is attained and the value there, and produce a. Then — and this is the part that matters — take X = (0, 1) and f(x) = x, and say which clause of the argument breaks. The conclusion is not "positive continuous functions are bounded away from zero"; it is a conclusion about positive continuous functions on a compact space, and the difference is one attained infimum.⟩

Self-check 1. Which implication is false?

The third, and the prediction gate already refuted it — ℕ with the discrete metric has all three hypotheses and no convergent subsequence of xₙ = n. The first is Proposition 3.5.7, the second Proposition 3.5.4, the fourth Proposition 3.5.8. The tempting reason to believe the third is Corollary 3.5.5, which is true and carries the hypothesis "a subset of ℝⁿ" — dropping a hypothesis while keeping the conclusion is the single most common way a true theorem becomes a false one.

Self-check 2. Problem 2(e) replaces Banach's Theorem 3.4.5 by: X compact and d(f(x), f(y)) < d(x, y) for all x ≠ y — strict, with no uniform factor s < 1. That f still has a unique fixed point. What has compactness bought, and what has been lost?

Compactness makes g(x) = d(x, f(x)) attain its minimum, and if that minimum were positive at c then g(f(c)) < g(c) contradicts minimality — so the minimum is 0 and c is fixed. What is lost is the estimate (3.4.1): Banach's proof produces the fixed point as the limit of an explicit iteration with a geometric error bound, and this one produces it as a minimiser with no rate at all. The fourth option is true as a fact (Proposition 3.5.7) but it is not what compactness is buying here — completeness alone would not give the minimum. Uniqueness comes from the strict inequality in both theorems.

Segment 2 · 30 min

The repair, and a source that supplies its own negative control

If "bounded" is the hypothesis that fails to generalise, the question is what to put in its place. Boundedness is a statement at a single scale: one number M bounds every distance. Total boundedness is the same statement made to hold at every scale, with a finite count allowed to grow as the scale shrinks.

Definition 3.5.11 (printed 66). A ⊆ X is totally bounded if for each ε > 0 there are finitely many balls B(a₁, ε), …, B(aₙ, ε) with centres in A that cover A.
Proposition 3.5.12 (printed 66). Every compact set is totally bounded. Proved, contrapositively, by constructing a sequence with d(xₙ, x_m) ≥ ε for every n ≠ m.

Theorem 3.5.13 (printed 66–67). A subset A of a complete metric space X is compact if and only if it is closed and totally bounded. Proved, by nesting balls of radius 1, ½, ⅓, … each containing infinitely many terms and reading a subsequence off the nesting.

The sequence Proposition 3.5.12 builds — every pair at distance at least ε — is the prediction gate's space wearing a different hat, and it is why the discrete ℕ was the right counterexample all along. An infinite discrete space is bounded and not totally bounded: at any ε < 1 every ball is a single point, so no finite collection covers.

Guided proof — total boundedness is the right repair, and completeness is what makes it work.

Two parts. First prove Proposition 3.5.12. Then run Lindström's own negative control, Exercise 10 to §3.5, on the completeness hypothesis of Theorem 3.5.13: with the usual metric on ℚ, show that A = {q ∈ ℚ : 0 ≤ q ≤ 2} is closed in ℚ and totally bounded, but not compact.

Start: for the first part, argue contrapositively and build the sequence greedily — pick any x₁, then any point outside B(x₁, ε), then any point outside both balls, and keep going. The construction never stops precisely because no finite collection covers.
Reveal the model answer

Proposition 3.5.12. Suppose A is not totally bounded: some ε > 0 admits no finite covering by ε-balls. Pick x₁ ∈ A. Since B(x₁, ε) does not cover A, pick x₂ ∈ A ∖ B(x₁, ε). Since B(x₁, ε) ∪ B(x₂, ε) does not cover A, pick x₃ outside both. Continuing, we get {xₙ} with xₙ ∉ B(x₁, ε) ∪ … ∪ B(x_{n−1}, ε), so d(xₙ, x_m) ≥ ε for all n ≠ m. No subsequence of such a sequence is Cauchy, so by Proposition 3.4.2 no subsequence converges, and A is not compact. ∎

Exercise 10. Totally bounded. Given ε > 0, choose rationals 0 = q₀ < q₁ < … < q_N = 2 with consecutive gaps below ε; the finitely many balls B(qᵢ, ε) cover A. Closed in ℚ. Its complement in ℚ is {q < 0} ∪ {q > 2}, a union of two sets open in ℚ. Not compact. The rational decimal truncations of √2 form a sequence in A; every subsequence converges in ℝ to √2, so no subsequence converges to a point of A, since a limit in A would be a second limit in ℝ and Proposition 3.2.3 forbids that.

Which hypothesis was load-bearing, and it is not the obvious one. A is closed — genuinely closed, in the space it lives in. The temptation is to say it "has a hole at √2", but √2 is not a point of ℚ and closedness is relative, as an2-02's Proposition 3.4.4 insisted. What fails is the completeness of the ambient space: the nesting construction in Theorem 3.5.13's proof produces a Cauchy subsequence, and nothing in ℚ turns a Cauchy sequence into a limit. Lindström says so before stating the theorem — "we have now added the assumption that X is complete – without this condition, the statement is false (see Exercise 10)".

Worth noticing about the source. A textbook stating a theorem with a hypothesis, asserting the theorem is false without it, and pointing at the exercise that exhibits the failure is a source shipping its own watched failure. It is rarer than it should be, and it is the reason you can trust the hypothesis is not decoration.

The third description, which is the one that survives

Lindström opens §3.6 by saying why he is about to do the whole thing again: the covering description "is also the right one to use if one wants to extend the concept of compactness to even more general spaces, topological spaces. In such spaces, sequences are not always an efficient tool, and it is better to have a description of compactness in terms of coverings by open sets." — printed 68–69. top-08 is the syllabus unit that takes compactness up there; nothing in an2 assumes it.

Definition 3.6.1 (printed 69). K has the open covering property if every open covering of K has a finite subcovering. Lindström notes the term is not standard and "will disappear once we have proved that it is equivalent to compactness".
Proposition 3.6.2 (printed 69). Open covering property ⟹ compact. Proved, contrapositively, after an observation: if no subsequence converges to x, some ball around x holds only finitely many terms.

Lemma 3.6.3 (printed 70). For an open covering 𝒪 of A, the function f(x) = sup{r < 1 : B(x; r) ⊆ O for some O ∈ 𝒪} is continuous and strictly positive. Proved, by the Lipschitz bound |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ d(x, y), so δ = ε works.

Theorem 3.6.4 (printed 70). Compact ⟺ open covering property. Proved. Some authors call it the Heine–Borel theorem.

Corollary 3.6.5 (printed 70). The reformulation by closed sets: if a family ℱ of closed sets has the finite intersection property over K — every finite subfamily meets K — then K ∩ ⋂ℱ ≠ ∅. Stated, with "Proof. Left to the reader (see Exercise 7)."

Lemma 3.6.3 has a parenthesis that is easy to skim past and should not be: "the strange cut-off at 1 in the definition of f(x) below is just to make sure that the function is finite". Without it f could be a supremum over an unbounded set of radii — not a real number, so not something Theorem 3.5.10 can be applied to. That is an2-01 Problem 2(d) again: a supremum over an unbounded set falls out of Definition 3.1.1's codomain [0, ∞) before any condition is tested.

Now trace the dependency of Theorem 3.6.4, because it is the opposite of what the phrase "alternative description" suggests. The hard direction goes from the sequential definition to the covering property, and it passes through Theorem 3.5.10 (to get a positive minimum r for Lemma 3.6.3's function) and Proposition 3.5.12 (to cover K by finitely many balls of radius r/2). Both of those are metric facts. In a space with no distance, Lemma 3.6.3's function cannot even be written down — it is a supremum of radii — so the covering property has to be taken as the definition rather than derived. That is exactly Lindström's stated reason for preferring it.

Self-check 3. Lindström quotes an argument that every locally compact space is complete, in order to have it refuted — Problem 11(c) to §3.5. It says: since d(xₙ, x_m) gets small, "we can find an r > 0 such that B̄(xₙ, r) is compact, and x_m ∈ B̄(xₙ, r) for all m ≥ n". Where is the error?

The quantifiers have been swapped. Local compactness says: for each a there exists r_a > 0 with B̄(a; r_a) compact. The argument reads it as though one r served every point. In X = ℝ ∖ {0}, as xₙ approaches the missing 0 the available radii shrink to zero, and no fixed r works for a tail. The third option describes Lemma 3.5.6, which is true and is in fact the last step of the quoted argument — the only correct step in it. This is the same uniformity failure as an2-02's Self-check 3, and it is the third time in two units that a proof has broken at a quantifier and not at a theorem.

Segment 3 · 30 min

The same repair, for functions

Definition 4.8.1 (printed 107). A ⊆ X is dense if every x ∈ X is the limit of a sequence from A. Definition 3.7.1 repeated; Lindström restates Proposition 3.7.2 alongside it.

Definition 4.8.2 (printed 107). (X, d) is separable if it has a countable dense subset.

Definition 4.8.4 (printed 108). A family ℱ of functions X → Y is equicontinuous if for every ε > 0 there is one δ > 0 such that d_Y(f(x), f(y)) < ε whenever d_X(x, y) < δ — for every f ∈ ℱ at once. Definition 4.1.3 repeated from printed 80.

Read Definition 4.8.4 against Definition 4.1.1, uniform continuity: that one demands a single δ across all points; this one demands a single δ across all points and all functions in the family. Two uniformities stacked. And Proposition 4.1.2 — on a compact domain, continuous implies uniformly continuous — closes the first of them for free, which is why compactness of X is a standing hypothesis from here on.

Proposition 4.8.3 (printed 108). Every compact metric space is separable, and the countable dense set A can be chosen so that for each δ > 0 there is a finite A_δ ⊆ A with everything within δ of it. Proved, from Proposition 3.5.12: take the centres of the 1/n-ball coverings, for all n.

Lemma 4.8.5 (printed 108–109). For an equicontinuous sequence in C(X, Y) with X compact and Y complete, convergence at every point of such a dense A is enough to force convergence in C(X, Y). Proved, by an ε/4 argument with three legs.

Proposition 4.8.6 (printed 109). If X is compact and {fₙ} is a bounded, equicontinuous sequence in C(X, ℝᵐ), then {fₙ} has a convergent subsequence. Proved, by the diagonal argument. This is the hard half of Arzelà–Ascoli.

Two things about Proposition 4.8.3 that Lemma 4.8.5 then spends. Countability is what the diagonal argument needs — you cannot index the stages of a diagonal construction by an uncountable set. The finite A_δ is what the ε/4 argument needs, because it takes a maximum over A_δ and a maximum of infinitely many things is not available. Both halves of one proposition, each cashed separately.

Corollary 4.8.7 (printed 110). For X compact, every bounded, closed, equicontinuous 𝒦 ⊆ C(X, ℝᵐ) is compact. Proved, in three lines from Proposition 4.8.6.

Lemma 4.8.8 (printed 110). If continuous fₙ → f uniformly and xₙ → a, then fₙ(xₙ) → f(a). Proved, using Proposition 4.2.4 (an2-05's) for continuity of f. Lindström's Remark: "This lemma is not as obvious as it may seem – it is not true if we replace uniform convergence by pointwise!"

Theorem 4.8.9 (The Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem, printed 110–111). Let X be a compact metric space. A subset 𝒦 of C(X, ℝᵐ) is compact if and only if it is closed, bounded and equicontinuous. Proved; the remaining direction is a contradiction argument using Lemma 4.8.8.

Self-check 4. Lindström warns that Lemma 4.8.8 fails for pointwise convergence. Which family witnesses that?

The moving spike. Let fₙ be piecewise linear, 0 outside (1/(n+1), 1/(n−1)) and 1 at 1/n. Each fₙ is continuous; fₙ → 0 pointwise, since each fixed x > 0 is eventually outside the shrinking support and fₙ(0) = 0. But with xₙ = 1/n → 0 we get fₙ(xₙ) = 1, which does not tend to f(0) = 0. The third option fails because xₙ = 1 is a constant sequence and fₙ(1) = 1 → 1, which is the pointwise limit's value at 1 — no contradiction. Check against this 1. Compactness is "every sequence has a subsequence converging inside", and the last two words are the whole definition. It implies closed and bounded (3.5.4), complete (3.5.7), totally bounded (3.5.12) and separable (4.8.3); it travels forward along continuous maps (3.5.9) where openness and closedness travel backward; and the reason to want it is Theorem 3.5.10 — a continuous real function on a compact set attains its bounds, so an infimum over a compact set is reached, not merely approached.
