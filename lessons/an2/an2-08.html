Function spaces: C[a,b] (an2-08)

Five units of this module have leaned on one theorem. an2-02, an2-03, an2-04, an2-05, an2-06 and an2-07 all cite Theorem 4.6.4 — C(X, Y) is a complete metric space when X is compact and Y complete — and every one of them records it as "an2-08's page, printed 102". Turn to printed 102. Lindström states the theorem and gives no proof: it "sums up the results above". The summary is correct and this lesson assembles it. But the verb that six lessons have been implying is the wrong one, and finding that out is the first thing this unit does.

Predict before we start. Is the set of continuous functions a closed subset of the set of all functions [0, 1] → ℝ?

Closedness is not a property of a set. It is a relation between a set and a topology on the ambient space, and this is the cleanest place in the module to feel that. Under the uniform metric ρ, Cb(X, Y) is closed in B(X, Y) — Lindström's Proposition 4.6.1. Under the topology of pointwise convergence, which Munkres identifies as the product topology, it is not: gₙ(x) = xⁿ converges pointwise to the step function, and that is the same sequence an2-05 used to show a pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous. The two facts are one fact in two vocabularies. The fourth option's error is the parenthesis: Munkres exhibits three inequivalent topologies on a function space and the sup metric induces only one of them.

Why this matters for the mission: Filtration functions live in C(X); treating a whole space of functions as one object is the key move.

The strip is right, and this unit's job is to say which one object — because there are three, and only compactness makes two of them agree.

The move is real, and Lindström names it. "We shall now take a bold step and consider functions as elements in metric spaces. As we shall see later in the chapter, this will make it possible to use results from the theory of metric spaces to prove theorems about functions." That is the strip's second clause, and an2-04 already cashed it: Banach's Theorem 3.4.5 proved a differential equation had a solution, because the solution was a fixed point in a complete function space.

But "C(X)" is not one object. Munkres's Theorem 46.7 gives three topologies on the same set — uniform ⊃ compact convergence ⊃ pointwise — and they are genuinely different. The first two coincide exactly when X is compact. So a claim indexed to "C(X)" is ambiguous until the domain's compactness is settled, and a claim written with ‖f − g‖∞ is in the finest of the three.

And without compactness the metric does not exist. Lindström's warning at printed 102 is blunt: the supremum "can be infinite when f and g are just assumed to be continuous. As a metric is not allowed to take infinite values, this creates problems for the theory." His Exercise 1 to §4.6 is f(x) = x, g(x) = 0 on ℝ. So a stability bound of the shape tda1-07 reports — db(dgm f, dgm g) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞ — presupposes that the right side is finite, and Proposition 4.6.3 is what guarantees it: X compact.

No syllabus action on the strip. Both clauses are true. The constraint is that "filtration functions live in C(X)" is a claim with a hypothesis attached to X, and the hypothesis is compactness. Everything attributed to tda1-07 here is reported from that lesson and through it from Oudot; this unit opens neither.
Segment 1 · 25 min

Making a space out of functions, and the first thing that must be checked

Before ρ(f, g) = sup{dY(f(x), g(x)) : x ∈ X} can be a metric it must be a number, and for arbitrary continuous f and g it is not. Lindström's first move is therefore to restrict to bounded functions and prove finiteness by hand: fix a ∈ X, take Ma and Na bounding f and g away from their values at a, and then for every x

dY(f(x), g(x)) ≤ dY(f(x), f(a)) + dY(f(a), g(a)) + dY(g(a), g(x)) ≤ Ma + dY(f(a), g(a)) + Na.

The bound does not mention x, which is the whole of the argument and also the whole of the next one.

Proposition 4.5.1 (printed 99). ρ is a metric on B(X, Y) = {f : X → Y bounded}. Proved for the triangle inequality; positivity and symmetry are set as Exercise 4.

Proposition 4.5.2 (printed 100). {fn} → f in (B(X, Y), ρ) if and only if fn → f uniformly. Proved, in two lines, by quoting an2-05's Proposition 4.2.3.

Proposition 4.5.2 is the hinge of the unit and it says something worth pausing over: uniform convergence was never a new kind of convergence. It is ordinary metric convergence, in a metric space nobody had built yet. an2-05 spent a unit on a distinction of quantifiers; this page says the distinction was between convergence in one metric space and convergence in a product, and the rest of the module has been working in the first without saying so.

Theorem 4.5.3 (printed 100). If (Y, dY) is complete, so is (B(X, Y), ρ). Proved in three stages — pointwise limit from completeness of Y, upgrade to uniform, boundedness of the limit; the third stage is set as Exercise 5.

Note the shape, because it will repeat: the completeness comes from Y and nothing is asked of X at all — not compactness, not even a topology, since a bounded function needs only a set for a domain. This is an2-07's Theorem 5.4.8 in a different costume, and for the same reason: every limit taken is a limit in the codomain.

Worked — two distances, computed. Lindström's Exercises 1 and 2 to §4.5, printed 100, both set and neither worked.

f(x) = x and g(x) = x² on [0, 1]. ρ(f, g) = max[0,1](x − x²). Differentiate: 1 − 2x = 0 at x = ½, where the value is ¼. The endpoints give 0. So ρ(f, g) = ¼.

f(x) = sin x and g(x) = cos x on [0, 2π]. |sin x − cos x| = √2 |sin(x − π/4)|, whose maximum over a full period is √2, attained at x = 3π/4 and x = 7π/4. So ρ(f, g) = √2.

Lindström's own comment on this: "The metric ρ is mainly used for theoretical purpose, and we don't have to find the exact distance between two functions very often, but in some cases it's possible using techniques you know from calculus." Both of these are maximisation problems in one variable, which is what the sup metric always reduces to on a compact interval.
Proposition 4.6.1 (printed 101). Cb(X, Y) is a closed subset of B(X, Y). Proved, and the proof is three citations long: Proposition 3.3.7 (an2-01's) reduces closedness to sequences; Proposition 4.5.2 turns ρ-convergence into uniform convergence; Proposition 4.2.4 (an2-05's) says the uniform limit is continuous.

Self-check 1. Theorem 4.5.3 assumes Y complete and assumes nothing about X. Why is that not surprising?

Every limit is in the codomain. Fix x; the values {fn(x)} are Cauchy in Y because dY(fn(x), fm(x)) ≤ ρ(fn, fm), and completeness of Y produces f(x). X is only ever a place to stand. This is an2-07's Theorem 5.4.8 in different notation — ℒ(V, W) is complete when W is, with no hypothesis on V — and the reason is the same one. The fourth option is false twice over: an2-02 established that completeness is not a topological property, and X does carry a topology here whenever it is a metric space.

Printed 101 and 102, read carefully

Theorem 4.6.2 (printed 101). If Y is complete, so is (Cb(X, Y), ρ). Proved, in three lines: B(X, Y) is complete by Theorem 4.5.3, Cb is closed in it by Proposition 4.6.1, and a closed subspace of a complete space is complete by Proposition 3.4.4 — which is an2-02's, and which an2-02's lesson already flagged as the promise Lindström makes in Chapter 3 and keeps here.

Proposition 4.6.3 (printed 102). If X is compact, every continuous f : X → Y is bounded. Proved: the function h(x) = dY(f(x), f(a)) is continuous by the inverse triangle inequality Proposition 3.1.4, and attains a maximum by the Extreme Value Theorem Theorem 3.5.10.

The displayed inequality inside that last proof carries a slip worth noticing, because reading past it is how one learns to read past worse things. It renders as |h(x) − h(y)| = |dY(f(x), a) − dY(f(y), a)| ≤ dY(f(x), f(y)) — but a ∈ X and f(x) ∈ Y, so dY(f(x), a) is not a well-formed expression. The intended middle term is |dY(f(x), f(a)) − dY(f(y), f(a))|, which is exactly h's definition and exactly what Proposition 3.1.4 bounds. The proof is right; the line is mistyped.

Theorem 4.6.4 (printed 102). Let X be compact. Then ρ is a metric on C(X, Y), and if Y is complete so is (C(X, Y), ρ). States. Lindström's words before it: "The following theorem sums up the results above for X compact." No proof follows, and no exercise is attached. Reveal the model answer

Step 1 — that ρ is a metric on C(X, Y). X is compact, so by Proposition 4.6.3 every continuous f : X → Y is bounded; hence C(X, Y) = Cb(X, Y) as sets. On Cb(X, Y) ⊆ B(X, Y) the function ρ is finite and is a metric, by Proposition 4.5.1 restricted to a subset. This step consumes compactness of X and nothing else — Y is not asked to be complete, or anything.

Step 2 — that it is complete. Y complete gives (Cb(X, Y), ρ) complete by Theorem 4.6.2. Since the two sets are equal by Step 1 and carry the same ρ, (C(X, Y), ρ) is complete. This step consumes completeness of Y and nothing else. ∎

What the proof is made of, unwound. Theorem 4.6.2 is Theorem 4.5.3 (B complete) plus Proposition 4.6.1 (Cb closed in B) plus Proposition 3.4.4 (closed in complete is complete). Proposition 4.6.1 is Proposition 3.3.7 plus Proposition 4.5.2 plus Proposition 4.2.4. So the theorem six lessons have cited rests on one result from an2-01, one from an2-02, one from an2-03 and one from an2-05, and this unit is where they meet.

The answer to the real question: something proved elsewhere. The theorem is true and every ingredient is proved on the facing pages, so no citing lesson asserted anything false. Segment 3 · 30 min

Three topologies, and the one the corpus is written in

One basis element around f in each of Munkres's three topologies, with a competitor g inside it. Pointwise: finitely many vertical intervals; g may do anything between them. Compact convergence: an ε-band over a compact C ⊂ X only; g departs outside C. Uniform: the band covers everything. The first two coincide when X is discrete and the last two when X is compact — Theorem 46.7.

Theorem 46.1 (Munkres, printed 282). Convergence in the topology of pointwise convergence is pointwise convergence. Proved in one sentence: the pointwise topology on YX is the product topology, so this is Munkres's Lemma 43.3 restated. top-05's subject, arriving from the other side.

Theorem 46.2 (printed 283). Convergence in the topology of compact convergence is uniform convergence on every compact subspace. Stated; no proof and no exercise.

Theorem 46.7 (printed 285). (uniform) ⊃ (compact convergence) ⊃ (pointwise). If X is compact the first two coincide; if X is discrete the last two do. Stated — "whose proof is straightforward" — and set as Exercise 2.

Theorem 46.7 is the sentence the strip needs. "Filtration functions live in C(X)" names a set; it does not name a topology; and there are three. For a compact X the top two agree and the ambiguity is harmless, which is the case the corpus is always in — but it is harmless because of a hypothesis, not because the question does not arise.

Lemma 46.3 (printed 283). Locally compact, or first countable, implies compactly generated. Proved, both clauses.

Lemma 46.4 (printed 284). On a compactly generated X, a function is continuous as soon as each restriction to a compact subspace is. Proved.

Theorem 46.5 (printed 284). For X compactly generated and Y metric, 𝒞(X, Y) is closed in YX in the topology of compact convergence. Proved. Corollary 46.6 (printed 284). Hence a compact-convergence limit of continuous functions is continuous. Stated; immediate.

Lemma 46.3 has two clauses and an2-03 explains why. C([0, 1], ℝ) with the sup metric is not locally compact — an2-03 recorded that as Lindström's Exercise 7 to §4.8 — so the first clause fails for it. But it is a metric space, hence first countable, so the second clause applies and it is compactly generated anyway. A lemma with a redundant-looking second hypothesis is usually carrying a case the first one loses.

Theorem 46.8 (printed 285–286). On 𝒞(X, Y) with Y metric, the compact-open topology and the topology of compact convergence coincide. Proved, both inclusions.

Corollary 46.9 (printed 286). The compact-convergence topology on 𝒞(X, Y) does not depend on the metric of Y. Hence if X is compact, neither does the uniform topology. Stated; immediate from Theorem 46.8, whose statement of the compact-open topology mentions no metric.

One row of that table deserves a second look. an2-05's Theorem 6.2.6 — a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous — was proved there by an ε/3 argument about quantifiers. Proposition 4.6.1 and Munkres's Theorem 46.5 say the same thing as a statement about a set: 𝒞 is closed in the ambient function space. The ε/3 argument is what closedness costs when you have not yet built the space it is closed in.

That is an2-02's lesson, one level up. an2-02 showed that completeness is a property of the metric and not of the topology, with ρ(x, y) = |arctan x − arctan y| on ℝ as the witness — same open sets as the usual metric, not complete. Here the same split appears on a space of functions: the topology of C(X, Y) is determined by the topology of Y alone, while the metric ρ and every estimate written with it depend on which metric on Y was chosen. Completeness sits on the metric side of that line, which is why Theorem 4.6.4 has "Y complete" in its hypotheses and Corollary 46.9 does not.

Self-check 3. Corollary 46.9 says the topology of C(X, Y) does not depend on the metric of Y. an2-07 exhibited two inequivalent norms on C([0, 1], ℝ). Is that a counterexample?

The corollary's scope is the metrics on the codomain. Replace d on Y by any metric inducing the same topology and the compact-convergence topology on 𝒞(X, Y) is unchanged; for compact X so is the uniform topology. an2-07's ‖f‖₁ = ∫|f| is a metric on the function space built by integration, not by taking a supremum of dY-distances, so no choice of metric on Y produces it. The third option inverts the hypothesis — compactness of X is what makes the second sentence of the corollary available. The fourth is false on its facts: C([0, 1], ℝ) is first countable, hence compactly generated by Lemma 46.3.

Self-check 4. Theorem 46.11's converse needs X locally compact Hausdorff. What does that mean for homotopies?

Joint continuity is the stronger notion, and that is exactly what top-04 told at1-01. Theorem 46.11's free direction is f continuous ⟹ F continuous, with no hypothesis on X; the converse is the one that buys local compactness and Hausdorffness. So defining a homotopy as a continuous path t ↦ ht in the function space is a genuinely weaker definition on a badly behaved X, while defining it as a map on X × I always gives the strong version. The third option has the directions exactly reversed. The fourth overstates: the free direction is free.

Self-check 5. Lindström's Exercise 6 to §4.5 (printed 101) defines c₀ as the bounded real sequences under the sup metric; his Example 1 to §5.2 (printed 140) defines c₀ as the sequences tending to 0. What is going on?

A notation collision, and both of Lindström's claims are true of the object he defines in each place. Every null sequence is bounded, so §5.2's space sits inside §4.5's; the constant sequence 1 is bounded and not null, so the inclusion is strict. Exercise 6's space is complete — it is B(ℕ, ℝ) with ρ, so Theorem 4.5.3 gives it — and §5.2's has {ek} as a basis, which the bounded sequences do not. Standard usage reserves c₀ for the null sequences and calls the bounded ones ℓ∞. The first option's reasoning is wrong twice: a bounded sequence need not converge, and a convergent subsequence would not make the sequence null.

Self-check 6. Which of these is a property of the metric ρ rather than of the topology it generates?

Completeness — and an2-02 is where that was settled, with ρ(x, y) = |arctan x − arctan y| on ℝ generating the usual topology and failing to be complete. Corollary 46.9 is the same point read forwards: the topology of 𝒞(X, Y) is determined by the topology of Y, so anything topological about the function space survives a change of metric on Y, while ρ's numerical values and completeness need not. The other three options are all topological statements: closedness, convergence of a specified sequence, and preservation of continuity under limits are each phrased entirely in terms of open sets.
