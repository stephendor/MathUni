The contraction mapping theorem and applications (an2-04)

an2-02 built the machinery for producing a limit you cannot write down, and an2-03 built the machinery for extracting one from a crowd. This unit spends both, on the same problem, and the answers disagree. Two theorems solve y′ = f(t, y), y(0) = y₀. One demands more of f and hands back a unique solution on all of [0, ∞), produced by an iteration whose error after n steps you can compute before running it. The other demands almost nothing and hands back some solution on some interval, with no uniqueness and no bound. Which one you get is decided by a single inequality.

Predict before we start. Banach's Fixed Point Theorem says a contraction on a complete space has a unique fixed point, reached from any starting point. Which part of that conclusion does completeness pay for?

Existence alone, on one line of the proof. Uniqueness comes from d(a, b) ≤ s·d(a, b) with s < 1 and never mentions the space. The geometric estimate on d(xₙ, x_{n+k}) is arithmetic with the contraction factor and holds in any metric space at all. The fourth option overshoots: a contraction on an incomplete space still has at most one fixed point, and may have one — take a constant map. What completeness supplies is the one thing the iteration cannot supply for itself, which is somewhere for it to land.

Why this matters for the mission: Fixed-point arguments recur wherever an algorithm's convergence must be proven, not merely hoped.

This strip is true, and it is the first in the module that needs no rescoping — so the work is earning it rather than bounding it. Three things have to be in place before "not merely hoped" means anything precise.

The theorem. Theorem 3.4.5 turns an iteration into a proof: iterate anything, from anywhere, and you converge. The estimate. (3.4.1) turns "converges" into "converges this fast", and it is computable from two numbers before a single step is taken. The hypothesis. Completeness, without which neither is available. Segment 1 is those three.

Then the contrast, because a strip about proof rather than hope needs something to be contrasted with. Segment 3's Theorem 4.9.2 also produces a solution as the limit of computed approximations — Euler's method is an algorithm too — and its convergence proof gives you no rate, no uniqueness, and no way to tell which solution you computed. Lindström says it himself at printed 116: compactness methods "are strong in the sense that they can often prove local existence of solutions under very general conditions, but they are weak in the sense that they give very little information about the nature of the solution." That sentence is the strip's real content.

Where the corpus stands. tda2-09 reports that for the Fréchet mean on 𝔻₂², "an iterative algorithm computing a local minimum exists" and "the computational question remains open". That is Theorem 4.9.2's situation, not Theorem 3.4.5's: an algorithm, a limit, and no rate. What would upgrade it is a contraction factor — and the corpus reports neither that there is one nor that there is not.
Segment 1 · 25 min

One inequality, and everything that follows from it

Contraction (printed 61). f : X → X is a contraction if there is a positive s < 1 with d(f(x), f(y)) ≤ s·d(x, y) for all x, y ∈ X. Lindström's parenthesis is the whole definition: "note that the same s should work for all x, y ∈ X".

He then asserts two things without proof. Every contraction is continuous — take δ = ε, which works at every point, so contractions are uniformly continuous; and his Example 2 to §4.1 goes further, observing that the set of all contractions on X is equicontinuous in the sense of Definition 4.1.3, with δ = ε serving every one of them at once. And by induction d(f∘n(x), f∘n(y)) ≤ sⁿ d(x, y).
Theorem 3.4.5 (Banach's Fixed Point Theorem, printed 61). Let (X, d) be a complete metric space and f : X → X a contraction. Then f has a unique fixed point a, and for every starting point x₀ ∈ X the sequence x₀, x₁ = f(x₀), x₂ = f∘2(x₀), … converges to a. Proved in the body.

The proof has three movements and it is worth separating them, because only one of them mentions the space at all.

Uniqueness is one line and needs no hypothesis beyond the contraction inequality: if f(a) = a and f(b) = b then d(a, b) = d(f(a), f(b)) ≤ s·d(a, b), so (1 − s)d(a, b) ≤ 0, so d(a, b) = 0, so a = b by the "only if" half of Definition 3.1.1(i). Note what this means for an incomplete space: a contraction there still has at most one fixed point.

The estimate is arithmetic. By repeated use of the Triangle Inequality and then the induction above,

d(xₙ, x_{n+k}) ≤ ∑i=0k−1 d(x_{n+i}, x_{n+i+1}) ≤ ∑i=0k−1 sn+i d(x₀, x₁) = sn(1 − sk)/(1 − s) · d(x₀, x₁) ≤ sn/(1 − s) · d(x₀, x₁),

which is as small as you like for large n because s < 1. A geometric series was summed and nothing about X was used, so the iterates form a Cauchy sequence in any metric space.

Existence is the third movement and it is one sentence long: "Since (X, d) is complete, the sequence must converge to a point a." Then xₙ₊₁ = f(xₙ) for every n, and taking limits — legitimate because f is continuous, which is the first thing we proved about contractions — gives a = f(a). That is the entire use of Definition 3.4.3 in the theorem.

The estimate (3.4.1) (printed 62). Letting m → ∞ in the bound above,

d(xₙ, a) ≤ sn/(1 − s) · d(x₀, x₁).
Lindström: "This gives us complete control over the rate of convergence – if we know the contraction factor s and the length d(x₀, x₁) of the first step in the iteration process, we have very precise information about how good an approximation xₙ is of the fixed point. This is important in numerical applications of the method."

Read that against everything an2-03 proved. Theorem 3.5.10 says a continuous function on a compact set attains its minimum — and gives you no way whatever to say where, or how close the tenth term of a minimising sequence is. (3.4.1) is a different kind of object: two numbers in, and a decision about how many steps to run comes out, before any step is taken.

The iteration xₙ₊₁ = 0.55·xₙ + 0.3 from x₀ = 0.05, staircasing to the fixed point a = 2/3 where the graph meets the diagonal. Each vertical step applies f; each horizontal step carries the output back to the input axis. The contraction factor is the slope, 0.55, and it is what makes the steps shrink. The table is the point of the figure: for this map the estimate (3.4.1) is not an over-estimate but an equality, at every n. That is a fact about affine contractions and it is proved in the box below — which means (3.4.1) cannot be improved in general, since a bound attained by an example is the best a theorem of that form can say.

Guided proof — (3.4.1) is attained, so it is sharp.

Let f(x) = sx + c on ℝ with 0 < s < 1. Show that f is a contraction with factor exactly s, find its fixed point a, and then prove that for every starting point x₀,

|xₙ − a| = sn/(1 − s) · |x₀ − x₁|,
with equality, not merely ≤. Conclude that no constant smaller than 1/(1 − s) can replace the one in (3.4.1).

Start: compute x₁ − a in terms of x₀ − a, and separately compute x₁ − x₀ in terms of x₀ − a. Both are one line, and the second is the one people skip.
Reveal the model answer

Contraction. |f(x) − f(y)| = |sx − sy| = s|x − y|, so s works and no smaller number does — the factor is exactly s. ℝ is complete, so Theorem 3.4.5 applies.

Fixed point. a = sa + c gives a = c/(1 − s), which exists because s ≠ 1.

The error. x₁ − a = f(x₀) − f(a) = s(x₀ − a), and by induction xₙ − a = sn(x₀ − a). So |xₙ − a| = sn|x₀ − a|.

The first step. x₁ − x₀ = sx₀ + c − x₀ = (s − 1)x₀ + c. Now substitute c = a(1 − s): x₁ − x₀ = (s − 1)x₀ + a(1 − s) = (1 − s)(a − x₀). So |x₁ − x₀| = (1 − s)|x₀ − a|.

Put them together. sn/(1 − s) · |x₀ − x₁| = sn/(1 − s) · (1 − s)|x₀ − a| = sn|x₀ − a| = |xₙ − a|. Equality at every n and every x₀. ∎

Why sharpness matters. (3.4.1) is a bound over all complete spaces and all contractions, and this family attains it. So any attempt to prove a better constant must fail on this example — the looseness you will see for other maps is not slack in the theorem, it is slack in those maps. Segment 2's Picard iteration on y′ = y overshoots by a factor of about 220, and the reason is that the exponential's Taylor error shrinks factorially while (3.4.1) can only promise geometrically.

Not in Lindström. He states (3.4.1) and calls it "very precise information"; he does not exhibit a case of equality. The computation above is this lesson's.

Self-check 1. f : (0, 1) → (0, 1), f(x) = x/2. It is a contraction with s = 1/2. How many fixed points?

None. Every iterate sequence is Cauchy — the estimate is arithmetic and holds here — but 0 is not a point of (0, 1), so it converges to nothing in the space. an2-02's Proposition 3.4.4 says why: (0, 1) is not closed in the complete ℝ, so it is not complete. This is the cleanest demonstration that completeness is doing existence work and nothing else: uniqueness survives here in the vacuous form "at most one", and there is not one.

For Exercise 5, the Mean Value Theorem converts the derivative bound into |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ s|x − y|, and [0, 1] is closed in ℝ, hence complete by Proposition 3.4.4.

Self-check 2. Lindström's Exercise 7 to §3.4: if f∘n is a contraction for some n, then f has a unique fixed point — even if f itself is not a contraction. Why does the fixed point of f∘n have to be fixed by f?

Let a be the unique fixed point of g = f∘n. Then g(f(a)) = f∘n(f(a)) = f(f∘n(a)) = f(a), so f(a) is a fixed point of g; uniqueness forces f(a) = a. The first option has the implication in the easy direction only — a fixed point of f is certainly fixed by f∘n, and the converse is what needs the argument. This matters in practice for exactly the reason Segment 2 will show: an operator can fail to be a contraction on a long interval while some iterate of it is one.

Segment 2 · 30 min

A differential equation is a fixed-point problem

Lindström opens §4.7 with the question the whole chapter has been inviting: "So far it may seem that we have been creating a theory of metric spaces for its own sake. It's an impressive construction where things fit together nicely, but is it of any use?" — printed 103

The first move is to stop looking at the derivative. Integrating y′(t) = f(t, y(t)) with y(0) = y₀ gives

y(t) = y₀ + ∫₀t f(s, y(s)) ds,

and differentiating goes back, so the two problems have the same solutions. That is not a cosmetic rewriting. Define F by F(z)(t) = y₀ + ∫₀t f(s, z(s)) ds. F accepts any continuous z and returns a differentiable function, so it is a self-map of C([0, a], ℝⁿ) — whereas "apply z ↦ z′" is not an operator on that space at all, because a continuous function need not be differentiable. The integral form is what makes the fixed-point framing possible, and a solution of the differential equation is exactly a fixed point of F.

Lindström's aside, printed 104: "Don't worry if you feel a little dizzy; that's just normal at this stage! Note that F is a function acting on a function z to produce a new function u = F(z) – it takes some time to get used to such creatures!"

Uniformly Lipschitz (printed 105). f : [a, b] × ℝⁿ → ℝⁿ is uniformly Lipschitz with constant K if ‖f(t, y) − f(t, z)‖ ≤ K‖y − z‖ for all t ∈ [a, b] and all y, z ∈ ℝⁿ.

Lipschitz in the second variable, uniform in the first — the same K at every time. That asymmetry is what "uniformly" names, and it is what lets Theorem 4.7.2 extend its solution interval after interval without the constant degrading.
Lemma 4.7.1 (printed 105). If f is continuous and uniformly Lipschitz with constant K on [0, ∞), and a < 1/K, then F : C([0, a], ℝⁿ) → C([0, a], ℝⁿ) is a contraction. Proved, in four inequalities.

Theorem 4.7.2 (printed 105–106). Under the same hypotheses, the initial value problem y′ = f(t, y), y(0) = y₀ has a unique solution on all of [0, ∞). Proved, from Lemma 4.7.1, Theorem 3.4.5, and Theorem 4.6.4 for the completeness of C([0, a], ℝⁿ) — printed 102, an2-08's.
Worked — Lemma 4.7.1, and the move you have now made three times.

For v, w ∈ C([0, a], ℝⁿ) and any t ∈ [0, a]:

‖F(v)(t) − F(w)(t)‖ = ‖∫₀t (f(s, v(s)) − f(s, w(s))) ds‖
  ≤ ∫₀t ‖f(s, v(s)) − f(s, w(s))‖ ds  (norm inside the integral)
  ≤ ∫₀t K‖v(s) − w(s)‖ ds  (the Lipschitz condition, at each s)
  ≤ K ∫₀a ρ(v, w) ds  (each value is at most the sup; and t ≤ a)
  = Ka · ρ(v, w).

The right-hand side does not depend on t, so it bounds every member of the set whose supremum is ρ(F(v), F(w)) — and therefore bounds that supremum:

ρ(F(v), F(w)) ≤ Ka · ρ(v, w),
with Ka < 1 by the choice of a. ∎

That last step is an2-01's, for the third time. "An upper bound for every member is an upper bound for the supremum" proved the sup metric's triangle inequality in an2-01, bounded the bottleneck infimum in an2-01's Self-check 5, and is now the whole reason an integral inequality becomes a statement about a metric on a function space. It is worth collecting.

Theorem 4.7.2 then extends. Lemma 4.7.1 gives a unique solution on [0, a]; repeat the argument on [a, 2a] with y(a) as the new initial value, and the two pieces agree because the integral splits. The crucial point is that the same a works every time, because a was chosen from K alone — a < 1/K, with no reference to the initial value or to which interval you are on. So the intervals tile [0, ∞) and the solution is global.

Lindström names the iteration — printed 106: "the fixed point in that theorem is obtained by iteration. This means that the solutions of our differential equation can be approximated by iterating the map F. In numerical analysis this way of obtaining an approximate solution is referred to as Picard iteration."

Faded — Picard iteration on y′ = y, and the bound before the run.

Take y′ = y, y(0) = 1, so F(z)(t) = 1 + ∫₀t z(s) ds, and start from z₀ ≡ 1. Then

z₁(t) = 1 + ∫₀t 1 ds = 1 + t,   z₂(t) = 1 + ∫₀t (1 + s) ds = 1 + t + t²/2,   z₃(t) = 1 + t + t²/2 + t³/6.

Here K = 1, so take a = 1/2 and the contraction factor is Ka = 1/2.

Self-check 3. Why does Theorem 4.7.2's extension to [a, 2a], [2a, 3a], … cover all of [0, ∞), while Theorem 4.9.2's does not extend at all?

a < 1/K is fixed in advance by the Lipschitz constant, so every interval has the same length and finitely many of them reach any T. In Theorem 4.9.2, a is chosen so that the Euler approximations cannot escape a box around the current position — so it depends on that position. Lindström at printed 115: "the second interval may be very small compared to the first one, the third one even smaller, and so on. Even if we add an infinite number of intervals, we may still only cover a finite part of the real line." And he adds that this is not a defect of the proof: "The differential equation may only have solutions that survive for a finite amount of time." y′ = 1 + y² is that equation.

Segment 3 · 25 min

The other engine, and what it costs

Drop the Lipschitz condition entirely and keep only continuity. Theorem 4.7.2 is gone — there is no contraction and no K. §4.9 solves the problem anyway, with an2-03's machinery instead of an2-02's.

The idea is Euler's method. Partition time into steps of length Δt, move along the current derivative on each step, and write s̲ for the largest grid point ≤ s. The resulting broken-line approximation satisfies

ŷ(t) = y₀ + ∫₀t f(s̲, ŷ(s̲)) ds = y₀ + ∫₀t f(s, ŷ(s)) ds + ∫₀t (f(s̲, ŷ(s̲)) − f(s, ŷ(s))) ds,

and the last integral is exactly the amount by which ŷ fails to solve the equation. The proof's job is to make it vanish.

Lemma 4.9.1 (printed 113–114). If continuous ŷ_k → y uniformly, then ∫₀t f(s, ŷ_k(s)) ds → ∫₀t f(s, y(s)) ds uniformly. Proved, using uniform continuity of f on a compact box — Proposition 4.1.2 — and the boundedness of a uniformly convergent sequence, which he flags "(prove this!)" and sets as Exercise 1 to §4.9.

Theorem 4.9.2 (printed 114). If f : [0, ∞) × ℝm → ℝm is merely continuous and y₀ ∈ ℝm, then there exist a > 0 and y : [0, a] → ℝm with y(0) = y₀ and y′(t) = f(t, y(t)) on [0, a]. Proved, printed 114–115.

Lindström's Remark, attached to the statement: "Note that there is no uniqueness statement (the problem may have more than one solution), and that the solution is only guaranteed to exist on a bounded interval (it may disappear to infinity after finite time)."

The architecture is five steps, and compactness appears in three of them. Bound f on a big compact box C, using Theorem 3.5.10. Choose a so small that the Euler approximations cannot leave a smaller box A ⊆ C — possible because every derivative is bounded by M. Observe that {ŷₙ} is bounded, since it stays in A, and equicontinuous, since the derivatives are bounded by the same M for every n. Extract a uniformly convergent subsequence by Proposition 4.8.6, which is Arzelà–Ascoli's hard half. Pass to the limit, using Lemma 4.9.1 on one integral and uniform continuity of f on the compact A — Proposition 4.1.2 again — on the other.

Now Lindström's Remark, and where each half of it comes from. No uniqueness is the subsequence's fault: Proposition 4.8.6 produces a convergent subsequence, a different extraction may converge elsewhere, and nothing shows the whole sequence converges. For y′ = (3/2)y1/3 nothing could, since the limits genuinely differ. Only a bounded interval is the box's fault: a was chosen to keep the approximations inside A, and that choice depends on M, which depends on the box, which depends on where the solution currently is.

Self-check 4. Which step of Theorem 4.9.2's proof is responsible for the absence of uniqueness?

The subsequence. Proposition 4.8.6 says a bounded equicontinuous sequence has a convergent subsequence; it says nothing about which, and nothing forces two extractions to agree. For y′ = (3/2)y1/3 they demonstrably do not, since that problem has a one-parameter family of solutions. Compare Theorem 3.4.5, where the limit is independent of the starting point, let alone of any extraction — that independence is what uniqueness is.

Self-check 5. You need to solve y′ = f(t, y) numerically to three decimal places and certify the answer. Which theorem do you want to apply, and what do you have to check first?

Theorem 4.7.2, and the thing to check is the uniform Lipschitz condition, because that is what makes the Picard operator a contraction and hands you (3.4.1). Self-check 6. For which of these is (3.4.1) an equality rather than a strict inequality?

Affine contractions, as the guided proof shows: xₙ − a = sⁿ(x₀ − a) and x₁ − x₀ = (1 − s)(a − x₀), so the two sides of (3.4.1) coincide identically. That makes (3.4.1) sharp — no smaller constant than 1/(1 − s) can work in general. The fourth option describes the degenerate case where both sides are 0, which is true and is not what sharpness means. The first is wrong for the Picard example, where the bound overshoots by a factor of about 220.

Check against this 1. Theorem 3.4.5 has three separable movements and completeness pays for exactly one. Uniqueness is d(a,b) ≤ s·d(a,b) and needs no space. The Cauchy estimate is a geometric series and needs no space.
