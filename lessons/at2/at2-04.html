Cup product and the cohomology ring (at2-04)

Cohomology classes multiply. Two spaces with identical Betti numbers can now be told apart.

Homology has an addition and nothing else. You can say that two spaces have the same Betti numbers, and if they do, homology has nothing further to offer. Cohomology has a multiplication — and the reason is a piece of luck we can now name: contravariance. A product needs a map X → X × X to pull back along, and the diagonal x ↦ (x,x) is sitting right there.

The multiplication is defined by an embarrassingly concrete formula on cochains: cut a simplex at a vertex, evaluate one cochain on the front piece, the other on the back, and multiply in R. Everything in this lesson follows from that formula and one Leibniz rule.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The formula (φ ⌣ ψ)(σ) = φ(σ|[v0,…,vk]) · ψ(σ|[vk,…,vk+ℓ]) obviously depends on the order of the vertices. Predict what that costs us: is the induced product on cohomology commutative?

Correct, and the sign is not a blemish but the whole character of the subject. Theorem 3.11 says α ⌣ β = (−1)kℓ β ⌣ α for a commutative coefficient ring, which for two 1-dimensional classes means α ⌣ β = −β ⌣ α. You will see this happen concretely on a genus-2 surface in Segment 1, and the immediate consequence — that odd-dimensional classes satisfy 2α² = 0 — is what makes the whole theory usable.

Why this matters for the mission: The cup product makes H∗(X;R) a graded ring (aa-05's structure, now on a topological invariant), and it is strictly finer than homology: S2 × S1 and S2 ∨ S1 ∨ S3 have isomorphic homology groups but non-isomorphic cohomology rings.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The formula, the Leibniz rule, and three computations

Hatcher notes that the introduction to the chapter sketched cup product via a cross product, but that defining the cross product from scratch takes work, so we go the other way: an elementary formula first, cross product afterwards. Throughout, coefficients live in a ring R — most commonly ℤ, ℤn or ℚ.

Cup product of cochains. For φ ∈ Ck(X;R) and ψ ∈ Cℓ(X;R), the cup product φ ⌣ ψ ∈ Ck+ℓ(X;R) is the cochain whose value on a singular simplex σ : Δk+ℓ → X is

(φ ⌣ ψ)(σ) = φ(σ | [v0, ⋯, vk]) · ψ(σ | [vk, ⋯, vk+ℓ]),

the product on the right being taken in R. — Hatcher §3.2, p. 206

Read the formula geometrically. The simplex is cut at the single vertex vk: φ eats the front face spanned by v0 through vk, ψ eats the back face spanned by vk through vk+ℓ, and the two faces share exactly that one vertex. The picture below is worth more than the formula.

To know that this descends to cohomology we need one identity relating it to δ.

Lemma 3.6. δ(φ ⌣ ψ) = δφ ⌣ ψ + (−1)k φ ⌣ δψ, for φ ∈ Ck(X;R) and ψ ∈ Cℓ(X;R). — Hatcher §3.2, Lemma 3.6, p. 206

Proof. For σ : Δk+ℓ+1 → X, expanding the two terms gives

(δφ ⌣ ψ)(σ) = Σi=0..k+1 (−1)i φ(σ|[v0,⋯,v̂i,⋯,vk+1]) ψ(σ|[vk+1,⋯,vk+ℓ+1]),
(−1)k(φ ⌣ δψ)(σ) = Σi=k..k+ℓ+1 (−1)i φ(σ|[v0,⋯,vk]) ψ(σ|[vk,⋯,v̂i,⋯,vk+ℓ+1]).

Adding them, the last term of the first sum cancels the first term of the second, and what remains is exactly (φ ⌣ ψ)(∂σ) = δ(φ ⌣ ψ)(σ), since ∂σ = Σi (−1)i σ|[v0,⋯,v̂i,⋯,vk+ℓ+1]. ∎

Everything now follows in three lines. The cup product of two cocycles is a cocycle. The cup product of a cocycle and a coboundary is a coboundary in either order: if δφ = 0 then φ ⌣ δψ = ±δ(φ ⌣ ψ), and if δψ = 0 then δφ ⌣ ψ = δ(φ ⌣ ψ). Hence there is an induced product

Hk(X;R) × Hℓ(X;R)  ⌣⟶  Hk+ℓ(X;R),

associative and distributive because the cochain-level product visibly is, and with identity the class 1 ∈ H0(X;R) of the 0-cocycle taking the value 1 on every singular 0-simplex. The same formula defines a cup product for simplicial cohomology, and the canonical isomorphism between simplicial and singular cohomology respects it — which is what makes hand computation possible.

Worked example 1: the genus-g surface

Example 3.7. Let M be the closed orientable surface of genus g ≥ 1, with the Δ-complex structure Hatcher draws for g = 2. The product of interest is H1(M) × H1(M) → H2(M), with ℤ coefficients.

Step 1 — a basis, and its dual. A basis for H1(M) is formed by the edges ai and bi. Since H1(M) ≅ Hom(H1(M), ℤ) — by cellular cohomology or by the universal coefficient theorem of at2-02 — the basis determines a dual basis: αi assigns 1 to ai and 0 to the other basis elements, and βi is dual to bi.

Step 2 — represent by cocycles, geometrically. To represent αi by a simplicial cocycle φi we must choose values on the edges radiating from the central vertex so that δφi = 0. This is the cocycle condition from at2-01, which there acquired a geometric reading in terms of curves transverse to the edges. Take the arc labelled αi, a loop meeting ai in one point and disjoint from the other basis loops, and let φi have value 1 on edges meeting that arc and 0 elsewhere: φi counts intersections with the arc. Similarly ψi counts intersections with the arc βi.

Step 3 — apply the definition. Keeping the vertex ordering of each 2-simplex compatible with the indicated edge orientations, φ1 ⌣ ψ1 takes the value 0 on every 2-simplex except the one with outer edge b1, where it takes the value 1. So φ1 ⌣ ψ1 evaluates to 1 on the 2-chain c formed by the signed sum of all the 2-simplices, and ∂c = 0. With no 3-simplices present, c is not a boundary, so it represents a non-zero element of H2(M).

Step 4 — read off the class. That (φ1 ⌣ ψ1)(c) generates ℤ shows both that c generates H2(M) ≅ ℤ and that φ1 ⌣ ψ1 represents the dual generator γ of H2(M) ≅ ℤ. So α1 ⌣ β1 = γ, and in the same fashion:

Distributivity extends this table to the whole of H1(M) × H1(M). Note that the product is not commutative here: αi ⌣ βi = −(βi ⌣ αi). Hatcher's remark is that this is the worst that can happen, and Theorem 3.11 below says so precisely. Note also the geometry: non-zero products occur exactly when the corresponding loops intersect.

Worked example 2 (faded): Example 3.9. Let X be the 2-complex obtained by attaching a 2-cell to S1 by the degree m map z ↦ zm, with the Δ-complex structure of an m-gon of triangles Ti around a central vertex, outer edges identified by rotations. Compute α² for the generator α of H1(X;ℤm).

Why ℤm coefficients. With ℤ coefficients Hn(X;ℤ) is ℤ for n = 0 and ℤm for n = 2, so there is nothing in dimension 1 to multiply. With ℤm coefficients Hi(X;ℤm) ≅ ℤm for i = 0, 1, 2, so a product of two 1-dimensional classes can be non-zero.

The cocycle. A generator α is represented by φ with φ(e) = 1 on the edge e generating H1(X). The cocycle condition reads φ(ei) + φ(e) = φ(ei+1) with subscripts mod m, so we may take φ(ei) = ⟨your step: which value?⟩.

The square. By the cup product formula, (φ ⌣ φ)(Ti) = φ(ei)φ(e) = i. Evaluating on the generator ΣiTi of H2(X;ℤm) gives 0 + 1 + ⋯ + (m−1), which in ℤm equals ⟨your step: for m odd? for m = 2k?⟩.

Conclusion. Writing β for a generator of H2(X;ℤm): α² = 0 when m is odd, and α² = kβ when m = 2k. For m = 2 the space is ℝP2 and α² = β, agreeing with Example 3.8, where the nonorientable surface of genus g is treated with ℤ2 coefficients and αi ⌣ αi comes out as the non-zero element of H2(N;ℤ2).

Gap solutions: φ(ei) = i; and 0 + 1 + ⋯ + (m−1) is 0 in ℤm for odd m, and k for m = 2k, since 1 cancels with m−1, 2 with m−2, and so on.

Self-check 1. Why does the cup product formula produce a cochain of degree exactly k + ℓ?

Count vertices. The front face uses v0 through vk, which is k + 1 vertices; the back face uses vk through vk+ℓ, which is ℓ + 1; they overlap in the single vertex vk, so the total is k + ℓ + 1 vertices, spanning Δk+ℓ. The overlap of exactly one vertex is what makes the degrees add rather than adding with an error term.

Self-check 2. In Lemma 3.6, why is the sign (−1)k attached to the second term rather than the first?

The proof is a cancellation: the term of (δφ ⌣ ψ)(σ) omitting vk+1 and the term of (φ ⌣ δψ)(σ) omitting vk are the same expression, and the factor (−1)k is exactly what makes them appear with opposite signs. Move the sign and the cancellation fails. The third option is a serviceable mnemonic — it is the graded Leibniz pattern — but it is not the reason the identity is true here.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The ring, its commutativity, and what it can distinguish

Two structural facts turn the product into an algebraic invariant worth carrying about.

Proposition 3.10. For a map f : X → Y, the induced maps f∗ : Hn(Y;R) → Hn(X;R) satisfy f∗(α ⌣ β) = f∗(α) ⌣ f∗(β), and similarly in the relative case. — Hatcher §3.2, Proposition 3.10, p. 210

Proof. Everything happens at cochain level. Evaluating on σ,

(f♯φ ⌣ f♯ψ)(σ) = φ(fσ|[v0,…,vk]) ψ(fσ|[vk,…,vk+ℓ]) = (φ ⌣ ψ)(fσ) = f♯(φ ⌣ ψ)(σ),

so f♯(φ) ⌣ f♯(ψ) = f♯(φ ⌣ ψ); as f♯ is a cochain map, this descends to classes. ∎

Theorem 3.11. The identity α ⌣ β = (−1)kℓ β ⌣ α holds for all α ∈ Hk(X,A;R) and β ∈ Hℓ(X,A;R), when R is commutative. — Hatcher §3.2, Theorem 3.11, p. 210

Sketch of the proof, and why it is not a formality. The cochains φ ⌣ ψ and ψ ⌣ φ differ by a permutation of the vertices of Δk+ℓ, and the trick is to use the one permutation that reverses their order totally — because that also reverses the ordering of vertices in every face. For a singular n-simplex σ, let σ̄ be σ precomposed with the linear homeomorphism reversing the vertex order, and set ρ(σ) = εnσ̄ with εn = (−1)n(n+1)/2, the sign accounting for the n(n+1)/2 adjacent transpositions involved. Hatcher verifies that ρ is a chain map, using εn = (−1)nεn−1, and constructs an explicit chain homotopy P from ρ to the identity, modelled on the prism operator. Granting that, the two displayed formulas for ρ∗φ ⌣ ρ∗ψ and ρ∗(ψ ⌣ φ) give εkεℓ(ρ∗φ ⌣ ρ∗ψ) = εk+ℓρ∗(ψ ⌣ φ), and the arithmetic identity εk+ℓ = (−1)kℓεkεℓ finishes it once the ρ∗'s disappear on cohomology. The proof also covers A ≠ ∅, since ρ and P take chains in A to chains in A. ∎

The consequence that gets used constantly: taking α = β with k odd gives α² = −α², so 2α² = 0. Hence if H2k(X,A;R) has no element of order two, then α² = 0 outright. For the space of Example 3.9 this says the square of a generator of H1(X;ℤm) is zero when m is odd, and is either zero or the unique element of order two when m is even — and the explicit computation showed it is the latter.

The cohomology ring

H∗(X;R) as a graded ring. Define H∗(X;R) to be the direct sum of the groups Hn(X;R); elements are finite sums Σiαi with αi ∈ Hi(X;R), multiplied by (Σiαi)(Σjβj) = Σi,jαiβj. This is a ring, with identity if R has one, and an R-algebra once scalar multiplication is taken into account. A graded ring is a ring A with a decomposition ⊕k≥0Ak into additive subgroups such that multiplication takes Ak × Aℓ into Ak+ℓ; for a ∈ Ak we write |a| = k and call it the dimension. — Hatcher §3.2, pp. 212–213

A graded ring satisfying ab = (−1)|a||b|ba is called simply commutative in algebraic topology, in spite of the potential for misunderstanding; older sources say graded commutative, anticommutative or skew commutative. Adding classes of different dimensions has little topological significance on its own — the point is the economy: H∗(X;R) often has a far more compact description than the list of groups Hn(X;R).

Two families of graded rings account for most of the examples one meets first.

  • Polynomial rings (Example 3.12). R[α] and its truncations R[α]/(αn). The instance already computed is H∗(ℝP2;ℤ2) ≅ ℤ2[α]/(α3) with |α| = 1. Hatcher notes a constraint worth internalising: a polynomial ring R[α] is strictly commutative, so for it to be commutative in the graded sense we need |α| even or 2 = 0 in R — which is exactly why the real projective spaces need ℤ2 coefficients while the complex ones do not.
  • Exterior algebras (Example 3.13). ΛR[α1,…,αn] is the free R-module with basis the products αi1⋯αik with i1 < ⋯ < ik, multiplication determined by αiαj = −αjαi for i ≠ j and αi² = 0, the empty product giving the identity. It is a commutative graded ring once the generators are declared odd-dimensional. The instance already in hand is the torus: H∗(T2;ℤ) ≅ Λℤ[α,β] with |α| = |β| = 1, by the g = 1 case of Example 3.7.

Worked example 3: telling two spaces apart

Example 3.14. The isomorphism H∗(⨿αXα;R) ≅ ∏αH∗(Xα;R), with coordinates induced by the inclusions, is a ring isomorphism for coordinatewise multiplication, because each iα∗ is a ring homomorphism by Proposition 3.10. The same holds for a wedge sum: H̃∗(⋁αXα;R) ≅ ∏αH̃∗(Xα;R), taking reduced cohomology to be cohomology relative to a basepoint and using relative cup products — assuming the basepoints are deformation retracts of neighbourhoods, so that the isomorphism does hold.

The application. ℂP2 is S2 with a cell e4 attached by a map f : S3 → S2. Homology cannot distinguish it from S2 ∨ S4, and neither can the additive structure of cohomology. Cup products can. In the wedge, the ring is a product ring, so a class in H2 has its square computed coordinatewise: in the S2 coordinate the square lands in H̃4(S2;ℤ) = 0, and in the S4 coordinate the class itself is zero. Every square in H2(S2 ∨ S4;ℤ) therefore vanishes. But H∗(ℂP2;ℤ) ≅ ℤ[α]/(α3), so the square of a generator of H2 is non-zero.

The conclusion, and why it is legitimate. A homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism of graded rings, by Proposition 3.10 applied to it and to its homotopy inverse. So ℂP2 is not homotopy equivalent to S2 ∨ S4 — and in particular the attaching map f is not homotopic to a constant, a fact no amount of homology could have delivered.

The mission strip's own example, S2 × S1 against S2 ∨ S1 ∨ S3, has exactly this shape, and half of it is available now: Example 3.14 shows every product of positive-dimensional classes in the wedge vanishes. The other half — that the product space has a non-zero product of a 1-dimensional with a 2-dimensional class — needs the cohomology of a product, which is Theorem 3.15, the Künneth formula, and that is at2-05.

Self-check 3. Hatcher notes that H∗(ℝPn;R) cannot be the polynomial ring R[α] with |α| = 1 for arbitrary R. What is the obstruction?

Graded commutativity forces α² = −α² for |α| odd, that is 2α² = 0. In a genuine polynomial ring R[α] the element α² is a basis element, so 2α² = 0 requires 2 = 0 in R. That is why the real projective spaces are computed with ℤ2 coefficients while ℂPn, whose generator has |α| = 2, is fine over ℤ. The third option confuses R[α]/(αn+1) with R[α]; truncation is a separate matter.

Self-check 4. Which fact makes the wedge-sum argument of Example 3.14 work?

The additive statement — cohomology of a wedge splits — is true but useless here, since ℂP2 and S2∨S4 have the same additive cohomology. What does the work is that the splitting is a ring isomorphism onto a product ring, in which a product of elements supported in different coordinates is zero and a square is computed within one coordinate. The third option is simply false: ℂP2 is simply connected and has a non-zero square.

Self-check 5. On the genus-2 surface, what is (α1 + β2) ⌣ (β1 + α2)?

Expand by distributivity into four terms: α1 ⌣ β1 = γ; α1 ⌣ α2 = 0; β2 ⌣ β1 = 0; and β2 ⌣ α2 = −(α2 ⌣ β2) = −γ. The total is γ − γ = 0. Answering 2γ is the characteristic slip: it treats the ring as ordinarily commutative, so that the two surviving terms add rather than cancel.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove that the cup product structure is a homotopy invariant: if f : X → Y is a homotopy equivalence then f∗ : H∗(Y;R) → H∗(X;R) is an isomorphism of graded rings. Then use this, with Example 3.14, to prove that ℝP2 is not homotopy equivalent to a wedge S1 ∨ S2, working with ℤ2 coefficients.

Hint: for the first part you need Proposition 3.10 twice and homotopy invariance of induced maps from at2-03. For the second, recall from Example 3.8 that the square of a generator of H1(ℝP2;ℤ2) is the non-zero element of H2(ℝP2;ℤ2).

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer, first part. Let g : Y → X be a homotopy inverse, so gf ≃ 𝟙X and fg ≃ 𝟙Y. By homotopy invariance of induced maps (at2-03), (gf)∗ = 𝟙∗ = 𝟙 on H∗(X;R) and (fg)∗ = 𝟙 on H∗(Y;R). By contravariance, (gf)∗ = f∗g∗ and (fg)∗ = g∗f∗. Hence f∗ and g∗ are mutually inverse additive isomorphisms. Each preserves dimension, and each is multiplicative by Proposition 3.10. A dimension-preserving multiplicative bijection with multiplicative inverse is an isomorphism of graded rings, so f∗ is one. ∎

Second part. Work with ℤ2 coefficients throughout. By Example 3.8, taking N to be the nonorientable surface of genus 1, which is ℝP2, the cup product of a generator α of H1(ℝP2;ℤ2) with itself is a generator of H2(ℝP2;ℤ2) ≅ ℤ2; in particular α² ≠ 0.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that ℝP2 ≃ S1 ∨ S2. By Example 3.14 the reduced cohomology ring of the wedge is the product ring H̃∗(S1;ℤ2) × H̃∗(S2;ℤ2) with coordinatewise multiplication. Let x be any element of H1(S1 ∨ S2;ℤ2). Its S2-coordinate lies in H̃1(S2;ℤ2) = 0, so x is carried by the S1 coordinate alone; and its square is computed in that coordinate, landing in H̃2(S1;ℤ2) = 0. So every element of H1 of the wedge squares to zero.

But by the first part a homotopy equivalence would give a graded ring isomorphism carrying α to some x ∈ H1 with x² = f∗(α)² = f∗(α²) ≠ 0, since f∗ is injective and α² ≠ 0. This contradicts the previous paragraph. Hence ℝP2 is not homotopy equivalent to S1 ∨ S2. ∎

What did the work. Additively the two spaces are indistinguishable with ℤ2 coefficients — both have ℤ2 in dimensions 0, 1, 2. The entire argument lives in the multiplication, and the only inputs were Proposition 3.10 (multiplicativity), homotopy invariance, and the product-ring structure of a wedge.
