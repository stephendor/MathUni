The Künneth formula and products of spaces (at2-05)

If you know the cohomology of X and of Y, what is the cohomology of X × Y? Usually the tensor product — usually.

You have a product of two spaces and you know each factor completely. The honest expectation is that nothing new can happen: the answer ought to be assembled from the parts. It very nearly is, and the assembly instructions are a tensor product with one sign in the multiplication.

The interesting part is the word "usually". Theorem 3.15 carries a freeness hypothesis, and Hatcher says outright that it cannot always be dispensed with. Knowing which hypothesis is load-bearing is the difference between a formula and a habit.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The cross product a × b = p1∗(a) ⌣ p2∗(b) is defined on H∗(X;R) × H∗(Y;R) and is linear in each variable separately. Predict: can such a map be an isomorphism of groups onto H∗(X × Y;R)?

Correct, and the failure is elementary rather than topological. A homomorphism out of A × B must send (a + a′, b + b′) to the sum of two values; a bilinear map sends it to a sum of four. Ordinary multiplication ℤ × ℤ → ℤ is bilinear, yet doubling the input quadruples the output. The fix is not to weaken the claim but to change the source: replace the direct product by the tensor product, whose entire purpose is to convert bilinear maps into homomorphisms.

Why this matters for the mission: Künneth computes the cohomology of a product (top-05's product topology) from its factors as a tensor product, with a Tor correction that vanishes over a field. It is how the topology of a product feature space decomposes into the topology of its parts.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cross product, tensor product, and the sign

Cross product. The cross product, or external cup product, is the map H∗(X;R) × H∗(Y;R) → H∗(X × Y;R) given by a × b = p1∗(a) ⌣ p2∗(b), where p1 and p2 are the projections of X × Y onto X and Y. Since cup product is distributive, the cross product is bilinear — linear in each variable separately. — Hatcher §3.2, p. 214

The prediction gate has already told you the difficulty. Hatcher's phrasing is exact: a bilinear map is rarely a homomorphism, so it could hardly be an isomorphism. The algebraic solution is to replace H∗(X;R) × H∗(Y;R) by the tensor product H∗(X;R) ⊗R H∗(Y;R).

Tensor product. For abelian groups A and B, A ⊗ B is the abelian group with generators a ⊗ b for a ∈ A, b ∈ B, and relations (a + a′) ⊗ b = a ⊗ b + a′ ⊗ b and a ⊗ (b + b′) = a ⊗ b + a ⊗ b′. Its zero element is 0 ⊗ 0 = 0 ⊗ b = a ⊗ 0, and n(a ⊗ b) = na ⊗ b = a ⊗ nb for all n ∈ ℤ. — Hatcher §3.2, p. 215

Seven properties do all the work, and the first five are the whole of the arithmetic:

  1. A ⊗ B ≅ B ⊗ A;
  2. (⊕iAi) ⊗ B ≅ ⊕i(Ai ⊗ B);
  3. (A ⊗ B) ⊗ C ≅ A ⊗ (B ⊗ C);
  4. ℤ ⊗ A ≅ A;
  5. ℤn ⊗ A ≅ A/nA;
  6. homomorphisms f : A → A′ and g : B → B′ induce f ⊗ g : A ⊗ B → A′ ⊗ B′ by (f ⊗ g)(a ⊗ b) = f(a) ⊗ g(b);
  7. a bilinear map φ : A × B → C induces a homomorphism A ⊗ B → C sending a ⊗ b to φ(a,b).

Properties (1), (2), (4) and (5) between them compute the tensor product of any two finitely generated abelian groups, and property (7) is precisely the fix for the prediction gate's problem. For modules over a commutative ring R one defines A ⊗R B as the quotient of A ⊗ B by the further relations ra ⊗ b = a ⊗ rb; this makes A ⊗R B an R-module, and (1)–(3), (6) and (7) survive, with (4) becoming R ⊗R A ≅ A. The two constructions agree when R is ℤ or ℤm, and also when R is ℚ or any subring of ℚ; they differ in general — for R = ℤ[√2] one has R ⊗R R = R of rank 2 while R ⊗ R is free of rank 4.

Property (7) now supplies the map we actually want:

H∗(X;R) ⊗R H∗(Y;R)  ×⟶  H∗(X × Y;R),   a ⊗ b ↦ a × b,

also called the cross product. For this to be a ring homomorphism the tensor product of graded rings must be given the right multiplication, and "right" here means one sign:

Multiplication in a graded tensor product. (a ⊗ b)(c ⊗ d) = (−1)|b||c| ac ⊗ bd, where |x| denotes the dimension of x. — Hatcher §3.2, p. 216

Worked example 1: why that sign and no other

Example. Write μ for the cross product map and verify that it is a ring homomorphism for the multiplication just defined.

Left-hand side. μ((a ⊗ b)(c ⊗ d)) = (−1)|b||c| μ(ac ⊗ bd) = (−1)|b||c| (a ⌣ c) × (b ⌣ d), by the definition of the multiplication and then of μ.

Unfold the cross product. That equals (−1)|b||c| p1∗(a ⌣ c) ⌣ p2∗(b ⌣ d), and since p1∗ and p2∗ are ring homomorphisms by Proposition 3.10 of at2-04, this is

(−1)|b||c| p1∗(a) ⌣ p1∗(c) ⌣ p2∗(b) ⌣ p2∗(d).

The one non-formal step. Commute p1∗(c) past p2∗(b). By Theorem 3.11 this costs (−1)|b||c|, which cancels the sign already present, leaving

p1∗(a) ⌣ p2∗(b) ⌣ p1∗(c) ⌣ p2∗(d) = (a × b)(c × d) = μ(a ⊗ b) μ(c ⊗ d).

Read the moral. The sign in the definition is not decoration: it is placed exactly so as to be consumed by the graded commutativity of cup product. Delete it and the two sides differ by (−1)|b||c| — equal only when |b||c| is even or 2 = 0 in R.

Worked example 2 (faded). Compute ℤ4 ⊗ ℤ6 and ℤm ⊗ ℤn, using only properties (1)–(5).

Apply (5). ℤ4 ⊗ ℤ6 ≅ ℤ6/4ℤ6. Now 4ℤ6 = {0, 4, 2} as a subset, which is the subgroup ⟨your step: name it⟩ of order ⟨your step⟩.

Quotient. ℤ6 has order 6, so the quotient has order 6/3 and ℤ4 ⊗ ℤ6 ≅ ℤ2.

Generalise. In ℤn the subgroup mℤn is generated by the residue of m, which generates the same subgroup as gcd(m,n); that subgroup has index gcd(m,n). Hence ℤm ⊗ ℤn ≅ ℤgcd(m,n). In particular the tensor product of two cyclic groups of coprime order is zero.

Gap solutions: 4ℤ6 = 2ℤ6, of order 3.

Self-check 1. Which property of the tensor product is doing the essential work in passing from the bilinear cross product to a homomorphism?

Property (7) is the universal property that defines what a tensor product is for: it converts bilinearity into linearity by moving the difficulty into the source object. Properties (2) and (4) are computational conveniences used later to evaluate the answer, not to construct the map.

Self-check 2. In the graded tensor product, why is the sign (−1)|b||c| — with b and c, rather than any other pair?

Line the four factors up as a, b, c, d and ask what it takes to reach the order a, c, b, d: only c and b cross. In the verification this appears as commuting p1∗(c) past p2∗(b), which Theorem 3.11 charges at (−1)|b||c|. The convention answer describes the formula correctly but explains nothing; the third option is simply false.

Theorem 3.15, and what it computes

Theorem 3.15 (Künneth formula). The cross product H∗(X;R) ⊗R H∗(Y;R) → H∗(X × Y;R) is an isomorphism of rings if X and Y are CW complexes and Hk(Y;R) is a finitely generated free R-module for all k. — Hatcher §3.2, Theorem 3.15, p. 216

Two remarks Hatcher attaches are worth as much as the statement. The CW hypothesis is removable — §4.1 does it, by CW approximation of arbitrary spaces. The freeness hypothesis is not: it cannot always be dispensed with, and §3.B obtains a completely general Künneth formula for the homology of a product, with a correction term for the non-free case. That correction is the Tor term named in this unit's mission strip; it lies outside §3.2 and so outside this lesson's source, and this lesson does not prove anything about it. What §3.2 does establish is the clean case, and the clean case covers every example below.

The proof strategy, for orientation: fix Y and consider the two functors hn(X,A) = ⊕i(Hi(X,A;R) ⊗R Hn−i(Y;R)) and kn(X,A) = Hn(X × Y, A × Y;R). A relative cross product defines μ : hn → kn, and the task is to show it is an isomorphism. Proposition 3.17 is the engine: a natural transformation between unreduced cohomology theories on CW pairs which is an isomorphism on the pair (point, ∅) is an isomorphism for all CW pairs. Theorem 3.18 then records the relative version, that H∗(X,A;R) ⊗R H∗(Y,B;R) → H∗(X × Y, A × Y ∪ X × B;R) is a ring isomorphism under the same freeness hypothesis on Hk(Y,B;R).

Visual: reading a Künneth answer off a grid

The additive content of the theorem is a grid. Put the ranks of Hi(X) along one axis and Hj(Y) along the other; the rank of Hn(X × Y) is the sum of the products along the anti-diagonal i + j = n. The button walks n upwards.

Worked example 3: products of spheres

Example 3.16. The exterior algebra ΛR[α1,…,αn] is the graded tensor product over R of the one-variable exterior algebras ΛR[αi] with the αi odd-dimensional. So Theorem 3.15 gives

H∗(Sk1 × ⋯ × Skn; ℤ) ≅ Λℤ[α1,…,αn]   when every ki is odd.

With some ki even, one gets the tensor product of an exterior algebra for the odd-dimensional spheres and truncated polynomial rings ℤ[α]/(α²) for the even-dimensional ones. As ungraded rings Λℤ[α] and ℤ[α]/(α²) are isomorphic; as graded rings they must be kept apart, α being odd-dimensional in the first and even-dimensional in the second — and the tensor product's sign reads exactly that dimension. Over ℤ2 the distinction evaporates, signs being irrelevant.

Instance: T3. All three circles are odd, so H∗(T3;ℤ) ≅ Λℤ[α1,α2,α3] with each |αi| = 1, of ranks 1, 3, 3, 1 in degrees 0 to 3.

Instance: S2 × S2. Both factors are even, giving ℤ[α]/(α²) ⊗ ℤ[β]/(β²) with |α| = |β| = 2, of ranks 1, 0, 2, 0, 1. Note that α² = β² = 0 but (α + β)² = 2αβ ≠ 0 — Theorem 3.11 said nothing here, the dimensions being even.

Worked example 4 (faded): the mission's pair. Compare S2 × S1 with S2 ∨ S1 ∨ S3.

The product. By Theorem 3.15, H∗(S2 × S1;ℤ) ≅ ℤ[β]/(β²) ⊗ Λℤ[α] with |β| = 2, |α| = 1 — the freeness hypothesis holding since each Hk(S1;ℤ) is free of finite rank. Additively this is ℤ in degrees 0, 1, 2, 3, spanned by 1, α, β and ⟨your step: the degree-3 basis element⟩. In particular the product of the 1-dimensional class with the 2-dimensional class is ⟨your step: zero or a generator?⟩.

The wedge. By Example 3.14 of at2-04, the reduced cohomology ring of a wedge is a product ring with coordinatewise multiplication. Each sphere factor has non-zero reduced cohomology in one degree only, so a product of two positive-dimensional classes lands either in a degree where the relevant factor vanishes or in a coordinate where one of the factors was already zero. Every such product is therefore ⟨your step⟩. Additively the ring is again ℤ in degrees 0, 1, 2, 3.

The conclusion. Same groups in every degree, different rings. Since a homotopy equivalence induces a graded ring isomorphism (at2-04), the two spaces are not homotopy equivalent.

Gap solutions: the degree-3 basis element is αβ; the product α ⌣ β = αβ is a generator of H3, hence non-zero; and every product of positive-dimensional classes in the wedge is zero.

Lemma K (reading homology back from free cohomology). Let C be a chain complex of free abelian groups with all Hn(C) finitely generated, and suppose Hn(C;ℤ) is free of finite rank rn for every n, with Hn(C;ℤ) = 0 for n greater than some N. Then every Hn(C) is free of rank rn.

Proof. Corollary 3.3 of at2-02 gives Hn(C;ℤ) ≅ (Hn/Tn) ⊕ Tn−1, where Tn is the torsion subgroup of Hn. Work downwards from the top. For n > N both sides vanish, so Tn = 0 there. Suppose Tn is not known yet but Tn+1 = 0 has been established; the group Hn+1(C;ℤ) ≅ (Hn+1/Tn+1) ⊕ Tn is free by hypothesis, and a direct summand of a free abelian group is torsion-free, so the finite group Tn must be zero. By downward induction Tn = 0 for every n, and then Hn(C;ℤ) ≅ Hn/Tn = Hn has rank rn. ∎

This is the step that lets a Künneth computation settle the homology as well: both spaces of worked example 4 have free cohomology concentrated in degrees 0 to 3, so both have Hn ≅ ℤ there and zero elsewhere — identical homology, different rings. The hypothesis that matters is finite generation; drop it and Corollary 3.3 does not apply at all.

Self-check 3. Theorem 3.15 requires Hk(Y;R) to be finitely generated and free for all k. Which of these is the correct status of that hypothesis?

Hatcher separates the two hypotheses explicitly. The CW hypothesis is removable by CW approximation in §4.1. The freeness hypothesis is not: §3.B derives a completely general Künneth formula precisely because the naive statement fails without it. The third option inverts the situation — it is the additive statement that acquires the correction term.

Self-check 4. Using the grid picture, what is the rank of H2(S2 × S2;ℤ)?

The ranks of H∗(S2;ℤ) are 1, 0, 1 in degrees 0, 1, 2. On the anti-diagonal i + j = 2 the contributing cells are (2,0) with 1 × 1 = 1 and (0,2) with 1 × 1 = 1; the cell (1,1) contributes 0 × 0 = 0. Total 2, spanned by α and β. The last option computes the total rank of the whole ring, which is 4 and is spread over degrees 0, 2 and 4.

Self-check 5. Why must Λℤ[α] and ℤ[α]/(α²) be distinguished as graded rings even though they are isomorphic as rings?

Both are the free module R ⊕ Rα with α² = 0, so as ungraded rings they are the same object. The difference is the declared dimension of α — odd in the exterior algebra, even in the truncated polynomial ring — and the tensor product multiplication consults exactly that. Over ℤ2, where signs vanish, the distinction genuinely stops mattering, which is a good check that it is the sign and nothing else at stake.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove that ℤm ⊗ ℤn ≅ ℤgcd(m,n) using only properties (1)–(5), and deduce that the tensor product of two finite cyclic groups of coprime order is zero. Then use the result to explain, in two or three sentences, why a Künneth-style answer over ℤ can lose information that the same computation over a field would keep.

Hint: property (5) turns the question into identifying the subgroup mℤn of ℤn.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. By property (5), ℤm ⊗ ℤn ≅ ℤn/mℤn. So it suffices to identify mℤn, the subgroup of ℤn generated by the residue of m. Write d = gcd(m,n). Since d divides m, every multiple of m in ℤn is a multiple of d, so mℤn ⊆ dℤn. Conversely, by Bézout there are integers s, t with d = sm + tn, so modulo n we have d ≡ sm, putting d ∈ mℤn and hence dℤn ⊆ mℤn. Therefore mℤn = dℤn, which is the unique subgroup of ℤn of order n/d, and so

ℤm ⊗ ℤn ≅ ℤn/dℤn ≅ ℤd = ℤgcd(m,n). ∎

If gcd(m,n) = 1 then d = 1 and the quotient is trivial, so the tensor product is the zero group.

The consequence for computation. Tensoring can annihilate torsion outright: two non-zero groups can have zero tensor product, so a Künneth-type answer over ℤ may show nothing in a degree where each factor separately had something. Over a field every module is free, all the groups in sight are vector spaces, and the tensor product of non-zero vector spaces is never zero — the dimensions simply multiply, degree by degree along the anti-diagonal. That is the same reason at2-02 gave for Ext vanishing over a field, and it is why a persistence computation fixes a coefficient field before anything else.
