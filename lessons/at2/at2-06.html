Orientation and the fundamental class (at2-06)

Can you pick a consistent positive direction at every point of a surface at once? On the Klein bottle you cannot — and homology says so.

Walk an anticlockwise arrow once round a Möbius band and it comes back clockwise. Nothing went wrong locally: at every instant the arrow turned smoothly and continuously. The obstruction is global, and it is the kind of obstruction that algebraic topology exists to name.

Hatcher's move is to refuse any geometric definition of "orientation" and take an algebraic one instead: an orientation at a point is a choice of generator of a local homology group. That sounds like a retreat from geometry, and it buys three things at once — a definition that works on every manifold, a covering space that measures the failure, and a single homology class, the fundamental class, that exists exactly when the choices can be made consistently.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Suppose M is a closed connected n-manifold that is not orientable. Predict what happens to the top homology group Hn(M;ℤ2).

Correct. Theorem 3.26 says the map Hn(M;R) → Hn(M|x;R) ≅ R is injective with image {r ∈ R : 2r = 0} in the non-orientable case — the 2-torsion of R. Over ℤ that is zero; over ℤ2 it is everything. The reason is that an orientation is a coherent choice of sign, and in ℤ2 there is no sign to be inconsistent about: every manifold is ℤ2-orientable. This is why ℤ2 coefficients are the standard refuge in manifold topology, and why a duality statement over a field of characteristic 2 needs no orientability hypothesis at all.

Why this matters for the mission: For a closed connected n-manifold M, H_n(M;Z) is Z when M is orientable and 0 otherwise, and a generator is the fundamental class [M]. Capping with [M] is the map that implements duality in at2-07.

Local orientations and the orientation covering space

Start with the local question: what is an orientation of ℝn? Whatever it is, it should be preserved by rotations and reversed by reflections — clockwise and anticlockwise in ℝ2, right- and left-handed in ℝ3, both have this property. Hatcher's viewpoint is that this property is what characterises orientations, so anything with it may be taken as one.

Local orientation. An orientation of ℝn at a point x is a choice of generator of the infinite cyclic group Hn(ℝn, ℝn − {x}). More generally, a local orientation of an n-manifold M at x is a choice of generator μx of Hn(M, M − {x}). — Hatcher §3.3, pp. 233–234

Why this has the characteristic property. Use the natural isomorphisms Hn(ℝn, ℝn − {x}) ≅ Hn−1(ℝn − {x}) ≅ Hn−1(Sn−1), for Sn−1 a sphere centred at x. A self-map of ℝn fixing x acts on all three groups compatibly, and on Hn−1(Sn−1) it acts by its degree. Rotations are homotopic to the identity and so have degree 1, fixing a generator α; reflections have degree −1 and send α to −α. Exactly what was wanted.

Notational convention. Groups of the form Hn(X, X − A) will appear constantly, so write them Hn(X|A), or Hn(X|A;G) when the coefficients matter. By excision Hn(X|A) depends only on a neighbourhood of the closure of A, which is why it makes sense to call it the local homology of X at A.

Orientation. An orientation of an n-manifold M is a function x ↦ μx assigning to each x a local orientation μx ∈ Hn(M|x), satisfying local consistency: each x has a neighbourhood ℝn ⊂ M containing an open ball B of finite radius about x such that all the μy for y ∈ B are images of one generator μB of Hn(M|B) under the natural maps Hn(M|B) → Hn(M|y). If an orientation exists, M is orientable. — Hatcher §3.3, p. 234

Note what the definition does not say. It does not ask for a single global gadget; it asks for a pointwise choice plus a local compatibility. The whole difficulty of orientability is that local compatibility need not add up to global consistency, and the object that measures the gap is a covering space.

The orientation double cover

As a set, let M̃ = {μx : x ∈ M and μx is a local orientation of M at x}. The map μx ↦ x is two-to-one, since Hn(M|x) ≅ ℤ has exactly two generators. Topologise M̃ by the basis of sets U(μB), consisting of those μx with x ∈ B whose value is the image of a chosen generator μB ∈ Hn(M|B); these make the projection a covering space. And M̃ is itself orientable: each μx ∈ M̃ carries the canonical local orientation corresponding to μx under Hn(M̃|μx) ≅ Hn(U(μB)|μx) ≅ Hn(B|x), and these satisfy local consistency by construction.

Proposition 3.25. If M is connected, then M is orientable iff M̃ has two components. In particular, M is orientable if it is simply connected, or more generally if π1(M) has no subgroup of index two. — Hatcher §3.3, Proposition 3.25, p. 234

Proof. M̃ is a two-sheeted cover of a connected M, so it has one or two components. If two, each maps homeomorphically to M, so M is homeomorphic to a component of the orientable manifold M̃ and is orientable. Conversely, if M is orientable then, being connected, it has exactly two orientations, and each defines a component of M̃. The last statement follows because connected two-sheeted covers of M correspond to index-two subgroups of π1(M). ∎

The first sentence formalises the Möbius intuition exactly: going round a loop and returning with the opposite orientation corresponds to a loop in M lifting to a path in M̃ joining two distinct points over the same base point, and such paths exist precisely when M̃ is connected.

R-orientations: which coefficient rings can see the obstruction

Enlarge M̃ to the covering space Mℤ → M whose points are all elements αx ∈ Hn(M|x), topologised by the analogous basis. It is infinite-sheeted. Setting αx = 0 gives a copy M0 of M inside it, and the rest is a sequence of copies Mk of M̃, where Mk consists of the αx that are k times either generator. A continuous map x ↦ αx is a section, and an orientation is exactly a section whose value at each x is a generator.

R-orientation. For a commutative ring R with identity, an R-orientation assigns to each x ∈ M a generator of Hn(M|x;R) ≅ R, subject to the same local consistency condition — a generator meaning an element u with Ru = R, equivalently a unit. It is a section of the covering space MR → M taking generator values. — Hatcher §3.3, p. 235

The structure of MR settles which rings work. Using Hn(M|x;R) ≅ Hn(M|x) ⊗ R, each r ∈ R gives a subcovering Mr consisting of the points ±μx ⊗ r. If r has order 2 in R then r = −r, the two signs coincide, and Mr is a copy of M; otherwise Mr ≅ M̃. So:

Which manifolds are R-orientable. An orientable manifold is R-orientable for every R. A nonorientable manifold is R-orientable iff R contains a unit of order 2, which is equivalent to 2 = 0 in R. In particular every manifold is ℤ2-orientable, so the two cases to keep in mind are R = ℤ and R = ℤ2. — Hatcher §3.3, p. 235

Read "order 2" additively. Hatcher's phrase is the one above, and the sentence before it fixes the sense: if r has order 2 in R then r = −r. So the condition on the unit u is 2u = 0, equivalently u = −u — its order in the additive group (R, +). It is emphatically not the multiplicative condition u² = 1, which is what "order 2" suggests to most readers and which gives the wrong answer: in ℤ3 the unit 2 satisfies 2² = 4 = 1, so the multiplicative reading would make every manifold ℤ3-orientable, while 2 ≠ 0 in ℤ3 and the Klein bottle is not. Nothing here requires u² = 1, and nothing implies it: in 𝔽4 every non-zero element satisfies 2u = 0, but only u = 1 squares to 1.

Self-check 1. Proposition 3.25 says M is orientable if π1(M) has no subgroup of index two. What does it tell you about ℝP2, whose fundamental group is ℤ2?

The trivial subgroup of ℤ2 has index two, so the hypothesis is not met and the proposition is silent. A sufficient condition that fails proves nothing either way — the third option commits exactly that error. ℝP2 is in fact nonorientable, but that has to be established otherwise, for instance by exhibiting the orientation cover S2 → ℝP2 as connected.

Self-check 2. Why is a nonorientable M still R-orientable when 2 = 0 in R?

Non-orientability is the impossibility of choosing signs consistently. When 2 = 0 the two candidate generators ±μx ⊗ 1 are literally the same element, so the subcovering M1 is a copy of M rather than the two-sheeted M̃, and the section exists. The third option is false — Hn(M|x;R) ≅ R is non-zero — and the second is not a statement about rings at all.

Top homology and the fundamental class

Theorem 3.26. Let M be a closed connected n-manifold. Then:

(a) if M is R-orientable, the map Hn(M;R) → Hn(M|x;R) ≅ R is an isomorphism for all x ∈ M;
(b) if M is not R-orientable, that map is injective with image {r ∈ R : 2r = 0} for all x ∈ M;
(c) Hi(M;R) = 0 for i > n.

In particular Hn(M;ℤ) is ℤ or 0 according as M is orientable or not, and in either case Hn(M;ℤ2) = ℤ2. — Hatcher §3.3, Theorem 3.26, p. 236

Fundamental class. An element of Hn(M;R) whose image in Hn(M|x;R) is a generator for every x is a fundamental class for M with coefficients in R, also called an orientation class. By Theorem 3.26 one exists whenever M is closed and R-orientable. — Hatcher §3.3, p. 236

The converse, which is where the compactness comes from. Let μ ∈ Hn(M;R) be a fundamental class and μx its image in Hn(M|x;R). Then x ↦ μx is an R-orientation, because the map Hn(M;R) → Hn(M|x;R) factors through Hn(M|B;R) for any open ball B containing x — and that factoring is the local consistency condition. Moreover M must be compact, since μx can be non-zero only for x in the image of a cycle representing μ, and that image is compact. So a fundamental class exists exactly when M is closed and R-orientable. ∎

Step 3 — one dimension down. Corollary 3.28 records what the theorem still yields about Hn−1: for a closed connected n-manifold, the torsion subgroup of Hn−1(M;ℤ) is trivial if M is orientable and ℤ2 if M is nonorientable. Hatcher's proof is an application of the universal coefficient theorem for homology, using that the homology groups of M are finitely generated: in the orientable case, torsion in Hn−1(M;ℤ) would make Hn(M;ℤp) larger than the ℤp coming from Hn(M;ℤ) for some prime p; in the nonorientable case Hn(M;ℤm) is ℤ2 or 0 according as m is even or odd, which forces the torsion subgroup to be ℤ2.

Read the table. The whole of orientability is a single bit, and only coefficient rings in which 2 fails to be invertible can see it. Over ℚ or ℤ3 a nonorientable closed manifold looks, in top degree, exactly like a noncompact one.

Worked example 2 (faded): a simplicial fundamental class. Let M be a closed n-manifold with a Δ-complex structure. Construct a fundamental class with ℤ coefficients, and see where it fails.

The shape of the answer. A simplicial fundamental class must be a linear combination Σikiσi of the n-simplices of M.

Constraint one. For the class to map to a generator of Hn(M|x;ℤ) at points x interior to σi, each coefficient ki must be ⟨your step: which values?⟩.

Constraint two. The combination must be a cycle. If σi and σj share an (n−1)-face, then ki determines kj and conversely, so the sign choice ⟨your step: propagates how?⟩ across the complex.

When it succeeds. Analysing more closely, a choice of signs making Σikiσi a cycle is possible iff M is orientable, and when possible the resulting cycle defines a fundamental class. With ℤ2 coefficients there is no issue of signs at all, and Σiσi always defines a fundamental class — the concrete face of "every manifold is ℤ2-orientable".

Gap solutions: each ki must be ±1; and the sign propagates across every shared (n−1)-face, so that one choice determines all the others along any chain of adjacent simplices — the obstruction being that a loop of adjacencies may demand both signs at once.

Where the theorem comes from

Theorem 3.26 follows from a more technical statement about compact subsets, and the technical statement is where the actual work lives.

Lemma 3.27. Let M be a manifold of dimension n and A ⊂ M compact. Then: (a) if x ↦ αx is a section of the covering space MR → M, there is a unique class αA ∈ Hn(M|A;R) whose image in Hn(M|x;R) is αx for all x ∈ A; (b) Hi(M|A;R) = 0 for i > n. — Hatcher §3.3, Lemma 3.27, p. 236

How the theorem follows. Take A = M, compact by assumption. Part (b) gives Theorem 3.26(c) at once. For the rest, let ΓR(M) be the set of sections of MR → M; sums and scalar multiples of sections are sections, so ΓR(M) is an R-module, and there is a homomorphism Hn(M;R) → ΓR(M) sending α to x ↦ αx. Part (a) of the lemma says precisely that this homomorphism is an isomorphism. When M is connected each section is determined by its value at a single point, so (a) and (b) of the theorem drop out of the description of MR given above. ∎

Hatcher's proof of the lemma runs in four steps: (1) if it holds for compact A, B and A ∩ B then it holds for A ∪ B, by a Mayer–Vietoris argument; (2) an arbitrary compact A is a finite union of compact sets each inside a coordinate chart, so (1) reduces to A ⊂ ℝn ⊂ M; (3) inside ℝn the same induction reduces to A convex, where the result is immediate by excision because both ℝn − A and ℝn − {x} deformation retract onto a sphere about x; (4) a limiting argument covers an arbitrary compact A by finitely many small closed balls. Step (1) is the one worth doing yourself, and it is the guided proof below.

Self-check 3. M is a closed connected n-manifold with Hn(M;ℤ) = 0. What can you conclude?

By Theorem 3.26 a closed connected R-orientable M has Hn(M;R) ≅ R, so Hn(M;ℤ) = 0 rules out ℤ-orientability. Corollary 3.28 then pins the torsion of Hn−1(M;ℤ) as exactly ℤ2. "Closed" already means compact, so the first option contradicts the hypothesis.

Self-check 4. Which statement about fundamental classes is exactly right?

Theorem 3.26 supplies one when M is closed and R-orientable; the converse argument shows a fundamental class forces both R-orientability (via local consistency) and compactness (the representing cycle has compact image). The first option drops compactness — a noncompact manifold has no fundamental class over any R. The third confuses the definition: a fundamental class is defined by its local images being generators, and it is that condition, not generating the top group, that makes the definition work.

Self-check 5. In Lemma 3.27 step (3), why is the convex case immediate?

The point is not that A is contractible — that alone says nothing about the relative groups — but that the complements ℝn − A and ℝn − {x} have the same homotopy type, both retracting onto a sphere centred at x. That makes the comparison map an isomorphism, which is both halves of the lemma at once for convex A.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove step (1) of Lemma 3.27: if the lemma holds for compact sets A, B and A ∩ B, then it holds for A ∪ B. Write out the Mayer–Vietoris sequence you use, justify the zero at its left-hand end, and prove both parts — the vanishing (b) for A ∪ B, and existence and uniqueness in (a).

Hint: the relevant sequence is 0 → Hn(M|A ∪ B) → Hn(M|A) ⊕ Hn(M|B) → Hn(M|A ∩ B), with Φ(α) = (α, −α) and Ψ(α, β) = α + β.

Model answer. Write the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for the local homology groups,

0 → Hn(M|A ∪ B)  Φ⟶  Hn(M|A) ⊕ Hn(M|B)  Ψ⟶  Hn(M|A ∩ B),

with Φ(α) = (α, −α) and Ψ(α, β) = α + β, all maps induced by inclusions. The zero at the left is the term Hn+1(M|A ∩ B), which vanishes by the hypothesis applied to A ∩ B.

Part (b). For i > n the term Hi(M|A ∪ B) sits in the sequence between Hi+1(M|A ∩ B) and Hi(M|A) ⊕ Hi(M|B), both of which vanish by hypothesis. Exactness forces Hi(M|A ∪ B) = 0.

Part (a), existence. Let x ↦ αx be a section. The hypothesis supplies unique classes αA, αB and αA∩B with the right local images. The image of αA in Hn(M|A ∩ B) has image αx at every x ∈ A ∩ B, so by the uniqueness clause it equals αA∩B; likewise for αB. Hence Ψ(αA, −αB) = αA∩B − αA∩B = 0, so (αA, −αB) ∈ Ker Ψ = Im Φ and there is αA∪B with Φ(αA∪B) = (αA, −αB). This αA∪B maps to αA and to αB, hence has image αx for every x ∈ A ∪ B.

Part (a), uniqueness. Suppose α ∈ Hn(M|A ∪ B) has image zero in Hn(M|x) for every x ∈ A ∪ B. Then its images in Hn(M|A) and Hn(M|B) have that property too, so by the uniqueness clause of the hypothesis — applied to the zero section — both are zero. Thus Φ(α) = 0, and Φ is injective, so α = 0. Applying this to the difference of two candidate classes for αA∪B gives uniqueness. ∎

What the step actually buys. It converts the lemma into a statement that can be proved by induction over a finite cover, which is what steps (2) and (3) then exploit: any compact A is a finite union of pieces inside coordinate charts, and inside a chart a finite union of convex sets. The Mayer–Vietoris step is the only place the two halves of the lemma interact — the vanishing (b) for A ∩ B is exactly what supplies the injectivity that uniqueness in (a) needs.
