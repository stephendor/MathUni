The Universal Coefficient Theorem (at2-02)

Cohomology is not quite the dual of homology — there is a correction term, and it is exactly the torsion you met in aa-20.

At the end of at2-01 we had a map h : Hn(C;G) → Hom(Hn(C), G), knew it was surjective and split, and had one uncomfortable example: the little complex 0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ → 0 with maps 0, 2, 0, where H2(C) = 0 and yet H2(C;ℤ) = ℤ2. The target of h was the zero group; the source was not. Something is unaccounted for.

That something has a name, a construction, and — the point of this lesson — a completely computable answer for finitely generated groups. It also has a habit of disappearing the moment you work over a field, which is why nobody computing persistence over 𝔽p ever mentions it.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. In that complex, H1(C) = ℤ2, H2(C) = 0, and H2(C;ℤ) = ℤ2. Predict where the ℤ2 in H2 comes from.

Correct. H2(C) really is zero, and ℤ really is torsion-free; the only ℤ2 anywhere in sight is the torsion of H1(C), one dimension below. The Universal Coefficient Theorem makes this precise: Hn(C;G) is assembled from Hom(Hn(C),G) — the same degree — together with a group Ext(Hn−1(C),G) built from the degree below. Everything in this lesson is a way of constructing that second group and proving it is the only correction needed.

Why this matters for the mission: The UCT gives a split short exact sequence 0 → Ext(Hn−1(X), G) → Hn(X;G) → Hom(Hn(X), G) → 0. Over a field Ext vanishes and Hn is simply the dual of Hn — the reason TDA over 𝔽p never has to reason about Ext.

Free resolutions, and why Ext is well defined

Recall the obstruction from at2-01. We had the short exact sequence 0 → Bn−1 → Zn−1 → Hn−1(C) → 0, and the group we could not identify was Coker of its dual. Hatcher's observation is that this sequence has a special feature: both Bn−1 and Zn−1 are free. That makes it an instance of a general construction.

Free resolution. A free resolution of an abelian group H is an exact sequence ⋯ → F2 → F1 → F0 → H → 0 with each Fn free, the maps being written f2, f1, f0. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 193

Dualising a free resolution by Hom(−, G) may lose exactness — that is the whole point — but it always leaves a complex ⋯ ← F2∗ ← F1∗ ← F0∗ ← H∗ ← 0. Write Hn(F;G) for the homology group Ker fn+1∗ / Im fn∗ of that dual complex.

Every abelian group has an easy resolution, obtained in two steps. Choose generators for H and let F0 be free on a basis matching them, giving a surjection f0 : F0 → H. The kernel of f0 is a subgroup of a free abelian group, hence itself free, so we may take F1 = Ker f0 with f1 the inclusion, and Fi = 0 for i > 1. For this resolution Hn(F;G) = 0 whenever n > 1, simply because the complex is zero there.

Which raises the obvious worry. Different generating sets give different resolutions. If H1(F;G) depended on the resolution, it would be no invariant of H at all. It does not, and the reason is the following lemma — the technical heart of the section.

Lemma 3.1. (a) Given free resolutions F and F′ of abelian groups H and H′, every homomorphism α : H → H′ can be extended to a chain map from F to F′; furthermore, any two such chain maps extending α are chain homotopic. (b) For any two free resolutions F and F′ of H, there are canonical isomorphisms Hn(F;G) ≅ Hn(F′;G) for all n. — Hatcher §3.1, Lemma 3.1, p. 194

Proof of (a), existence. The maps αi : Fi → Fi′ are constructed inductively, and since each Fi is free it suffices to define αi on a basis. For α0: given a basis element x of F0, surjectivity of f0′ provides x′ ∈ F0′ with f0′(x′) = αf0(x); set α0(x) = x′. For the inductive step we want αi(x) = x′ with fi′(x′) = αi−1fi(x), and such an x′ exists provided αi−1fi(x) lies in Im fi′ = Ker fi−1′. It does: fi−1′αi−1fi = αi−2fi−1fi = 0, using the previously built square and exactness of F. ∎

Proof of (a), uniqueness up to chain homotopy. If αi′ is another such chain map, the differences βi = αi − αi′ form a chain map extending the zero map H → H′, so it suffices to build λi : Fi → Fi+1′ with βi = fi+1′λi + λi−1fi. Take λ−1 = 0; then the required relation at i = 0 is β0 = f1′λ0, achievable on a basis because f0′β0(x) = βf0(x) = 0 puts β0(x) in Ker f0′ = Im f1′. Inductively one needs βi(x) − λi−1fi(x) ∈ Im fi+1′ = Ker fi′, and indeed fi′(βi − λi−1fi) = βi−1fi − fi′λi−1fi = (βi−1 − fi′λi−1)fi = λi−2fi−1fi = 0. ∎

How (b) follows. Dualising the αi gives a chain map between the dual complexes, hence induced maps α∗ : Hn(F′;G) → Hn(F;G), independent of the choices because chain-homotopic maps dualise to chain-homotopic maps. Taking α to be an isomorphism and β its inverse gives α∗β∗ = (βα)∗ = 𝟙 and likewise the other way, so α∗ is an isomorphism. Specialising to α = 𝟙 with two different resolutions of the same H produces the canonical isomorphism. ∎

Now the definition can be made without apology.

Ext. Since every H has a free resolution with Fi = 0 for i > 1, and by Lemma 3.1(b) the groups Hn(F;G) do not depend on the resolution, Hn(F;G) = 0 for all n > 1 and every free resolution. The only interesting group is H1(F;G), which depends only on H and G; its standard notation is Ext(H, G). — Hatcher §3.1, p. 195

The name comes from an interpretation of Ext(H,G) as the isomorphism classes of extensions of G by H, that is, short exact sequences 0 → G → J → H → 0. Hatcher records the fact and then says plainly that this interpretation is rarely needed in algebraic topology; we shall not use it either.

Worked example 1: Ext of a cyclic group

Example. Compute Ext(ℤn, G) for an arbitrary abelian group G.

Step 1 — resolve. ℤn is generated by one element, so take F0 = ℤ with f0 the quotient map ℤ → ℤn. Its kernel is nℤ ≅ ℤ, so F1 = ℤ with f1 multiplication by n:

0 ⟶ ℤ  n⟶  ℤ ⟶ ℤn ⟶ 0.

Step 2 — dualise. Apply Hom(−, G) and delete the H∗ term, as the definition of Hn(F;G) requires. Since Hom(ℤ, G) ≅ G and the dual of multiplication by n is again multiplication by n, the dual complex is

0 ⟶ G  n⟶  G ⟶ 0,   with G in degrees 0 and 1.

Step 3 — take homology in degree 1. There is nothing in degree 2, so the kernel is all of G; the image of the incoming map is nG. Hence

Ext(ℤn, G) = G / nG.

Step 4 — sanity check in degree 0. H0(F;G) = Ker(multiplication by n on G) = {g : ng = 0}, which is Hom(ℤn, G): a homomorphism out of ℤn is exactly a choice of element killed by n. The bookkeeping is consistent.

Worked example 2 (faded). Compute Ext(ℤ4, ℤ6).

Apply the formula. By worked example 1, Ext(ℤ4, ℤ6) = ℤ6 / 4ℤ6.

Identify the subgroup. 4ℤ6 = {0, 4, 8 mod 6, …} = ⟨your step: list the elements, then name the subgroup⟩.

Take the quotient. ℤ6 has order 6 and the subgroup has order ⟨your step⟩, so the quotient is ⟨your step: which group?⟩.

Moral. The answer is neither ℤ4 nor ℤ6. Ext(H,G) is "the torsion of H" only in the special case G = ℤ; with torsion in the coefficient group as well, the two interact.

Gap solutions: 4ℤ6 = {0, 4, 2} = 2ℤ6, of order 3; the quotient has order 2, so Ext(ℤ4, ℤ6) ≅ ℤ2.

Self-check 1. Why does Lemma 3.1(b) matter for the definition of Ext, as opposed to being a technical curiosity?

Constructing a resolution involves choosing generators, and different choices give genuinely different complexes. Without Lemma 3.1(b) the symbol Ext(H,G) would name a different group for each choice. Existence of resolutions is the easy separate paragraph before the lemma; vanishing for free H is a consequence afterwards.

Self-check 2. Where in the proof of Lemma 3.1(a) is freeness of the Fi used, and where is exactness of F′ used?

The two hypotheses do different jobs and it is worth keeping them apart. Freeness of the source is a licence to define: a homomorphism out of a free abelian group may be prescribed however we like on a basis. Exactness of the target resolution is a guarantee of supply: the element we must hit, αi−1fi(x), is shown to lie in Ker fi−1′, which exactness identifies with Im fi′ — so a preimage is available to choose.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The theorem, and how to compute with it

Everything is now in place. In at2-01 we found a split short exact sequence with an unidentified kernel; Coker of the dual of Bn−1 → Zn−1 is by definition H1(F;G) for the free resolution 0 → Bn−1 → Zn−1 → Hn−1(C) → 0, and that is Ext(Hn−1(C), G).

Theorem 3.2 (universal coefficient theorem for cohomology). If a chain complex C of free abelian groups has homology groups Hn(C), then the cohomology groups Hn(C;G) of the cochain complex Hom(Cn, G) are determined by split exact sequences

0 ⟶ Ext(Hn−1(C), G) ⟶ Hn(C;G)  h⟶  Hom(Hn(C), G) ⟶ 0.

— Hatcher §3.1, Theorem 3.2, p. 195

It is called universal because it expresses cohomology with any coefficient group in terms of homology with ℤ coefficients — one computation of H∗(C) serves for every G at once.

Visual: what goes where

The picture below is the whole theorem in one diagram. Each cohomology group in the bottom row receives the free part of the homology group directly above it, and the torsion of the homology group one place to its left.

Three facts make Ext computable for finitely generated groups, and they are all the arithmetic you will ever need:

Computing Ext. Ext(H ⊕ H′, G) ≅ Ext(H,G) ⊕ Ext(H′,G); Ext(H,G) = 0 if H is free; and Ext(ℤn, G) ≅ G/nG. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 195

The first comes from using the direct sum of free resolutions of H and H′ as a resolution of H ⊕ H′ — you will prove this in the guided proof below. The second follows from the resolution 0 → H → H → 0 available when H is free. The third is worked example 1.

Combining these with the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups (aa-20) gives the two facts that turn the theorem into a recipe: for finitely generated H, Ext(H, ℤ) is isomorphic to the torsion subgroup of H, and Hom(H, ℤ) is isomorphic to the free part of H. Hence:

Corollary 3.3. If the homology groups Hn and Hn−1 of a chain complex C of free abelian groups are finitely generated, with torsion subgroups Tn ⊂ Hn and Tn−1 ⊂ Hn−1, then Hn(C;ℤ) ≅ (Hn/Tn) ⊕ Tn−1. — Hatcher §3.1, Corollary 3.3, p. 196

Worked example 3: running the recipe

Example. Let C be a chain complex of free abelian groups with H0 = ℤ, H1 = ℤ2, H2 = ℤ ⊕ ℤ3, and Hn = 0 for n ≥ 3. Compute Hn(C;ℤ) for all n.

Step 1 — split each homology group. Free parts: H0/T0 = ℤ, H1/T1 = 0, H2/T2 = ℤ. Torsion: T0 = 0, T1 = ℤ2, T2 = ℤ3.

Step 2 — apply Corollary 3.3 degree by degree.

H0 ≅ ℤ ⊕ 0 = ℤ;   H1 ≅ 0 ⊕ T0 = 0;   H2 ≅ ℤ ⊕ T1 = ℤ ⊕ ℤ2;   H3 ≅ 0 ⊕ T2 = ℤ3.

Step 3 — check against Theorem 3.2 in one degree. For n = 2 the sequence reads 0 → Ext(H1, ℤ) → H2 → Hom(H2, ℤ) → 0, that is 0 → ℤ2 → H2 → ℤ → 0. It splits, so H2 ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ2, agreeing with step 2. Note that Hom(ℤ ⊕ ℤ3, ℤ) = ℤ, the ℤ3 contributing nothing to Hom.

Read the pattern. Every torsion summand appears in cohomology one dimension higher than it appeared in homology, and every free summand stays put. The complex has non-zero H3 even though H3 = 0 — the same phenomenon as the hook, one dimension along.

Worked example 4 (faded). The same complex, now with G = ℚ.

The Ext terms. Ext(ℤm, ℚ) = ℚ/mℚ, and multiplication by a non-zero integer m is ⟨your step: what kind of map on ℚ?⟩, so mℚ = ℚ and every Ext term is ⟨your step⟩.

The Hom terms. Hom(ℤm, ℚ) = {q ∈ ℚ : mq = 0} = 0, so only the free parts survive: Hom(H0, ℚ) = ℚ, Hom(H1, ℚ) = 0, Hom(H2, ℚ) = ℚ.

Conclusion. H0(C;ℚ) = ℚ, H1(C;ℚ) = 0, H2(C;ℚ) = ℚ, H3(C;ℚ) = 0. The torsion has vanished without trace, and what remains is exactly the sequence of ranks.

Gap solutions: multiplication by m is an isomorphism of ℚ, so mℚ = ℚ and every Ext term is 0.

Naturality, and one thing that is not natural

The sequences of Theorem 3.2 are natural: a chain map α between chain complexes C and C′ of free abelian groups induces a commutative diagram between the two sequences, with the vertical maps (α∗)∗ on the outer terms and α∗ in the middle. The splitting, however, is not natural — it depends on the choice of the projections p : Cn → Zn from at2-01, and a chain map need not respect such a choice on both sides. Hatcher notes that an exercise at the end of the section gives a topological example proving the splitting cannot be made natural.

Naturality of the sequences alone is enough for the result that gets used most often:

Corollary 3.4. If a chain map between chain complexes of free abelian groups induces an isomorphism on homology groups, then it induces an isomorphism on cohomology groups with any coefficient group G. — Hatcher §3.1, Corollary 3.4, p. 196

Proof. Naturality gives a commutative ladder whose two rows are the universal coefficient sequences for C and C′, padded with zeros to five terms. The outer vertical maps are (α∗)∗ applied to Ext and to Hom; since α∗ is an isomorphism on homology, both are isomorphisms, and the two outermost maps are the identity of the zero group. The five lemma then forces the middle map α∗ to be an isomorphism. ∎

Finally, the generalisation that matters for everything downstream. Replace abelian groups by modules over a ring R and Hom by HomR. The single fact the argument needed was that subgroups of free abelian groups are free; submodules of free R-modules are free when R is a principal ideal domain, so for such R one obtains natural split short exact sequences 0 → ExtR(Hn−1(C), G) → Hn(C;G) → HomR(Hn(C), G) → 0. And if R is a field, every R-module is free, so one may take free resolutions of the form 0 → F0 → H → 0 and the ExtR term is always zero.

That last sentence is the one to keep. Over a field the correction term disappears, h is an isomorphism, and cohomology is the honest dual of homology — which is why a persistence computation over 𝔽p may run on either side and get the same ranks, the fact at2-08 turns into an algorithm.

Self-check 3. A chain complex of free abelian groups has H3 = 0 and H2 = ℤ5. What is H3(C;ℤ)?

Corollary 3.3 reads off two different degrees: the free part from degree n and the torsion from degree n − 1. Here the free part of H3 is zero but T2 = ℤ5, so H3(C;ℤ) ≅ ℤ5. This is precisely the phenomenon the prediction gate asked about, one dimension along.

Self-check 4. Which of these is the correct reason ExtR vanishes when R is a field?

Ext(H,G) is by definition H1 of a dualised free resolution. Over a field every module is free, so H itself may serve as F0 with F1 = 0, and the dual complex has nothing in degree 1 to take homology of. The first option confuses a property of the ring's elements with a property of its modules, and the third is simply false — HomR(H,G) is the surviving term.

Self-check 5. Why is Corollary 3.4 not immediate from Theorem 3.2 alone?

Theorem 3.2 tells you what Hn(C;G) is built from, for each C separately. To conclude that a map inducing isomorphisms on homology induces one on cohomology, you need the two sequences to sit in a commuting ladder under the chain map — that is the naturality statement, proved separately, and it is what lets the five lemma bite. The non-naturality of the splitting is a real caveat but not the obstruction here: the ladder of sequences is all Corollary 3.4 uses. Theorem 3.2 has no finite-generation hypothesis; that enters only at Corollary 3.3.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove the first of the three computational properties: Ext(H ⊕ H′, G) ≅ Ext(H,G) ⊕ Ext(H′,G). Do it as Hatcher indicates — by exhibiting a free resolution of H ⊕ H′ built from resolutions of the two summands — and be explicit about which lemma licenses you to use that particular resolution.

Hint: you will need Hom(A ⊕ B, G) ≅ Hom(A,G) ⊕ Hom(B,G), and the fact that taking homology commutes with finite direct sums of complexes.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Let F → H and F′ → H′ be free resolutions. Form the degreewise direct sum F ⊕ F′, with maps fi ⊕ fi′. Each Fi ⊕ Fi′ is free, being a direct sum of free abelian groups. Exactness is checked degreewise: an element (x, x′) is killed by fi ⊕ fi′ iff fi(x) = 0 and fi′(x′) = 0, so Ker(fi ⊕ fi′) = Ker fi ⊕ Ker fi′, and likewise Im(fi+1 ⊕ fi+1′) = Im fi+1 ⊕ Im fi+1′; the two agree summand by summand by exactness of F and F′. So F ⊕ F′ is a free resolution of H ⊕ H′.

Now dualise. The natural isomorphism Hom(A ⊕ B, G) ≅ Hom(A,G) ⊕ Hom(B,G) identifies the dual complex (F ⊕ F′)∗ with F∗ ⊕ F′∗, compatibly with the coboundary maps, since the dual of fi ⊕ fi′ is fi∗ ⊕ fi′∗ under that identification. Taking homology in degree 1 and using that kernels and images of a direct sum of maps are the direct sums of the kernels and images,

H1(F ⊕ F′; G) ≅ H1(F;G) ⊕ H1(F′;G).

Finally, Lemma 3.1(b) says H1 of a dualised free resolution depends only on the group being resolved and on G, not on the resolution. So the left-hand side is Ext(H ⊕ H′, G) and the right-hand side is Ext(H,G) ⊕ Ext(H′,G). ∎

Where the lemma was indispensable. Without it, the computation above would establish only that this particular resolution of H ⊕ H′ gives that answer, leaving open the possibility that a different resolution gives another. It is Lemma 3.1(b) that turns a computation with one convenient resolution into a statement about Ext.
