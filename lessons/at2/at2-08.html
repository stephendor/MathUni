Persistent cohomology and circular coordinates (at2-08)

The same barcode, computed the other way round — and much faster. Then a bonus, if the class lifts to the integers: a 1-cocycle becomes a real circular coordinate on your data.

Seven units of cohomology have been abstract. This one cashes them. A point cloud sampled from something roughly annular has a one-dimensional hole; persistent homology will tell you the hole is there. What it will not do is tell you where you are around the hole — assign to each data point an angle, consistently, so that walking round the annulus advances the angle by exactly one turn.

Cohomology can. And the reason is a single sentence from homotopy theory that fixes the coefficient ring for you, whether or not it is the ring your barcode computation preferred.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Persistent cohomology is computed over a finite field 𝔽p, for good numerical reasons. Circular coordinates come from the fact that homotopy classes of maps X → S1 correspond to H1(X;ℤ). Predict what this mismatch costs.

Correct, and this is the distinction the whole lesson turns on. Duality over a field (at2-03) makes 𝔽p-cohomology a legitimate way to get the barcode. It says nothing about producing a map to S1, because that correspondence is with H1(X;ℤ) and with no other coefficient ring. Step 3 of the construction below exists solely to cross that gap, and it succeeds only under a hypothesis about p-torsion in H2(X;ℤ) — an assumption to be checked, not a fact to be assumed. A bigger prime does not help: the obstruction is torsion, not rounding.

Why this matters for the mission: Over a field, persistent cohomology of a filtration yields the same barcode as persistent homology while reducing far more cheaply in practice — the speed-up tda1-09 named. Circular coordinates are a SEPARATE construction with a stronger requirement: because S1 is a K(ℤ,1), a map from the data to S1 corresponds up to homotopy to an INTEGRAL class in H1(−;ℤ), so a class computed over 𝔽p must first be lifted to ℤ and the lift verified before it can be smoothed and integrated into an actual coordinate.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Simplicial cohomology, concretely, over 𝔽2

Dey and Wang set cohomology up over a field from the start, so that everything in sight is a vector space. Their §2.5.4 is the computational face of at2-01's algebra, and it is worth seeing the same definitions in the notation an implementation actually uses.

Cochains. A p-cochain is a homomorphism φ : Cp → ℤ2 from the chain group to the coefficient ring. It is given by its evaluation φ(σ) ∈ {0, 1} on every p-simplex σ, a chain c = Σiαiσi receiving φ(c) = Σiαiφ(σi). The p-cochains form a vector space Cp dual to Cp, with (φ + φ′)(c) = φ(c) + φ′(c) and (αφ)(c) = αφ(c). For a chain c, the cochain assigning 1 to exactly the simplices with non-zero coefficient in c is its dual cochain c∗. — Dey & Wang §2.5.4, p. 55

Coboundary. For a p-cochain φ, its coboundary is the homomorphism δφ : Cp+1 → ℤ2 defined by δφ(c) = φ(∂c), giving the sequence 0 = C−1 → C0 → C1 → ⋯ → Ck → Ck+1 = 0 for a simplicial k-complex. — Dey & Wang §2.5.4, p. 55

Note that this is at2-01's δφ = φ∂ written for a specific model. The p-coboundaries form Bp and the p-cocycles — those φ with δφ the zero homomorphism — form Zp.

Fact 2.13. For p > 0, δp ∘ δp−1 = 0, which implies Bp ⊆ Zp. — Dey & Wang §2.5.4, p. 56

Proof. For any chain c, δδφ(c) = δφ(∂c) = φ(∂∂c) = φ(0) = 0, using ∂∂ = 0 and that φ is a homomorphism. Hence every coboundary is a cocycle. ∎

Definition 2.29 (cohomology group). Since Bp is a subgroup of Zp, the quotient Hp = Zp/Bp is well defined and is called the p-th cohomology group. — Dey & Wang §2.5.4, Definition 2.29, p. 56

One warning Dey and Wang make explicitly, and it is worth taking seriously. Hp(K) is not equal to Hom(Hp(K), ℤ2), although over ℤ2 the two are isomorphic — a distinction at2-02 spent a whole lesson on. What you compute with is the quotient of cocycles by coboundaries, and what a computation hands you is a representative cocycle, not a functional on homology.

Worked example 1: Dey's Example 2.2

Example. Take K with vertices a, b, c and edges ab, bc, ac, and no triangle. Let φ be the 1-cochain with support {ac} — that is, φ(ac) = 1 and φ(ab) = φ(bc) = 0.

It is a cocycle. There is no triangle, so no non-zero 2-cochain exists and δ1φ = 0 automatically.

It is not a coboundary. Suppose φ = δ0φ′ for a 0-cochain φ′, an assignment of 0s and 1s to vertices. Then, since δ0φ′(uv) = φ′(u + v) = φ′(u) + φ′(v),

φ′(a) + φ′(c) = 1,   φ′(a) + φ′(b) = 0,   φ′(b) + φ′(c) = 0.

Add all three equations. Each of φ′(a), φ′(b), φ′(c) appears exactly twice, and over ℤ2 a repeated term cancels, so the left side is 0 while the right is 1. Contradiction, so φ is not a coboundary and [φ] is a non-trivial class in H1.

Now fill in the triangle abc. The same φ is no longer a cocycle: δ1φ(abc) = φ(ab + ac + bc) = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1 ≠ 0. But the cochain with support {ab, ac} is a cocycle, since 1 + 1 + 0 = 0 over ℤ2; and it is a coboundary, produced by the 0-cochain assigning 1 to the vertex a and 0 to b and c. The presence of a single 2-simplex has changed both which cochains are cocycles and which cocycles are trivial.

Cohomologous classes. Dey's third complex has a cocycle φ with support {cd, ce} that is not a coboundary, and another φ′ with support {bf, bg} whose class is cohomologous to it: [φ] + [φ′] = [φ + φ′] = [0], because φ + φ′ is the coboundary obtained by assigning 1 to the vertices a, b and c. Over ℤ2, where every element is its own inverse, "cohomologous" and "equal in H1" are the same statement.

Worked example 2 (faded). For the triangle-free K of worked example 1, compute dim H1(K) by counting.

Cocycles. With no 2-simplices, every 1-cochain is a cocycle, so dim Z1 = ⟨your step: how many edges?⟩.

Coboundaries. B1 is the image of δ0 on the space of 0-cochains, which has dimension 3. The kernel of δ0 is the cochains constant on each connected component; K is connected, so that kernel has dimension ⟨your step⟩, and by rank–nullity dim B1 = 3 − 1 = 2.

Quotient. dim H1 = dim Z1 − dim B1 = 1, so there are exactly two classes: the trivial one and [φ]. This agrees with the geometry — K is a triangle boundary, a circle, with one one-dimensional hole.

Gap solutions: dim Z1 = 3, one for each edge; the kernel of δ0 has dimension 1.

Fact 2.14. A simplicial map f : K1 → K2 induces a homomorphism f∗ : Hp(K2) → Hp(K1) for every p ≥ 0 — in the opposite direction to the map induced on homology. It comes from the cochain map f#(φ)(c) = φ(f#(c)). — Dey & Wang §2.5.4, Fact 2.14, p. 57

Contravariance again, and this time it has an operational consequence: in a filtration the inclusions run forwards, so the induced cohomology maps run backwards along the filtration. Any persistence algorithm built on cohomology processes the filtration in that reversed sense, which is exactly what Dey and Wang's annotation method of §4.2.1 exploits — the section Fact 2.14 explicitly points forward to.

Self-check 1. Adding the triangle abc to the triangle-free complex changed the status of the cochain with support {ac}. What exactly changed?

δ1 is a map into the 2-cochains, so when there are no 2-simplices it is the zero map and every 1-cochain is a cocycle for free. Introducing abc gives δ1 somewhere to land, and φ fails the test. This is worth internalising: whether a given cochain is a cocycle depends on the simplices above it, not only on the ones it is supported on.

Self-check 2. Why does Dey and Wang's chapter insist that Hp(K) is not equal to Hom(Hp(K), ℤ2)?

They are isomorphic over ℤ2 — that is stated in the same paragraph — so the first option is wrong, and the third is plainly false. The point is that the isomorphism is a theorem about the two objects, not an identification you may make silently. It matters here because everything downstream in this lesson operates on a representative cocycle: you smooth it, you integrate it. Only an actual cochain can be smoothed.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Circular coordinates, and the step that is easy to skip

Ghrist §6.13 poses the problem exactly. Persistent homology addressed the topology of a point cloud; a cohomological approach addresses the related problem of coordinatising one. Let Ω ⊂ ℝn be a point cloud whose topology is known or suspected to be sufficiently circular to merit outfitting circular coordinates Θ : Ω → S1, respecting the underlying topology of the space X ⊂ ℝn (homotopic to S1) that Ω is presumed to sample. The solution of de Silva, Morozov and Vejdemo-Johansson runs in four steps.

Step 1 — why cohomology at all. For any space X, the group of basepoint-preserving homotopy classes [X, S1] of maps X → S1 is isomorphic to H1(X;ℤ). The coordinatisation function Θ : X → S1 is therefore naturally approachable via cohomology. — Ghrist §6.13, p. 131

Step 2 — compute. To find a cohomology class for X from a sampling of nodes, compute the persistent cohomology of a sequence of Vietoris–Rips complexes. Field coefficients are required for the Structure Theorem (Ghrist's Theorem 5.21); coefficients in a finite field 𝔽p are preferred for numerical reasons, to avoid roundoff errors. — Ghrist §6.13, p. 131

Step 3 — lift to ℤ. A persistent class [αp] ∈ H1(X;𝔽p) can be converted to an integral class [α] ∈ H1(X;ℤ) using the short exact sequence of coefficients 0 → ℤ →·p ℤ → 𝔽p → 0, which yields a short exact sequence of cochain complexes and hence a long exact sequence

⟶ H1(X;ℤ) ⟶ H1(X;𝔽p)  δ⟶  H2(X;ℤ)  ·p⟶  H2(X;ℤ).

The kernel of ·p on H2(X;ℤ) consists of the p-torsional classes. By exactness ker(·p) = im δ; assuming this is zero, H1(X;ℤ) → H1(X;𝔽p) is surjective and classes over 𝔽p lift to integral classes. — Ghrist §6.13, pp. 131–132

Step 4 — smooth. The resulting integer cocycle α may be a poor S1-coordinatisation: all the circular motion may be concentrated over a small subset of X. To relax α, lift to ℝ coefficients and find a cohomologous harmonic cocycle ᾱ. Thanks to the local-averaging properties of the Laplacian, this 1-cocycle integrates to a well-regulated coordinate function Θ : X → S1. — Ghrist §6.13, p. 132

Worked example 3: the lift, done carefully

Example. Suppose H2(X;ℤ) has no p-torsion. Show that every class in H1(X;𝔽p) lifts to H1(X;ℤ).

Step 1 — locate the obstruction. In the long exact sequence, the map immediately after H1(X;𝔽p) is δ : H1(X;𝔽p) → H2(X;ℤ), and the map after that is multiplication by p on H2(X;ℤ).

Step 2 — exactness at H2(X;ℤ). Exactness there says im δ = ker(·p). The kernel of multiplication by p is by definition the set of classes killed by p, that is the p-torsion of H2(X;ℤ).

Step 3 — use the hypothesis. No p-torsion means ker(·p) = 0, hence im δ = 0, hence δ is the zero map and ker δ is the whole of H1(X;𝔽p).

Step 4 — exactness at H1(X;𝔽p). There, ker δ equals the image of H1(X;ℤ) → H1(X;𝔽p). Since ker δ is everything, that map is surjective: every 𝔽p class is the reduction of an integral class, which is exactly what "lifts" means. ∎

What the argument does not say. It gives no procedure for finding the lift, and it does not say the lift is unique — the sequence continues to the left, and the ambiguity is measured there. What it gives is existence, conditional on a hypothesis about X that the data does not hand you.

Worked example 4 (faded): what the hypothesis is worth. Ghrist writes that p-torsional classes in H2(X;ℤ) "would seem to be rare occurrences in organic spaces X living behind data sets" for p > 2.

Read the modality. "Would seem to be rare" is a heuristic expectation about the class of spaces one meets in practice, not a ⟨your step: what kind of statement would it need to be?⟩.

Consequence for a pipeline. The hypothesis ker(·p) = 0 is therefore an assumption to be declared or checked, not one that holds automatically. If it fails, worked example 3 does not apply and the class need not lift — so the object being smoothed in step 4 is ⟨your step: what is it not?⟩.

The observable symptom. A non-lifting class smoothed and integrated anyway produces a Θ that fails to close up: integrating around some loop returns a value differing from its start by something that is not a whole number of turns. Every local computation succeeds; only the global consistency fails — which is, of course, precisely the shape of every cohomological obstruction in this module, from the mountain trails of at2-01 onwards.

Gap solutions: it would need to be a theorem about X, with a hypothesis that can be checked; and the smoothed object is not an integral class, hence does not correspond to any homotopy class of maps X → S1.

The distinction to carry out of this unit. There are two separate uses of cohomology here and they have different requirements.

Barcode computation. Over a field, cohomology is the exact dual of homology (Hatcher §3.1, at2-03), so the dimensions agree degree by degree and computing on the cohomology side is a legitimate route to the same answer, chosen for speed. Here the coefficient field is largely an implementation choice — subject to the standing caveat that torsion invisible to one field may be visible to another.

Circular coordinates. Here the coefficient ring is the entire content. The correspondence [X, S1] ≅ H1(X;ℤ) holds over ℤ and over nothing else, so an 𝔽p class must be lifted, and the lift must be verified, before it is smoothed and integrated. Treating a barcode-grade 𝔽p class as a coordinate-grade integral class is the one error this unit exists to prevent.

Self-check 3. Which step of the four fixes the coefficient ring as ℤ, and why?

Step 1 is where the requirement enters, and it is not negotiable: the homotopy-theoretic input names H1(X;ℤ). Step 2 wants the opposite — the Structure Theorem requires a field, and 𝔽p is chosen for numerical reasons. That tension between steps 1 and 2 is the entire reason step 3 exists. Step 4 works over ℝ coefficients, after the integral class has been obtained.

Self-check 4. The kernel of multiplication by p on H2(X;ℤ) is non-zero. What follows?

The barcode is unaffected — step 2 is complete and correct in itself, which is exactly why the failure is easy to miss. What breaks is the bridge to step 1: by exactness im δ = ker(·p), so a non-zero kernel permits a non-zero δ, and a class with δ[αp] ≠ 0 is not in the image of the reduction map and does not lift. Nothing here says X fails to be circular; it says one particular class fails to be integral.

Self-check 5. Why is the harmonic representative of step 4 sought within the same cohomology class rather than as a fresh cocycle?

Step 1 identifies homotopy classes of maps with cohomology classes, not with individual cocycles. So every representative of [α] encodes the same circular structure, and one is free to choose the representative that integrates to the most usable coordinate — which is what the Laplacian's local averaging supplies. Leaving the class would change which map to S1 you are building.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove Fact 2.13 from the definition δφ(c) = φ(∂c), and then prove that the coboundary of a dual cochain behaves as follows: for a p-chain c, the cochain δ(c∗) evaluated on a (p+1)-chain d equals c∗(∂d). Using this, prove that in a complex with no (p+1)-simplices every p-cochain is a cocycle, and explain why that makes Hp = Cp/Bp in the top dimension.

Hint: everything follows by pushing δ across to ∂ and using ∂∂ = 0; for the last part ask what the target of δp is when there are no (p+1)-simplices.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Fact 2.13. Let φ be a (p−1)-cochain and c any (p+1)-chain. Then

(δpδp−1φ)(c) = (δp−1φ)(∂c) = φ(∂∂c) = φ(0) = 0,

using the definition of δ twice, then ∂∂ = 0, then that a homomorphism sends 0 to 0. As c was arbitrary, δpδp−1 = 0. Consequently any coboundary δψ satisfies δ(δψ) = 0 and is a cocycle, so Bp ⊆ Zp and Definition 2.29's quotient makes sense. ∎

The dual cochain. By definition δ(c∗)(d) = c∗(∂d) for any (p+1)-chain d — this is simply the definition of δ applied to the particular cochain c∗, and it says that δ(c∗) detects which simplices of ∂d appear in c. ∎

Top dimension. Suppose K has no (p+1)-simplices. Then Cp+1 is the zero vector space, since a cochain is determined by its values on (p+1)-simplices and there are none. The map δp : Cp → Cp+1 therefore has zero target, so δpφ = 0 for every p-cochain φ, and Zp = Cp. Hence Hp = Zp/Bp = Cp/Bp. ∎

What this explains. It is why the triangle-free complex of worked example 1 had every 1-cochain a cocycle, and why filling in the triangle abc changed that. In the top dimension the cocycle condition is vacuous, so all the information in Hp comes from which cochains are coboundaries — and that is a statement about the simplices one dimension down. The asymmetry is the mirror image of homology, where the top-dimensional condition that is vacuous is being a boundary.
