Poincaré duality (at2-07)

On a closed orientable n-manifold, the k-dimensional holes and the (n−k)-dimensional ones are the same holes counted from opposite ends.

Draw a grid on a torus. Now draw a second grid offset by half a cell in each direction. Every vertex of the first grid sits inside exactly one square of the second; every edge of the first crosses exactly one edge of the second; every square of the first contains exactly one vertex of the second. The two cell structures are the same size, counted in reverse.

That picture is the whole theorem, and it is also the reason Hatcher does not use it: constructing such dual cell structures on a general manifold takes real manifold theory. The route taken instead replaces the geometry with a single algebraic operation — cap product with the fundamental class of at2-06 — and gets a theorem that applies to every manifold.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. ℝP2 is a closed connected 2-manifold with H0(ℝP2;ℤ) = ℤ and, by Theorem 3.26 of at2-06, H2(ℝP2;ℤ) = 0. Duality would say Hk ≅ Hn−k. Predict which hypothesis has to be doing the work.

Correct. ℝP2 is certainly closed and connected; what it lacks is a ℤ-orientation, hence a fundamental class [M] ∈ H2(M;ℤ), hence any map to cap with. Notice how sharply the hypothesis is about the pair (M, R) and not about M alone: every manifold is ℤ2-orientable, so duality is restored for ℝP2 the moment you work over ℤ2. That is why manifold topologists reach for ℤ2 coefficients so readily, and why a TDA claim resting on duality must say which field it is over.

Why this matters for the mission: For a closed R-orientable n-manifold, cap product with the fundamental class gives an isomorphism H^k(M;R) -> H_{n-k}(M;R). The hypotheses are load-bearing — closed and orientable — and la-10's dual-space pairing is the linear-algebra shadow of the statement.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cap product

Duality will be implemented by a map Hk(M;R) → Hn−k(M;R). Nothing so far produces maps from cohomology to homology, so the first job is to build one. It is the cap product, and it is close kin to the cup product of at2-04 — the same trick of cutting a simplex at a vertex, with one of the two pieces kept as a chain rather than evaluated.

Cap product. For a space X and coefficient ring R, the R-bilinear cap product ⌢ : Ck(X;R) × Cℓ(X;R) → Ck−ℓ(X;R), defined for k ≥ ℓ, is given by

σ ⌢ φ = φ(σ | [v0, …, vℓ]) · σ | [vℓ, …, vk]

for σ : Δk → X and φ ∈ Cℓ(X;R). — Hatcher §3.3, p. 239

Compare the cup product formula of at2-04. There, both faces were fed to cochains and the two values multiplied in R. Here the front face is fed to φ, producing a scalar, and that scalar multiplies the back face, which survives as a chain. The degree bookkeeping matches: k − ℓ, because ℓ + 1 vertices were consumed and one, vℓ, was handed back.

Lemma C (boundary formula for cap product). ∂(σ ⌢ φ) = (−1)ℓ(∂σ ⌢ φ − σ ⌢ δφ). — Hatcher §3.3, p. 240, verified there by direct calculation

Hatcher checks it by writing out three sums. Expanding ∂σ ⌢ φ splits into the terms omitting a vertex from the front face and those omitting one from the back; σ ⌢ δφ reproduces the first group exactly; and what is left is ∂(σ ⌢ φ). The point is not the bookkeeping but what the formula immediately buys, and that is the guided proof below: cap product descends to homology and cohomology, giving

Hk(X;R) × Hℓ(X;R)  ⌢⟶  Hk−ℓ(X;R),

R-linear in each variable. The same formulas give relative forms Hk(X,A;R) × Hℓ(X;R) → Hk−ℓ(X,A;R) and Hk(X,A;R) × Hℓ(X,A;R) → Hk−ℓ(X;R), and — for A and B open in X — a more general Hk(X, A ∪ B;R) × Hℓ(X,A;R) → Hk−ℓ(X,B;R).

Naturality of cap product. For f : X → Y, both a covariant and a contravariant functor are involved, so no square simply commutes; the content is the formula

f∗(α) ⌢ φ = f∗(α ⌢ f∗(φ)).

It follows by substituting fσ for σ in the definition. — Hatcher §3.3, pp. 240–241

Read that formula slowly: it says a class pushed forward and then capped with a cochain upstairs equals the pushforward of the class capped with the pulled-back cochain. It is the correct statement of "cap product is natural" when the two variables run in opposite directions, and it is what makes the duality map behave under maps of manifolds.

Self-check 1. Why does cap product lower degree by ℓ rather than raising it?

Count vertices, as with the cup product. The simplex σ has k + 1 vertices; φ eats [v0,…,vℓ] and returns a scalar; the surviving chain spans [vℓ,…,vk], which is k − ℓ + 1 vertices, a (k − ℓ)-simplex. The variance remark is true but is not a proof of anything, and the third option describes a formula that does not appear.

Self-check 2. Which is the correct statement of naturality for cap product?

The first option is not even well typed: cohomology has no pushforward f∗. The third is not well typed either, since homology has no pullback. Only the middle statement respects the variances — the cohomology variable travels backwards along f while the homology variable travels forwards, which is precisely why Hatcher says the diagram does not quite commute but the spirit of commutativity is in this formula.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The duality theorem and what it forces

Theorem 3.30 (Poincaré Duality). If M is a closed R-orientable n-manifold with fundamental class [M] ∈ Hn(M;R), then the map D : Hk(M;R) → Hn−k(M;R) defined by D(α) = [M] ⌢ α is an isomorphism for all k. — Hatcher §3.3, Theorem 3.30, p. 241

Every word of the hypothesis is doing work. Closed and R-orientable are exactly the two conditions at2-06 showed to be equivalent to the existence of the fundamental class — Theorem 3.26 supplies it, and the converse argument showed a fundamental class forces both. Without [M] there is nothing to cap with, so the map D is not merely non-invertible: it is undefined.

Visual: the dual cell structure the proof deliberately avoids

The geometric picture Hatcher sketches for orientation, and then sets aside: on the torus, take the standard square grid lifted from the integer lattice in ℝ2, and translate it by (1/2, 1/2). Each i-cell of one structure meets exactly one (n−i)-cell of the other, transversely, in a single point. Press the button to overlay the dual grid.

For a surface this pairs vertices with faces and edges with edges; on the 3-torus, Hatcher notes, each edge has a dual 2-cell it pierces orthogonally and each vertex lies in a dual 3-cell. All the manifolds one commonly meets, differentiable ones in particular, carry such dually paired structures — but constructing them requires manifold theory, so the proof takes the algebraic route instead, by induction with Mayer–Vietoris sequences over open subsets, which forces the introduction of cohomology with compact supports. That machinery is beyond this lesson; what we take from §3.3 here is the statement, the computations, and the consequences.

Worked example 1: duality on a surface, computed with cap products

Example 3.31. Let M be the closed orientable surface of genus g, obtained from a 4g-gon by identifying edge pairs according to a1b1a1−1b1−1⋯agbgag−1bg−1, with the Δ-complex structure obtained by coning the polygon to its centre.

Step 1 — why simplicial computation is legitimate. Cap products are defined for simplicial homology and cohomology by exactly the same formula as in the singular theory, so the isomorphism between the theories respects cap products.

Step 2 — the fundamental class. [M] generating H2(M) is represented by the 2-cycle formed by the sum of all 4g 2-simplices with appropriate signs — the simplicial construction of at2-06, whose sign choice is available exactly because M is orientable.

Step 3 — the cohomology classes. The edges ai and bi form a basis of H1(M). Under H1(M) ≅ Hom(H1(M), ℤ), the class αi dual to ai assigns 1 to ai and 0 to the other basis elements; it is represented by the cocycle φi giving the value 1 to the 1-simplices meeting the arc labelled αi and 0 to the others. Similarly βi is represented by ψi.

Step 4 — cap. Applying the definition of cap product directly,

[M] ⌢ φi = bi,   [M] ⌢ ψi = −ai,

since in each case there is exactly one 2-simplex [v0,v1,v2] on whose front edge [v0,v1] the cocycle is non-zero. So bi is the Poincaré dual of αi and −ai is the Poincaré dual of βi.

Step 5 — read it geometrically. Identifying αi with its Hom-dual ai and βi with bi, the classes ai and bi are Poincaré duals of each other, up to sign. Geometrically this is the statement that the loop αi is homotopic to bi, and βi to ai — the transverse arc used to define the cocycle is, up to homotopy, the dual cycle itself.

The nonorientable case. The closed nonorientable surface N of genus g is treated the same way over ℤ2, viewing N as a 2g-gon with consecutive edge pairs identified according to a1²⋯ag². There [N] ⌢ φi = ai, so ai is its own Poincaré dual — and geometrically the loops ai are homotopic to their dual loops αi.

Worked example 2 (faded): duality degree by degree. Let M be the closed orientable surface of genus g. Check Theorem 3.30 in every degree using the known homology H0 = ℤ, H1 = ℤ2g, H2 = ℤ.

Cohomology first. All the homology is free, so the universal coefficient theorem of at2-02 gives Hk(M;ℤ) ≅ Hom(Hk(M),ℤ) with no Ext term: H0 = ℤ, H1 = ⟨your step⟩, H2 = ℤ.

Pair the degrees. With n = 2, duality pairs k with 2 − k: H0 → H2, H1 → H1, H2 → H0. Written out, these are ℤ → ℤ, ℤ2g → ℤ2g, ℤ → ℤ — each a map between free abelian groups of ⟨your step: what relation between the ranks?⟩, as Theorem 3.30 requires.

Notice the middle. Degree 1 is paired with itself. A self-paired middle degree is what turns the cup product into a bilinear form on a single group, which is the subject of Proposition 3.38 below.

Gap solutions: H1(M;ℤ) ≅ ℤ2g; and each pair has equal rank.

Three consequences

Corollary 3.37. A closed manifold of odd dimension has Euler characteristic zero. — Hatcher §3.3, Corollary 3.37, p. 249

Proof in the orientable case. Let M be closed and orientable of odd dimension n. Theorem 3.30 gives rank Hi(M;ℤ) = rank Hn−i(M;ℤ), which equals rank Hn−i(M;ℤ) by the universal coefficient theorem — the Ext term being finite, it contributes no rank. Since n is odd, i ≠ n − i for every i and (−1)n−i = −(−1)i, so the terms of Σi(−1)i rank Hi(M;ℤ) cancel in pairs. Hatcher handles the nonorientable case by the same argument over ℤ2, with dim Hi(M;ℤ2) in place of rank, and then checks that this alternating sum equals the Euler characteristic. ∎

Proposition 3.38. The cup product pairing is nonsingular for closed R-orientable manifolds when R is a field, or when R = ℤ and torsion in H∗(M;ℤ) is factored out. — Hatcher §3.3, Proposition 3.38, p. 250

Corollary 3.39. If M is a closed connected orientable n-manifold, then an element α ∈ Hk(M;ℤ) generates an infinite cyclic summand of Hk(M;ℤ) iff there exists β ∈ Hn−k(M;ℤ) such that α ⌣ β is a generator of Hn(M;ℤ) ≅ ℤ. With coefficients in a field this holds for any α ≠ 0. — Hatcher §3.3, Corollary 3.39, p. 250

Corollary 3.39 is duality turned into a working tool: it converts a question about summands into a question about finding a partner class. Hatcher's Example 3.40 uses exactly this to pin down the ring structure of ℂPn. By induction H2i(ℂPn;ℤ) is generated by αi for i < n; the corollary then supplies m with α ⌣ mαn−1 = mαn generating H2n, which can only happen if m = ±1, and therefore H∗(ℂPn;ℤ) ≅ ℤ[α]/(αn+1) — the ring at2-04 quoted and could not yet prove.

Self-check 3. Which failure does dropping "closed" produce, concretely?

ℝ2 is a clean counterexample: orientable, connected, and duality fails in degree 0 for the crudest possible reason. Deeper still, at2-06's converse argument showed that a fundamental class forces compactness, so on a noncompact manifold there is no [M] and D is undefined rather than merely non-bijective. Duality is recovered for noncompact manifolds, but only after replacing Hk by cohomology with compact supports — Hatcher's Theorem 3.35, beyond this lesson.

Self-check 4. The Klein bottle K has H1(K;ℤ) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ2 and H2(K;ℤ) = 0. Is the ℤ2 an instance of Poincaré duality?

Theorem 3.26 of at2-06 reads H2(K;ℤ) = 0 as non-orientability, which removes the hypothesis Theorem 3.30 needs. The ℤ2 torsion in H1 is precisely what Corollary 3.28 predicts for a closed nonorientable n-manifold in degree n − 1, and Hatcher proves that from the universal coefficient theorem, not from duality. Duality does return over ℤ2 — but then it relates ℤ2-cohomology to ℤ2-homology, and says nothing about integral torsion.

Self-check 5. Corollary 3.37 says a closed odd-dimensional manifold has χ = 0. Which is the sharpest correct reading?

The statement carries no orientability hypothesis, and that is not an oversight: ℤ2-orientability is free, so the pairing argument runs over ℤ2 for any closed M, once one checks that the ℤ2 alternating sum equals the integral Euler characteristic. The last option is plainly false — a closed orientable surface of genus g has χ = 2 − 2g, and evenness of the dimension is exactly what lets the middle degree pair with itself instead of cancelling.

Guided proof

Prompt. From the boundary formula ∂(σ ⌢ φ) = (−1)ℓ(∂σ ⌢ φ − σ ⌢ δφ), prove the three statements that make the cap product descend to homology and cohomology: the cap product of a cycle and a cocycle is a cycle; the cap product of a cycle and a coboundary is a boundary; and the cap product of a boundary and a cocycle is a boundary. Then explain in one sentence why those three together give a well-defined map Hk(X;R) × Hℓ(X;R) → Hk−ℓ(X;R).

Hint: set one of ∂σ, δφ to zero at a time and read what the formula collapses to. Extend from a single simplex σ to a general chain by R-bilinearity.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Throughout write the formula as ∂(σ ⌢ φ) = ±(∂σ ⌢ φ − σ ⌢ δφ); the sign (−1)ℓ never affects whether something is zero. The cap product is R-bilinear, so it suffices to argue on a single singular simplex σ and extend linearly to chains.

(i) Cycle ⌢ cocycle is a cycle. Suppose ∂σ = 0 and δφ = 0. Both terms on the right vanish, so ∂(σ ⌢ φ) = 0.

(ii) Cycle ⌢ coboundary is a boundary. Suppose ∂σ = 0 and φ = δψ. The first term vanishes, leaving ∂(σ ⌢ δψ) = ∓(σ ⌢ δδψ) = 0 — but that shows σ ⌢ δψ is a cycle, not yet a boundary. Instead apply the formula to ψ itself: with ∂σ = 0 it gives ∂(σ ⌢ ψ) = ∓(σ ⌢ δψ), exhibiting σ ⌢ δψ = ∓∂(σ ⌢ ψ) as a boundary.

(iii) Boundary ⌢ cocycle is a boundary. Suppose σ = ∂τ and δφ = 0. Apply the formula to τ: ∂(τ ⌢ φ) = ±(∂τ ⌢ φ − τ ⌢ δφ) = ±(∂τ ⌢ φ), so ∂τ ⌢ φ = ±∂(τ ⌢ φ) is a boundary.

Why this suffices. By (i) a cycle and a cocycle produce a cycle, so a class is defined; by (ii) and (iii) altering either representative by a coboundary or a boundary alters the result by a boundary, so that class depends only on the homology class of the first argument and the cohomology class of the second. Hence the induced map on Hk(X;R) × Hℓ(X;R) is well defined, and it is R-linear in each variable because the cochain-level operation is. ∎

The step worth noticing. In (ii) the naive move — substituting φ = δψ into the formula — proves the wrong thing. The right move is to apply the formula one degree down, to ψ, so that the coboundary appears on the right of the equation where it can be recognised as a boundary. The same manoeuvre appeared for cup product in at2-04, and it is the standard way a Leibniz rule is used to prove a product descends.
