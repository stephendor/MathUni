Cochain complexes and cohomology groups (at2-01)

Turn every arrow in the chain complex around. Almost nothing changes — and then everything does.

A hiking club is mapping a mountain. At every junction of trails they nail up a sign giving the net change in elevation along each trail to the next junction: +100 m along this one, −40 m along that one. Two things are now true at once. Anyone can walk the mountain using only the signs, and — if the signs were computed from real elevations — the numbers around any closed circuit must cancel exactly.

So a natural question arises. Given an arbitrary set of signs, was there ever an elevation function behind them? Hatcher opens Chapter 3 with exactly this graph, and the answer is a group: the obstruction to reconstructing the function from its differences is the first cohomology group.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Three junctions A, B, C, with trail signs reading: A → B is +100 m, B → C is +150 m, and the direct trail A → C is +200 m. Is there an assignment of elevations to A, B, C consistent with all three signs?

Correct. Walking A → B → C accumulates 100 + 150 = 250, while the direct sign says 200; no single value of the elevation at C can satisfy both. The mismatch, 250 − 200 = 50, is not an error in one sign in particular — it is a property of the whole system. By the end of Segment 1 you will be able to name it: the class of these signs is a non-zero element of H1(X; ℤ), and it is non-zero precisely because the deficit 50 is non-zero.

Why this matters for the mission: Cohomology is homology's dual theory: H^n(X;G) is the homology of the dualised complex Hom(C_*(X), G). Over a field its ranks agree with homology's, which is exactly why persistent cohomology computes the same barcode (at2-08).

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The idea of cohomology: differences on a graph

Hatcher starts a dimension lower than you might expect, and with no dualisation in sight. Let X be a one-dimensional Δ-complex — an oriented graph — and fix an abelian group G.

The two groups. Write Δ0(X;G) for the abelian group of all functions from the vertices of X to G, and Δ1(X;G) for the abelian group of all functions from the edges of X to G. Define the homomorphism δ : Δ0(X;G) → Δ1(X;G) by sending φ to the function whose value on an oriented edge [v0, v1] is the difference φ(v1) − φ(v0). — Hatcher, "The Idea of Cohomology", pp. 186–187

The mountain is Hatcher's own illustration: X is a system of trails, the vertices are junctions, φ assigns to each junction its elevation, and δφ measures the net change in elevation along each trail. An electrical circuit does just as well, with φ the voltage at each connection point and δφ the change in voltage across each component.

Simplicial cohomology of a graph. Regard δ : Δ0(X;G) → Δ1(X;G) as a chain complex with zeros before and after. Its homology groups are by definition the simplicial cohomology groups of X: H0(X;G) = Ker δ ⊂ Δ0(X;G) and H1(X;G) = Δ1(X;G) / Im δ. — Hatcher, p. 187

Read those two groups slowly, because they answer two different questions. The kernel asks which elevation functions have all differences zero. The quotient asks which systems of signs fail to come from any elevation function at all.

H0: the easy one

A function φ has δφ = 0 exactly when it takes the same value at both ends of every edge, which is to say that φ is constant on each component of X. So H0(X;G) is the group of all functions from the set of components of X to G — a direct product of copies of G, one for each component.

H1: solving δφ = ψ

The group H1(X;G) is trivial exactly when the equation δφ = ψ can be solved for every ψ. Hatcher's comparison is worth taking seriously: this is the calculus problem of finding a function with a prescribed derivative, with the difference operator δ in the role of differentiation. As in calculus, if a solution exists it is unique up to adding an element of Ker δ — a function constant on each component.

The solution method is the same as the one you just used on the mountain, made systematic. Choose a maximal tree in each component of X. Fixing φ arbitrarily at one basepoint vertex per component, the values of ψ along tree edges determine φ at every vertex by induction along the unique path in the tree. Every edge not in a maximal tree then imposes one further equation, which ψ may or may not satisfy — and ψ is free to take any value there. Hence:

Counting the obstruction. H1(X;G) is a direct product of copies of G, one copy for each edge of X not in one of the chosen maximal trees. Compare H1(X;G), which is a direct sum of copies of G indexed by the same set of edges. — Hatcher, p. 187

The same product-versus-sum relation holds one dimension down: H0(X;G) is a product of copies of G over the components and H0(X;G) a sum. For a finite graph the distinction is invisible; it is a genuine difference as soon as there are infinitely many factors.

Worked example 1: a graph with one independent loop

Example. Let X have vertices v0, v1, v2, v3 and edges a = [v0,v1], b = [v1,v2], c = [v0,v2], d = [v2,v3]. Compute H0(X;G) and H1(X;G).

Step 1 — write δ out. For φ ∈ Δ0(X;G):

δφ(a) = φ(v1) − φ(v0),   δφ(b) = φ(v2) − φ(v1),   δφ(c) = φ(v2) − φ(v0),   δφ(d) = φ(v3) − φ(v2).

Step 2 — kernel. Setting all four to zero forces φ(v0) = φ(v1) = φ(v2) = φ(v3). X is connected, so H0(X;G) ≅ G, one copy for the single component.

Step 3 — choose a maximal tree. T = {a, b, d} reaches every vertex and contains no loop, so it is a maximal tree. One edge, c, lies outside it.

Step 4 — solve along the tree. Given ψ ∈ Δ1(X;G), pick φ(v0) freely; the tree then forces φ(v1) = φ(v0) + ψ(a), φ(v2) = φ(v1) + ψ(b), φ(v3) = φ(v2) + ψ(d).

Step 5 — read the obstruction off the non-tree edge. For δφ = ψ we need in addition ψ(c) = φ(v2) − φ(v0) = ψ(a) + ψ(b). Nothing constrains ψ(c), so the failure is measured by the single element ψ(c) − ψ(a) − ψ(b) of G, and

H1(X;G) ≅ G, one copy for the one edge outside the maximal tree.

Note that the hook's mountain is this graph with d deleted and G = ℤ: the deficit was 200 − 100 − 150 = −50, non-zero, so those signs represented a non-zero class.

Worked example 2 (faded). Let Y have vertices u0, u1, w; edges e = [u0,u1] and f = [u0,u1] (two distinct edges with the same endpoints); and w isolated. Compute H0(Y;G) and H1(Y;G).

Components. Y has ⟨your step: how many components?⟩, namely {u0, u1} joined by e and f, and the isolated vertex w. Hence H0(Y;G) ≅ ⟨your step: which product of copies of G?⟩.

Maximal trees. In the first component take T1 = {e}; the second component is the single vertex w, whose maximal tree is empty. Edges outside the chosen trees: ⟨your step: list them⟩.

Conclusion. H1(Y;G) ≅ G, generated by the class of the deficit ψ(f) − ψ(e).

Gap solutions: two components; H0(Y;G) ≅ G × G; the only edge outside the trees is f.

You try (solo). Let Z be the complete graph on three vertices — edges [v0,v1], [v1,v2], [v0,v2] — with a fourth vertex v3 joined to v0 by two distinct edges. Compute H0(Z;G) and H1(Z;G) by the five steps above.

Hint: count vertices and edges first; a maximal tree of a connected graph on k vertices has k − 1 edges, so the number of copies of G in H1 is (number of edges) − (number of vertices) + (number of components).

Self-check 1. X is a graph with three components and no edges at all. What is H1(X;G)?

H1 counts one copy of G per edge outside the maximal trees. With no edges at all, Δ1(X;G) = 0, so H1(X;G) = 0. The three components show up in H0(X;G) ≅ G × G × G instead.

Self-check 2. Hatcher stresses that H1(X;G) is a direct product of copies of G while H1(X;G) is a direct sum. When does that distinction actually change the answer?

For a finite index set, direct product and direct sum coincide. They part company for infinite index sets: the sum consists of families with only finitely many non-zero entries, the product of arbitrary families. A function on the edges of an infinite graph may be non-zero on all of them, which is exactly why the cochain side gives a product.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Dualisation in general: cochain complexes and Hn(C;G)

Everything in Segment 1 was a special case of one algebraic move, and it is worth seeing why. A function from the i-simplices of X to G is the same thing as a homomorphism Δi(X) → G, because Δi(X) is free abelian with the i-simplices as a basis, and a homomorphism out of a free abelian group may be prescribed arbitrarily on a basis. So Δi(X;G) is the dual group Hom(Δi(X), G).

The relation between δ and ∂ is just as tidy. The general simplicial formula is

δφ([v0, …, vi+1]) = Σj (−1)j φ([v0, …, v̂j, …, vi+1]),

and the right-hand side is precisely φ(∂[v0, …, vi+1]). So δφ = φ∂: the operator δ sends φ to the composite Δi+1(X) → Δi(X) → G. In the language of linear algebra, δ is the dual map of ∂.

Dualising a chain complex. Let C be a chain complex of free abelian groups. Replace each chain group Cn by its dual cochain group Cn∗ = Hom(Cn, G), and each boundary map ∂ : Cn → Cn−1 by its dual coboundary map δ = ∂∗ : Cn−1∗ → Cn∗. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 191

Why does δ go the other way? For purely formal reasons, and it is worth being able to say them precisely rather than gesturing at "duality".

Lemma A (the dual homomorphism). For a homomorphism α : A → B of abelian groups, define α∗ : Hom(B,G) → Hom(A,G) by α∗(φ) = φα — that is, α∗ sends B → G to the composite A → B → G. Then (αβ)∗ = β∗α∗, and the identity and zero maps satisfy 𝟙∗ = 𝟙 and 0∗ = 0. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 191

Proof. All three are one-line checks on composites. For the first, (αβ)∗(φ) = φ(αβ) = (φα)β = β∗(φα) = β∗(α∗(φ)), where the middle step is associativity of composition. For the others, 𝟙∗(φ) = φ𝟙 = φ and 0∗(φ) = φ0 = 0. ∎

Notice that α∗ goes from Hom(B,G) to Hom(A,G) — dualising reverses the arrow, and that single fact is the whole reason cohomology is contravariant and δ raises degree. It also gives δδ = 0 for free:

δδ = ∂∗∂∗ = (∂∂)∗ = 0∗ = 0.

Cohomology of a chain complex. Since δδ = 0, the cohomology group Hn(C;G) is defined as the homology group Ker δ / Im δ at Cn∗ in the cochain complex ⋯ ← Cn+1∗ ← Cn∗ ← Cn−1∗ ← ⋯. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 191

Worked example 3: where the torsion goes

Example. Take Hatcher's opening complex, with C3, C2, C1, C0 all equal to ℤ:

0 ⟶ ℤ  0⟶  ℤ  2⟶  ℤ  0⟶  ℤ ⟶ 0,

where the labelled arrows are multiplication by 0 and by 2. Compute the homology, then the cohomology with G = ℤ.

Homology. ∂1 = 0, so H0 = ℤ. Next, Ker ∂1 = ℤ and Im ∂2 = 2ℤ, so H1 = ℤ/2ℤ = ℤ2. Then Ker ∂2 = 0 because multiplication by 2 is injective on ℤ, so H2 = 0. Finally Ker ∂3 = ℤ, so H3 = ℤ.

Dualise. Hom(ℤ, ℤ) ≅ ℤ, and under this identification the dual of multiplication by m is again multiplication by m. Reversing the arrows:

0 ⟵ ℤ  0⟵  ℤ  2⟵  ℤ  0⟵  ℤ ⟵ 0,

so as maps out of C0∗ we have δ = 0 : C0∗ → C1∗, δ = 2 : C1∗ → C2∗, δ = 0 : C2∗ → C3∗.

Cohomology. H0 = Ker(0) = ℤ. H1 = Ker(×2) / Im(0) = 0. H2 = Ker(0) / Im(×2) = ℤ/2ℤ = ℤ2. H3 = ℤ / Im(0) = ℤ.

Read the answer. The free parts stayed put, in dimensions 0 and 3; the ℤ2 moved up one dimension, from H1 to H2. Hatcher's general remark is that this is what always happens for a chain complex of finitely generated free abelian groups, since such a complex splits as a direct sum of elementary complexes 0 → ℤ → 0 and 0 → ℤ → ℤ → 0, and dualising each summand shifts its torsion up.

Worked example 4 (faded). Same complex, now with coefficients G = ℤ3.

Dual groups. Hom(ℤ, ℤ3) ≅ ℤ3, so every term of the cochain complex is ℤ3 and the maps are again multiplication by 0, 2, 0.

The one thing that changed. Multiplication by 2 on ℤ3 is ⟨your step: injective? surjective? neither?⟩, because 2 · 2 = 1 in ℤ3.

Consequences. H1(C; ℤ3) = Ker(×2)/Im(0) = ⟨your step⟩ and H2(C; ℤ3) = Ker(0)/Im(×2) = ⟨your step⟩.

Moral. The 2-torsion of this complex is completely invisible to ℤ3 coefficients, and completely visible to ℤ2 coefficients, where multiplication by 2 is the zero map and both H1 and H2 come out as ℤ2. Choosing a coefficient group is choosing what you are able to see.

Gap solutions: multiplication by 2 is an isomorphism of ℤ3; hence H1 = 0 and H2 = ℤ3/ℤ3 = 0.

How close is Hn(C;G) to Hom(Hn(C), G)?

The goal of the rest of §3.1 is to show that the cohomology groups are determined by G together with the homology groups Hn(C) alone. A first guess is that Hn(C;G) is simply Hom(Hn(C), G). Worked example 3 already refutes it: there H2 = 0, so Hom(H2(C), ℤ) = 0, while H2(C;ℤ) = ℤ2. But the guess is not far wrong, and the precise sense in which it fails is the content of the next unit.

Write Zn = Ker ∂ ⊂ Cn and Bn = Im ∂ ⊂ Cn. There is a natural map

h : Hn(C;G) → Hom(Hn(C), G)

built as follows. A class in Hn(C;G) is represented by φ : Cn → G with δφ = 0, that is φ∂ = 0, which says exactly that φ vanishes on Bn. Restricting to Zn gives φ0 = φ|Zn, which kills Bn and so induces a quotient homomorphism φ̄0 : Zn/Bn → G — an element of Hom(Hn(C), G). Send [φ] to φ̄0.

Two facts make this useful, and one lemma is doing the work in both.

Lemma B (freeness and splitting). A subgroup of a free abelian group is free. Consequently Bn−1 ⊂ Cn−1 is free, the short exact sequence 0 → Zn → Cn → Bn−1 → 0 splits, and there is a projection p : Cn → Zn restricting to the identity on Zn. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 191 (freeness of subgroups of free abelian groups is used there as known)

Why splitting follows. In 0 → Zn → Cn → Bn−1 → 0 the quotient Bn−1 is free, so choosing a preimage in Cn for each basis element of Bn−1 defines a homomorphism s : Bn−1 → Cn with ∂s = 𝟙. Then every c ∈ Cn decomposes as c = (c − s∂c) + s∂c with the first summand in Zn, and p(c) = c − s∂c is the required projection. ∎

With p in hand, any φ0 : Zn → G extends to φ0p : Cn → G, and if φ0 vanishes on Bn then so does the extension — so homomorphisms Hn(C) → G lift to cocycles. Following that lift by h simply undoes the extension, giving the identity. Hence h is surjective, and more: the sequence

0 → Ker h → Hn(C;G)  h⟶  Hom(Hn(C), G) → 0

is a split short exact sequence. Everything now turns on identifying Ker h. Hatcher's answer, which is deferred to at2-02, is that it equals Ext(Hn−1(C), G) — a group built from a free resolution of the homology one dimension down. That is the Universal Coefficient Theorem, and the reason a persistence computation over a field never has to think about any of it is that Ext vanishes for vector spaces.

Self-check 3. Why does the coboundary map δ raise degree while ∂ lowers it?

This is Lemma A and nothing more. Hatcher calls the reason "purely formal": α∗(φ) = φα needs α's source as its own target, so the direction flips. Contravariance of cohomology, the raising of degree by δ, and the eventual existence of the cup product all descend from this one reversal.

Self-check 4. In worked example 3, which statement correctly describes h in degree 2?

H2(C) = 0, so the target Hom(H2(C), ℤ) is the zero group and h must be the zero map. Since H2(C;ℤ) = ℤ2, the kernel is the whole of it. The split sequence is still perfectly true — it reads 0 → ℤ2 → ℤ2 → 0 → 0 — it is just carrying no information until Ker h is identified, which is at2-02's job.

Self-check 5. A cochain φ ∈ Cn∗ is a cocycle. What exactly does that say?

δφ = φ∂ by definition of the dual map, so δφ = 0 says the composite Cn+1 → Cn → G is zero, i.e. φ kills Im ∂ = Bn. Vanishing on cycles is a different and much stronger condition — it is what happens to a coboundary φ = δψ = ψ∂, since ∂ kills Zn. That asymmetry is what makes h well defined.

Guided proof

Prompt. Let X be a connected graph which is a tree, and let G be any abelian group. Prove that H1(X;G) = 0 — that is, prove that for every ψ ∈ Δ1(X;G) there is a φ ∈ Δ0(X;G) with δφ = ψ. Write the argument out properly, including why φ is well defined at every vertex.

Hint: fix a basepoint vertex and use the defining property of a tree — that between any two vertices there is a unique path with no repeated edge.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. Fix a vertex v0 of X and set φ(v0) = 0. Let v be any vertex. Since X is a connected tree, there is a unique path v0 = w0, w1, …, wk = v in X with no repeated edge. Define

φ(v) = Σi=1..k εi ψ(ei),

where ei is the edge joining wi−1 to wi, and εi = +1 if ei is oriented as [wi−1, wi] and εi = −1 if it is oriented as [wi, wi−1]. This is well defined precisely because the path is unique: there is no second path along which the sum could come out differently.

Now let e = [u, u′] be any edge of X. Removing e from the tree disconnects it, and the unique path from v0 to u′ is the unique path from v0 to u followed by e — or the unique path from v0 to u is the unique path to u′ followed by e traversed backwards; the two cases are symmetric, so take the first. Then by the defining formula φ(u′) = φ(u) + ψ(e), that is

δφ(e) = φ(u′) − φ(u) = ψ(e).

As e was arbitrary, δφ = ψ. Hence δ : Δ0(X;G) → Δ1(X;G) is surjective and H1(X;G) = Δ1(X;G)/Im δ = 0. ∎

Where the hypothesis was used. Connectedness gave the existence of a path; being a tree gave uniqueness, and uniqueness is what made φ well defined. On a graph with a loop the two routes round the loop can disagree, and their disagreement is exactly the class we counted in Segment 1.

Exit ritual: blank-page reconstruction

Close the page, look away, and reconstruct the argument skeleton from nothing — three load-bearing claims and why each forces the next:

  1. What δ is on a graph, what its kernel and cokernel are called, and why H0 counts components while H1 counts edges outside a maximal tree.
  2. Why the same construction works in every degree: Δi(X;G) = Hom(Δi(X),G) because Δi(X) is free, and δφ = φ∂ makes δ the dual of ∂ — from which δδ = 0 follows formally.
  3. What the map h is, why it is well defined and surjective, and the one example that shows it need not be injective.
Check your mental skeleton

If you can say: (1) δφ([v0,v1]) = φ(v1) − φ(v0), Ker δ = functions constant on components, and one copy of G per non-tree edge in the quotient; (2) free basis ⇒ functions equal homomorphisms, δ = ∂∗, and δδ = (∂∂)∗ = 0; (3) h[φ] = φ̄0, well defined because coboundaries vanish on cycles, surjective because Bn−1 is free so the sequence 0 → Zn → Cn → Bn−1 → 0 splits — and not injective, since H2 = 0 while H2 = ℤ2 in the worked example — then you have the whole of this lesson, and the only thing still missing is the name of Ker h.
