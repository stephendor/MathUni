Cohomology of spaces and cellular cohomology (at2-03)

The same CW machinery as at1-08, run backwards: transpose the boundary matrices and read cohomology off by hand.

Two units of algebra have bought us a theorem about chain complexes. Now we spend it. Attach the singular chain complex of a space, dualise, and every structural feature of homology comes back — long exact sequences, homotopy invariance, excision — with the arrows pointing the other way.

The payoff is concrete. If you computed a CW complex's cellular boundary matrices in at1-08, you already hold everything needed for its cohomology. You transpose them. That is not an analogy; it is a theorem, and the last part of this lesson proves it.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. A CW complex has cellular chain complex 0 → ℤ →2 ℤ →0 ℤ → 0 in degrees 2, 1, 0. Its boundary matrices are 1 × 1, and a 1 × 1 matrix equals its own transpose. Predict: what can possibly change when you dualise?

Correct, and this is the whole content of the transposition slogan. The numbers are unchanged; what changes is which map arrives at a given group and which leaves it. In the original complex, degree 1 has the map ×2 arriving (giving image 2ℤ) and the map 0 leaving (giving kernel ℤ), so H1 = ℤ2. After reversal, degree 1 has 0 arriving and ×2 leaving, so H1 = 0 — and the ℤ2 reappears in degree 2. Transposition is invisible in a 1 × 1 matrix; reversal never is.

Why this matters for the mission: Cellular cohomology computes H^* from a CW structure by transposing at1-08's cellular boundary maps — the finite linear algebra a persistence implementation actually performs.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cohomology of spaces

Everything so far has been algebra about an abstract chain complex C. Now take C to be the singular chain complex of a space and turn the handle.

Singular cochains. Given a space X and an abelian group G, the group Cn(X;G) of singular n-cochains with coefficients in G is the dual group Hom(Cn(X), G). An n-cochain φ assigns to each singular n-simplex σ : Δn → X a value φ(σ) ∈ G, and since the singular n-simplices form a basis for Cn(X) these values may be chosen arbitrarily — n-cochains are exactly the functions from singular n-simplices to G. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 197

Coboundary and cohomology. The coboundary map δ : Cn(X;G) → Cn+1(X;G) is the dual ∂∗, so for a singular (n+1)-simplex σ,

δφ(σ) = Σi (−1)i φ(σ | [v0, …, v̂i, …, vn+1]).

Since δ2 is the dual of ∂2 = 0, it is automatic that δ2 = 0, and Hn(X;G) is defined as Ker δ / Im δ at Cn(X;G). Elements of Ker δ are cocycles and elements of Im δ are coboundaries. — Hatcher §3.1, pp. 197–198

For a cochain φ to be a cocycle means δφ = φ∂ = 0 — φ vanishes on boundaries. That is the same sentence as in at2-01, and deliberately so: nothing about the topology has entered yet.

Because the singular chain groups Cn(X) are free, Theorem 3.2 applies verbatim and puts on topological clothing:

The universal coefficient theorem, topological form. There are split short exact sequences

0 ⟶ Ext(Hn−1(X), G) ⟶ Hn(X;G) ⟶ Hom(Hn(X), G) ⟶ 0,

describing how cohomology with arbitrary coefficients is determined purely algebraically by homology with ℤ coefficients. When the homology groups of X are finitely generated, Corollary 3.3 computes Hn(X;ℤ) from them. — Hatcher §3.1, p. 198

Two degrees come out by hand, and both are worth doing because they show what the abstract machinery is actually saying.

Worked example 1: H0 and H1 without machinery

Example. Identify H0(X;G) directly from the definitions, and then H1(X;G) from the universal coefficient sequence.

Degree 0, from the definition. Singular 0-simplices are just points of X, so a cochain in C0(X;G) is an arbitrary function φ : X → G — not necessarily continuous. For φ to be a cocycle means that for each singular 1-simplex σ : [v0,v1] → X,

δφ(σ) = φ(∂σ) = φ(σ(v1)) − φ(σ(v0)) = 0,

which says exactly that φ is constant on path-components. There are no coboundaries in degree 0, so H0(X;G) is all functions from path-components of X to G.

Degree 0, from the theorem. When n = 0 there is no Ext term, so the sequence collapses to H0(X;G) ≅ Hom(H0(X), G). Since H0(X) is free abelian on the path-components, that is the same group. The two routes agree, as they must.

Degree 1. Here the Ext term is Ext(H0(X), G), which vanishes because H0(X) is free. So H1(X;G) ≅ Hom(H1(X), G) with no correction at all. If X is path-connected, H1(X) is the abelianisation of π1(X), and since G is abelian we may identify Hom(H1(X), G) with Hom(π1(X), G).

Worked example 2 (faded). Describe the reduced group H̃0(X;G) explicitly.

Set-up. Reduced cohomology dualises the augmented chain complex ⋯ → C0(X) →ε ℤ → 0. As with homology, H̃n(X;G) = Hn(X;G) for n > 0, so only degree 0 is at issue.

What ε∗ does. The augmentation sends every singular 0-simplex σ to 1, so ε∗ sends a homomorphism φ : ℤ → G to the function σ ↦ φ(1) — that is, to a ⟨your step: what kind of function X → G?⟩. As φ(1) ranges over G, the image of ε∗ is ⟨your step: which subgroup of C0(X;G)?⟩.

Conclusion. H̃0(X;G) is the functions X → G that are constant on path-components, modulo the functions that are constant on all of X. For path-connected X this is the zero group, exactly as it should be.

Gap solutions: a constant function; the subgroup of all constant functions X → G.

Field coefficients: the correction term disappears

Take a field F for both homology and cohomology. The dual complex HomF(Cn(X;F), F) of F-module homomorphisms is the same as Hom(Cn(X), F), since both may be identified with the functions from singular n-simplices to F. In the principal-ideal-domain generalisation of the universal coefficient theorem, the ExtF terms vanish because F is a field, and one obtains isomorphisms

Hn(X;F) ≅ HomF(Hn(X;F), F).

With field coefficients, cohomology is the exact dual of homology. This is the sentence the whole of TDA leans on, and it is worth noticing how little it costs once at2-02 is done.

Everything else, dualised

The remaining structure carries over by dualising the corresponding argument for homology, and we shall need two pieces of it in Segment 2.

Relative groups and the long exact sequence of a pair. Applying Hom(−, G) to 0 → Cn(A) → Cn(X) → Cn(X,A) → 0 gives a sequence of cochain groups which is exact by a direct argument: the restriction map i∗ is surjective because a function on the singular simplices in A extends to all of X by taking the value 0 elsewhere, and its kernel is the cochains vanishing on simplices in A, which are exactly the homomorphisms Cn(X,A) → G. So relative cochains sit inside the absolute ones as a subgroup — Hatcher notes that this makes relative cohomology conceptually a little simpler than relative homology, where the relative chains are a quotient instead. Since i∗ and j∗ commute with δ, one gets the long exact sequence

⋯ → Hn(X,A;G) → Hn(X;G) → Hn(A;G) →δ Hn+1(X,A;G) → ⋯

Induced homomorphisms and contravariance. Dual to the chain maps f♯ induced by f : X → Y are cochain maps f♯ : Cn(Y;G) → Cn(X;G). The relation f♯∂ = ∂f♯ dualises to δf♯ = f♯δ, so f♯ induces f∗ : Hn(Y;G) → Hn(X;G). From (fg)♯ = g♯f♯ and 𝟙♯ = 𝟙 we get (fg)∗ = g∗f∗ and 𝟙∗ = 𝟙: cohomology is a contravariant functor, the induced maps running backwards. Homotopy invariance follows by dualising the chain homotopy: g♯ − f♯ = ∂P + P∂ becomes g♯ − f♯ = P∗δ + δP∗, so f∗ = g∗ whenever f ≃ g.

One asymmetry is worth flagging because it will not go away. The wedge axiom for cohomology involves a direct product where the homology version has a direct sum, because Hom(⊕αAα, G) ≅ ∏αHom(Aα,G). This is the same product-versus-sum phenomenon that appeared for graphs in at2-01, and it is a fact about Hom rather than a convention.

Self-check 1. Why is a 0-cochain allowed to be a discontinuous function X → G?

Cochains are algebraic objects: homomorphisms out of a free abelian group, whose basis happens to be the points of X. Freeness is exactly the licence to assign values arbitrarily. The cocycle condition then imposes something much weaker than continuity — constancy on path-components — which is why H0 sees only π0.

Self-check 2. For which n does the universal coefficient sequence give Hn(X;G) ≅ Hom(Hn(X),G) for every space X and every G?

The Ext term in degree n involves Hn−1. In degree 0 there is no such group; in degree 1 it is Ext(H0(X),G), and H0(X) is free abelian on the path-components, so that Ext vanishes. From degree 2 on, torsion one dimension down can and does contribute. Splitting is not the point — a split sequence with a non-zero left term is still not an isomorphism.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Cellular cohomology: transposing at1-08

For a CW complex X, cellular cohomology is built from the groups Hn(Xn, Xn−1; G), with cellular coboundary maps dn defined as the composites δnjn coming from the long exact sequences of the skeletal pairs. These satisfy dndn−1 = 0, because jnδn−1 = 0 — two consecutive maps in a long exact sequence.

Theorem 3.5. Hn(X;G) ≅ Ker dn / Im dn−1. Furthermore, the cellular cochain complex {Hn(Xn, Xn−1; G), dn} is isomorphic to the dual of the cellular chain complex, obtained by applying Hom(−, G). — Hatcher §3.1, Theorem 3.5, p. 203

Proof of the first statement. The universal coefficient theorem implies Hk(Xn, Xn−1; G) = 0 for k ≠ n, since Hk(Xn,Xn−1) is free and concentrated in degree n, making both outer terms of the sequence vanish. The long exact sequence of the pair (Xn, Xn−1) then gives isomorphisms Hk(Xn;G) ≅ Hk(Xn−1;G) for k ≠ n, n−1, so by induction on n we obtain Hk(Xn;G) = 0 for k > n; hence the diagonal sequences in the cellular diagram are exact. The universal coefficient theorem also gives Hk(X, Xn+1; G) = 0 for k ≤ n+1, so Hn(X;G) ≅ Hn(Xn+1;G). The diagram then yields

Hn(X;G) ≅ Hn(Xn+1;G) ≅ Ker δn ≅ Ker dn / Im δn−1 ≅ Ker dn / Im dn−1. ∎

Proof of the second statement. Consider the diagram whose top row is

Hk(Xk, Xk−1; G) ⟶ Hk(Xk; G)  δ⟶  Hk+1(Xk+1, Xk; G),

whose bottom row is the corresponding sequence of Hom groups obtained from the cellular chain complex, and whose three vertical maps are h. The cellular coboundary map is the composite across the top; the dual of the cellular boundary map is the composite across the bottom. The first and third verticals are isomorphisms by the universal coefficient theorem — the relevant homology groups being free, the Ext terms vanish and h is an isomorphism — so it suffices that the diagram commutes. The first square commutes by naturality of h, and the second by the duality between the connecting homomorphisms δ and ∂ established when the long exact sequence of a pair was set up. ∎

That second statement is the licence for the slogan. Once the cellular cochain complex is Hom(−, G) applied to the cellular chain complex, and the cellular chain groups are free with the cells as a basis, the dual of a map is represented by the transposed matrix in the dual bases. Computing cohomology is therefore a purely mechanical rerun of at1-08 with the matrices flipped.

Worked example 3: cohomology by transposition

Example. Let X be a CW complex whose cellular chain complex is

0 ⟶ ℤ  d2 = 2⟶  ℤ  d1 = 0⟶  ℤ ⟶ 0   (degrees 2, 1, 0).

Compute Hn(X;ℤ) and Hn(X;ℤ).

Homology. H0 = ℤ / Im d1 = ℤ; H1 = Ker d1 / Im d2 = ℤ / 2ℤ = ℤ2; H2 = Ker d2 = 0, multiplication by 2 being injective.

Dualise, by Theorem 3.5. The cellular cochain complex is the transpose, which for these 1 × 1 matrices means the same multipliers with the arrows reversed:

0 ⟶ ℤ  d1∗ = 0⟶  ℤ  d2∗ = 2⟶  ℤ ⟶ 0   (degrees 0, 1, 2).

Cohomology. H0 = Ker(0) = ℤ; H1 = Ker(×2) / Im(0) = 0; H2 = ℤ / Im(×2) = ℤ2.

Cross-check with Corollary 3.3. T0 = 0, T1 = ℤ2, T2 = 0, and the free parts are ℤ, 0, 0. So H0 ≅ ℤ, H1 ≅ 0 ⊕ T0 = 0, H2 ≅ 0 ⊕ T1 = ℤ2. Two independent routes, one answer — which is the practical value of having both Theorem 3.5 and Corollary 3.3 available.

Worked example 4 (faded). The same X, now with G = ℤ3.

Dual complex. Applying Hom(−, ℤ3) to each cellular chain group gives ℤ3 in degrees 0, 1, 2, with maps 0 and multiplication by 2 as before.

The one thing to decide. Multiplication by 2 on ℤ3 is ⟨your step: what kind of map?⟩.

Read off. H0(X;ℤ3) = ℤ3; H1(X;ℤ3) = Ker(×2) = ⟨your step⟩; H2(X;ℤ3) = ℤ3/Im(×2) = ⟨your step⟩.

Contrast. Over ℤ2 the map ×2 is the zero map, so H0 = H1 = H2 = ℤ2. Same space, same matrices, three different answers — the coefficient group is part of the question, not part of the apparatus.

Gap solutions: multiplication by 2 is an isomorphism of ℤ3; hence H1 = 0 and H2 = 0.

Self-check 3. In the proof of Theorem 3.5, why does the universal coefficient theorem give Hk(Xn, Xn−1; G) = 0 for k ≠ n?

The input is the homology of the skeletal pair, which is free abelian on the n-cells in degree n and zero elsewhere. Feeding that into Theorem 3.2, the Hom term vanishes for k ≠ n because Hk = 0, and the Ext term vanishes because Hk−1 is either zero or free. Nothing here is a general fact about CW pairs; it is a fact about the skeletal pairs specifically.

Self-check 4. A CW complex has cellular boundary matrix d2 = [3] and d1 = [0]. Which statement about H2(X;ℤ) is right?

This is the prediction gate again with a different number. Transposition leaves the entry 3 alone; reversal changes which side of degree 2 the map sits on. In homology, ×3 leaves degree 2, so H2 = Ker(×3) = 0. In cohomology it arrives, so H2 = ℤ/3ℤ = ℤ3. The first option is true about the map and irrelevant to the question; the third forgets to quotient.

Self-check 5. Where exactly does freeness of the cellular chain groups enter the transposition slogan?

A homomorphism has a matrix only relative to chosen bases, and its dual has the transposed matrix only relative to the dual bases — both of which require freeness of finite rank. The relation dndn−1 = 0 comes from exactness of the long exact sequences (jnδn−1 = 0) and holds regardless.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove that for every space X and every abelian group G there is an isomorphism H1(X;G) ≅ Hom(H1(X), G), and that when X is path-connected this may be identified with Hom(π1(X), G). Say exactly where each hypothesis is used, and why the corresponding statement in degree 2 can fail.

Hint: the universal coefficient sequence in degree 1 has an Ext term you can evaluate; and a homomorphism from a group to an abelian group factors through the abelianisation.

Click to reveal model answer

Model answer. By the universal coefficient theorem there is a split short exact sequence

0 ⟶ Ext(H0(X), G) ⟶ H1(X;G)  h⟶  Hom(H1(X), G) ⟶ 0.

Now H0(X) is free abelian, with basis the path-components of X. By the second of the three Ext rules, Ext(H,G) = 0 whenever H is free, so the left-hand term vanishes and h is an isomorphism H1(X;G) ≅ Hom(H1(X), G).

Suppose further that X is path-connected. Then H1(X) is the abelianisation of π1(X), say H1(X) = π1(X)ab with quotient map q : π1(X) → H1(X). Since G is abelian, every homomorphism π1(X) → G kills every commutator and hence factors uniquely through q; conversely composition with q turns any homomorphism H1(X) → G into one on π1(X). These two constructions are mutually inverse, so Hom(H1(X),G) ≅ Hom(π1(X),G), and combining with the isomorphism above gives H1(X;G) ≅ Hom(π1(X),G). ∎

Where the hypotheses were used, and why degree 2 differs. Freeness of H0(X) — which holds for every space — is what kills the Ext term; commutativity of G is what makes homomorphisms out of π1(X) factor through the abelianisation; path-connectedness is what makes H1(X) the abelianisation of a single fundamental group. In degree 2 the Ext term is Ext(H1(X),G), and H1(X) may perfectly well have torsion, in which case that term is non-zero and h is not injective: worked example 3 is exactly such a case, with H1 = ℤ2 and H2(X;ℤ) = ℤ2 mapping to Hom(H2(X),ℤ) = 0.

Exit ritual: blank-page reconstruction

Close the page, look away, and reconstruct the argument skeleton — three load-bearing claims and why each forces the next:

  1. What a singular cochain is, why it may be any function on singular simplices, and the two degrees in which Hn(X;G) is simply Hom(Hn(X),G).
  2. Which structural features of homology survive dualisation, and the one place the answer changes shape — direct product in place of direct sum.
  3. Both halves of Theorem 3.5, and precisely which half licenses "transpose the boundary matrices".
Check your mental skeleton

If you can say: (1) Cn(X;G) = Hom(Cn(X),G) with Cn(X) free on the singular simplices; H0 = functions on path-components = Hom(H0,G); H1 ≅ Hom(H1,G) because Ext(H0,G) = 0; (2) long exact sequence of a pair, contravariant induced maps, homotopy invariance by dualising the chain homotopy — but wedge sums give a product because Hom takes sums to products; (3) Hn(X;G) ≅ Ker dn/Im dn−1, and the cellular cochain complex is Hom(−,G) of the cellular chain complex — it is the second half, plus freeness giving dual bases, that turns cohomology into a transposition — then you have the lesson.
