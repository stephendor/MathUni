Cover, nerve, and multiscale Mapper (tda2-07)

In lab-08 you ran Mapper and it drew you a graph. Here is the awkward question: which theorem says that graph is not lying?

The obvious candidate is the Nerve Theorem — cover a space so that every intersection of cover elements is contractible, and the nerve of the cover is homotopy equivalent to the space. That is a magnificent guarantee. It is also, for a Mapper cover, essentially never available: the cover you actually get is the pullback of intervals through your lens function, and nobody checked that its intersections are contractible. Dey and Wang say so in the second paragraph of the chapter and then spend two sections working out what survives.

What survives is one-sided, and it is enough to make Mapper honest: every loop in the picture came from a loop in the data. Loops can die on the way up; they cannot be born.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. A Mapper graph, built from a pullback cover with no contractibility hypothesis whatsoever. Predict which statement about H1 is actually provable.

Correct, and the surjection is Theorem 9.5, upgraded to maps between nerves by Theorem 9.9. The hypothesis it needs is not contractibility but mere path connectedness of the cover elements — and a pullback cover consists of path connected components by construction, so Mapper gets it for free. The first option is what the Nerve Theorem would give if you had a good cover, and Dey and Wang exhibit a map from the sphere whose pullback nerve fails even surjectivity in H2. The third option overcorrects: it is exactly the surjection that stops Mapper inventing features, which is the guarantee a practitioner most needs.

Why this matters for the mission: The nerve theorem says that for a good cover the nerve is homotopy equivalent to the covered space — the guarantee lab-08's Mapper output was implicitly leaning on. Multiscale Mapper makes the cover a parameter and recovers stability in tda1-06's interleaving distance, turning a heuristic visualisation into a summary with an error bar.

Notation. Following Dey and Wang, 𝓤, 𝓥, 𝓦 denote covers and 𝐔, 𝐕, 𝐖 denote towers of covers. (Dey and Wang set towers in fraktur; the letters differ, the objects do not.) N(𝓤) is the nerve, and |N(𝓤)| its geometric realisation.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Covers, nerves, and the guarantee you actually have

Let X be a path connected topological space and 𝓤 = {Uα}α∈A an open cover. Dey and Wang assume throughout §9.1 that each cover element is path connected — not required in general, but free in the application to come.

Map of covers. For covers 𝓤 = {Uα}α∈A and 𝓥 = {Vβ}β∈B of the same space X, a map of covers from 𝓤 to 𝓥 is a set map ξ : A → B such that Uα ⊆ Vξ(α) for every α ∈ A. — Dey & Wang §9.1, p. 257

Proposition 9.1. Given a map of covers ξ : 𝓤 → 𝓥, there is an induced simplicial map N(ξ) : N(𝓤) → N(𝓥) given on vertices by the map ξ. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Proposition 9.1, p. 257

Proof. Take σ ∈ N(𝓤). Then

⋂β∈ξ(σ) Vβ = ⋂α∈σ Vξ(α) ⊇ ⋂α∈σ Uα ≠ ∅,

the last step because σ is a simplex of N(𝓤). So ξ(σ) spans a simplex of N(𝓥). ∎

Proposition 9.2 (induced maps are contiguous). Let ζ, ξ : 𝓤 → 𝓥 be any two maps of covers. Then the simplicial maps N(ζ) and N(ξ) are contiguous. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Proposition 9.2, p. 258

Proof. For every α we have both Uα ⊆ Vζ(α) and Uα ⊆ Vξ(α), hence Uα ⊆ Vζ(α) ∩ Vξ(α). So for σ ∈ N(𝓤),

⋂β∈ζ(σ)∪ξ(σ) Vβ = ⋂α∈σ (Vζ(α) ∩ Vξ(α)) ⊇ ⋂α∈σ Uα ≠ ∅,

so ζ(σ) ∪ ξ(σ) spans a simplex of N(𝓥). ∎

Contiguous maps induce identical maps on homology (Dey and Wang's Fact 2.11), so the homomorphism H∗(N(𝓤)) → H∗(N(𝓥)) does not depend on which map of covers you chose — it depends only on the pair of covers. Dey and Wang call it canonical for that reason. Keep this proposition in view: it is what will make interleaving of cover towers, which requires nothing to commute, produce interleaving of simplicial towers, which does.

The Nerve Theorem, as quoted here (Theorem 2.1). If every intersection of cover elements in a cover 𝓤 is contractible, then the nerve N(𝓤) is homotopy equivalent to the space X = ⋃𝓤; more precisely the nerve map φ𝓤 is a homotopy equivalence and φ𝓤∗ an isomorphism between H∗(X) and H∗(N(𝓤)). — Dey & Wang, quoted in the chapter opening and again in §9.1, pp. 256 and 259; the theorem itself is Chapter 2's, not this chapter's

Dey and Wang add two remarks in the same breath, and they set the agenda. First, contractibility may be weakened to a homology-ball condition and the isomorphism survives. Second, and decisively: "we cannot hope for such a good cover all the time and need to investigate what happens if the cover is not good." Without it, φ𝓤∗ need be neither injective nor surjective — their Figure 9.3 has a map 𝕊2 → ℝ2 whose pullback of one cover element is a band around the equator, pinching the nerve in the middle into two 2-cycles, so surjectivity fails in H2.

The apparatus: blow-up space and partitions of unity

To say anything at all one needs an actual map from X to the nerve. Hatcher's proof of the Nerve Theorem routes through a blow-up space X𝓤, and Dey and Wang reuse the construction without the contractibility hypothesis. For each nonempty finite intersection Uα0…αn = ⋂iUαi take the product Uα0…αn × Δnα0…αn with the n-simplex on the corresponding vertices, take the disjoint union over all such tuples, and glue along the face identifications coming from the inclusions Uα0…αn ⊂ Uα0…α̂i…αn. The quotient is X𝓤, and there are maps ζ : X → X𝓤, π : X𝓤 → |N(𝓤)| with φ𝓤 = π ∘ ζ the nerve map.

Definition 9.1 (locally finite). An open cover {Uα, α ∈ A} of X is a refinement of another open cover {Vβ, β ∈ B} if every Uα is contained in some Vβ. 𝓤 is locally finite if every x ∈ X has a neighbourhood contained in finitely many elements of 𝓤. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Definition 9.1, p. 260

Definition 9.2 (partition of unity). A collection of continuous φα : X → [0,1], α ∈ A, is a partition of unity if (i) Σα∈A φα(x) = 1 for all x ∈ X, and (ii) for every x only finitely many α have φα(x) > 0. It is subordinate to 𝓤 if supp(φα) ⊆ Uα for each α. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Definition 9.2, p. 260

Fact 9.1. For any open cover 𝓤 = {Uα, α ∈ A} of a compact space X, there exists a partition of unity {φα, α ∈ A} subordinate to 𝓤. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Fact 9.1, p. 261

This is the whole reason X is assumed compact. With a subordinate partition of unity in hand one defines ζ(x) := Σα φα(x)xα, lets π be induced by the projections onto the simplex factors, and obtains the nerve map in barycentric coordinates:

φ𝓤(x) = Σα∈A φα(x) uα.

Fact 9.2. The map ζ is a homotopy equivalence. — Dey & Wang §9.1, Fact 9.2, p. 261

Note what Fact 9.2 does and does not say. Under the standing assumption that X is compact, the map from X into the blow-up is a homotopy equivalence whatever the cover; it is π, the collapse of the blow-up onto the nerve, that a good cover is needed to control. All the loss happens there.

Worked example 1: what a path connected cover buys, in full

Step 1 — reduce to simplicial cycles. Proposition 9.3: every 1-cycle γ in |N(𝓤)| has a 1-cycle γ′ in N(𝓤) with [γ] = [|γ′|]. So a class of the realisation may always be represented in the 1-skeleton of the nerve itself.

Step 2 — lift the cycle. Proposition 9.4: if 𝓤 is path connected then φ𝓤∗ : H1(X) → H1(|N(𝓤)|) is a surjection. The construction is concrete. Given an edge cycle e0, e1, …, er−1 of γ′, with ei spanned by uαi and uαi+1, choose a point xi in the nonempty intersection Uαi ∩ Uαi+1. The edge path ẽi = ei × xi lies in X𝓤, and consecutive lifts are joined by a vertex path xαi ⇝ xαi+1 running inside the lifted Uαi. Concatenating gives a cycle γ𝓤 in X𝓤 with π(γ𝓤) = γ.

Step 3 — where path connectedness is used, and only there. Exactly one step needs it: the vertex path inside the lifted Uαi exists because Uαi is path connected. Everything else is formal — ζ∗ is an isomorphism by Fact 9.2, so some [γX] ∈ H1(X) has ζ∗[γX] = [γ𝓤], and φ𝓤∗ = π∗ ∘ ζ∗ gives φ𝓤∗[γX] = [γ].

Step 4 — move to simplicial homology. Composing with the isomorphism ι𝓤 : Hp(|N(𝓤)|) → Hp(N(𝓤)) gives φ̄𝓤∗ : H1(X) → H1(N(𝓤)), and Theorem 9.5: if 𝓤 is path connected then φ̄𝓤∗ is a surjection.

Step 5 — upgrade to maps between nerves. Proposition 9.6 supplies coherent partitions of unity: given a cover map θ : 𝓤 → 𝓥 on a compact X there is a partition of unity {φα} subordinate to 𝓤 whose pushforward ψβ = Σα∈θ−1(β) φα (and 0 off the image of θ) is subordinate to 𝓥. Claim 9.1 then gives τ̂(φ𝓤(x)) = φ𝓥(x) for the linear extension τ̂ of the induced simplicial map, whence Corollary 9.7: φ𝓥∗ = τ̂∗ ∘ φ𝓤∗; and passing to simplicial homology, Proposition 9.8: φ̄𝓥∗ = τ∗ ∘ φ̄𝓤∗.

Step 6 — the conclusion. Theorem 9.9: if τ : N(𝓤) → N(𝓥) is induced by a cover map between path connected covers, then τ∗ : H1(N(𝓤)) → H1(N(𝓥)) is a surjection. In persistence language: along a tower of nerves connected by cover maps, one-dimensional classes may die, but they cannot be born. That is the guarantee the prediction gate was about, and it is the one Mapper actually has.

Visual: the circle, its cover, and the nerve that coarsens

The button shows the mapper of one fixed map under successively coarser covers. With three or more intervals the nerve is a cycle — Dey and Wang's Figure 9.7 is exactly this situation, a map from a circle to a subset of ℝ whose mapper they describe as quadrangle-shaped — and once the cover is coarse enough it collapses to an edge and then a point. Two cautions on reading the picture. It is a sequence of separate covers, not yet a tower: linking them by cover maps, so that each refines into the next, is Definition 9.5's job and the subject of Segment 2. And Theorem 9.9 is what turns "no loop ever appears out of nowhere" from an observation about this animation into a theorem about any such tower. What the picture does make vivid is the complaint §9.3.1 answers: the output depends on a parameter you chose.

Self-check 1. Why does Proposition 9.2 matter beyond tidiness?

The payoff arrives in §9.4: Definition 9.7 asks nothing to commute at the level of covers, and Proposition 9.2 is exactly what repairs the gap, since two composites that are both maps of covers with the same source and target induce contiguous — hence homologically identical — simplicial maps. Functoriality is a separate observation, recorded as N(ζ ∘ ξ) = N(ζ) ∘ N(ξ). And contiguity is strictly weaker than equality of maps; it gives equality only after applying homology.

Self-check 2. Where in the proof of Proposition 9.4 is path connectedness of the cover used?

The lifted cycle alternates edge paths ei × xi with vertex paths xαi ⇝ xαi+1, and the latter must run inside the lifted Uαi — which requires that cover element to be path connected. Proposition 9.3 is the singular-to-simplicial transition and holds for any cover; Fact 9.2 is quoted for compact X and makes no path connectedness demand on the cover elements. This is worth pinning down, because it is the single hypothesis Mapper will supply for free.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Mapper, and making the cover a parameter

Now let the cover of X come from somewhere. Given a continuous f : X → Z and an open cover 𝓤 = {Uα} of Z, the sets f−1(Uα) cover X. Decompose each into path connected components, f−1(Uα) = ⋃i=1jα Vα,i, and call the resulting collection the pullback cover f∗𝓤. Every element of it is a path connected component of something, hence path connected — so Theorem 9.5 and Theorem 9.9 apply, with no hypothesis on f or 𝓤 beyond the next one.

The exception to guard against: some f shatter a pre-image into infinitely many path components. Call f well behaved if for every path connected open U ⊆ Z the pre-image f−1(U) has finitely many open path connected components; then all nerves of pullbacks of finite covers are finite.

Definition 9.4 (mapper). Let X and Z be topological spaces and f : X → Z a well-behaved continuous map. Let 𝓤 = {Uα}α∈A be a finite open cover of Z. The mapper arising from these data is the nerve of the pullback cover f∗(𝓤) of X; that is, M(𝓤, f) := N(f∗(𝓤)). — Dey & Wang §9.3, Definition 9.4, p. 269

Dey and Wang's Remark 9.1 is the reason tda2-06 was the right unit to come before this one. Allow 𝓤 infinite and take f : X → ℝ. Choosing Uα = (−∞, α) for α ∈ ℝ gives sublevel sets, which lead to merge trees — tda2-06's Definition 7.2. Choosing Uα = (α − ε, α + ε) for fixed ε > 0 gives ε-thick level sets, which induce a relaxed notion of Reeb graph — tda2-06's Definition 7.1. Mapper is the same collapse with the cover promoted to an input.

For data the construction is entirely finite. Given a finite metric space (P, dP) and r ≥ 0, build the graph Gr(P) with vertex set P and an edge (p, p′) whenever dP(p, p′) ≤ r. For f : P → ℝ and a set 𝓤 of intervals covering ℝ, put P(a,b) = f−1((a,b)) and split it into the vertex sets of the connected components of the subgraph of Gr(P) spanned by P(a,b). These sets over all intervals form a cover of P, and its nerve is the mapper M(P, f), intersections being intersections of finite sets. Taking intervals Ui = (i − ε, i + ε) with ε ∈ (0,1), so only consecutive ones overlap, gives a discretised approximation of the Reeb graph of the function f approximates; in the limit as each interval degenerates to a point, the discretised Reeb graph converges to the Reeb graph.

Worked example 2 (faded): the cover becomes an index

The complaint. M(𝓤, f) is one snapshot at one scale, the scale of 𝓤. Fix that by replacing the cover with a tower of covers 𝐔 = {𝓤a}, coarsening as the index grows.

Step 1 — pullbacks respect cover maps. Proposition 9.13: for f : X → Z and covers 𝓤, 𝓥 of Z with a cover map ξ, there is an induced cover map f∗(ξ) : f∗(𝓤) → f∗(𝓥). The proof is one observation: U ⊆ V gives f−1(U) ⊆ f−1(V), and each path connected component of f−1(U) lies in ⟨your step: how many?⟩ component(s) of f−1(V) — because a connected set cannot straddle two components. Send it there.

Step 2 — hence towers pull back. Composition is respected, f∗(ζ ∘ ξ) = f∗(ζ) ∘ f∗(ξ), so applying Step 1 all along a tower gives Proposition 9.14: if 𝐔 is a cover tower of Z and f is continuous, f∗(𝐔) is a cover tower of X.

Step 3 — take nerves. Definition 9.5: the multiscale mapper is the simplicial tower MM(𝐔, f) := N(f∗(𝐔)), the simplicial maps being those induced by the cover maps — Proposition 9.1 supplies them. For indices a1 < ⋯ < an above the resolution this reads N(f∗(𝓤a1)) → ⋯ → N(f∗(𝓤an)).

Step 4 — apply homology. With field coefficients one gets Hp(N(f∗(𝓤a1))) → ⋯ → Hp(N(f∗(𝓤an))). The covers are finite and f is well behaved, so every nerve is finite and every term is ⟨your step: what property of the module?⟩ — hence there is a persistence diagram DgmpMM(𝐔, f), a topological summary of f through the lens 𝐔.

Gap solutions: exactly one component; and the module is pointwise finite dimensional.

Definition 9.6 (pullback metric). Given a metric space (Z, dZ), define the pseudometric df on X by df(x, x′) := infγ∈ΓX(x,x′) diamZ(f ∘ γ), the infimum over continuous paths γ from x to x′ in X. — Dey & Wang §9.3.2, Definition 9.6, p. 273

This is the size notion Segment 3 needs, and it is the exact analogue of tda2-06's function-induced metric with a general codomain in place of ℝ. Proposition 9.15 records that it behaves: if 𝓤′ is the restriction of 𝓤 to f(X), then the pullback cover has the same Lebesgue number, λ(f∗𝓤) = λ(𝓤′).

Self-check 3. Why does a mapper automatically satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem 9.5?

The whole force of §9.1 is that it demands nothing but path connectedness, and the pullback cover is built by splitting pre-images into path components — so it is path connected by definition, not by assumption. Openness and compactness are used elsewhere, for Fact 9.1; they are not what Theorem 9.5 keys on. The third option is the good-cover hypothesis, and the chapter exists because it is not available.

Self-check 4. Remark 9.1 recovers merge trees and a relaxed Reeb graph as mappers. What does that tell you about the role of the cover?

Remark 9.1's point is that varying 𝓤 varies which summary you build from the same f — which is precisely why leaving the cover unexamined, as a fixed heuristic choice, is unsatisfactory. The second option overstates: thickened level sets give a relaxed notion, and Dey and Wang record the exact Reeb graph only as a limit as the intervals degenerate to points. The third is invented; Remark 9.1 assumes nothing about X being simplicial.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Stability: where the error bar comes from

Definition 9.7 (interleaving of cover towers). Let 𝐔 = {𝓤a} and 𝐕 = {𝓥a} be cover towers of a topological space X with res(𝐔) = res(𝐕) = r. Given η ≥ 0, 𝐔 and 𝐕 are η-interleaved if one can find cover maps ζa : 𝓤a → 𝓥a+η and ξa′ : 𝓥a′ → 𝓤a′+η for all a, a′ ≥ r. Replacing "+" by "·" gives multiplicatively η-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.1, Definition 9.7, p. 275

Nothing is required to commute — which is the whole subtlety, since interleaving of simplicial towers does require it. Proposition 9.2 pays the debt, as promised in Segment 1: the composite ξa+η ∘ ζa and the tower map ua,a+2η are both maps of covers from 𝓤a to 𝓤a+2η, so the simplicial maps they induce are contiguous, hence identical on homology, which is all the simplicial definition actually needs.

Proposition 9.17. (a) If 𝐔 and 𝐕 are (multiplicatively) η1-interleaved and 𝐕 and 𝐖 are (multiplicatively) η2-interleaved, then 𝐔 and 𝐖 are (multiplicatively η1η2-) (η1 + η2)-interleaved. (b) If f : X → Z is continuous and 𝐔, 𝐕 are (multiplicatively) η-interleaved cover towers of Z, then f∗(𝐔) and f∗(𝐕) are also (multiplicatively) η-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.1, Proposition 9.17, p. 275

Proposition 9.18. Let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be two (multiplicatively) η-interleaved cover towers of X with res(𝐔) = res(𝐕). Then N(𝐔) and N(𝐕) are also (multiplicatively) η-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.1, Proposition 9.18, p. 275

Corollary 9.19. For η ≥ 0, let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be two finite cover towers of Z with res(𝐔) = res(𝐕) > 0. Let f : X → Z be well behaved and let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be η-interleaved. Then MM(𝐔, f) and MM(𝐕, f) are η-interleaved. In particular, the bottleneck distance between the persistence diagrams DgmpMM(𝐔, f) and DgmpMM(𝐕, f) is at most η for all p ∈ ℕ. — Dey & Wang §9.4.1, Corollary 9.19, p. 276

That is the first error bar, and it is worth reading its scope precisely: f is held fixed and only the lens is perturbed. Dey and Wang say at once that the diagram is not necessarily stable under perturbations of f, and introduce a restricted family of covers to fix it.

Definition 9.8 ((c,s)-good cover tower). Given a cover tower 𝐔 = {𝓤ε}ε≥s>0, it is (c,s)-good if for any ε ≥ s > 0, (i) smax(𝓤ε) ≤ ε and (ii) λ(𝓤cε) ≥ ε. — Dey & Wang §9.4.2, Definition 9.8, p. 277

Two notions here are §9.2's, and Definition 9.8 does not parse without them. By Definition 9.3 the size s(X′) of a subset of a pseudometric space is its diameter, the size of a homology class is the infimum of the sizes of its cycles, and a cycle basis is optimal when the sum of the sizes of its cycles is minimal. From these, smax(𝓤) := maxU∈𝓤 s(U) is the maximum cover size, and the Lebesgue number λ(𝓤) is the largest real such that any subset of size at most λ(𝓤) lies inside at least one element of 𝓤. So (i) says cover elements at scale ε are no bigger than ε, and (ii) says that scaling the index up by c buys a cover coarse enough to swallow anything of size ε. Dey and Wang's example: 𝓤ε := {Bε/2(z) | z ∈ Z} is a (2,s)-good cover tower.

Theorem 9.20. Given a map f : X → Z, let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be two (c,s)-good cover towers of Z. Then the corresponding multiscale mappers MM(𝐔, f) and MM(𝐕, f) are multiplicatively c-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.2, Theorem 9.20, p. 277

The content is Claim 9.2 — any two (c,s)-good cover towers are multiplicatively c-interleaved — and the guided proof below asks you to produce it. The reading Dey and Wang draw is the important one: the persistence diagrams induced by any two (c,s)-good cover towers contain roughly the same information, so the arbitrariness of the analyst's cover has been bounded rather than merely deplored.

Definition 9.9 (truncation). Given a tower of covers 𝐔 = {𝓤ε} and ε0 ≥ res(𝐔), the ε0-truncation of 𝐔 is Trε0(𝐔) := {𝓤ε}ε0≤ε, of resolution ε0. — Dey & Wang §9.4.2, Definition 9.9, p. 277

Proposition 9.21. Let X be compact, (Z, dZ) a compact path connected metric space, and f, g : X → Z continuous with δ = maxx∈X dZ(f(x), g(x)). Let 𝐖 be any (c,s)-good cover tower of Z and ε0 = max(1, s). Then the ε0-truncations of f∗(𝐖) and g∗(𝐖) are multiplicatively (2c·max(δ,s) + c)-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.2, Proposition 9.21, pp. 277–278

Corollary 9.22. Let 𝐖 be a (c,s)-good cover tower of the compact connected metric space Z and f, g : X → Z any two well-behaved continuous functions with maxx∈X dZ(f(x), g(x)) = δ. Then

db(DgmlogMM(𝐖, f), DgmlogMM(𝐖, g)) ≤ log(2c·max(s,δ) + c) + max(0, log(1/s)).

— Dey & Wang §9.4.2, Corollary 9.22, p. 278

The second term is the price of the truncation, and its provenance is worth knowing rather than memorising. Proposition 9.21 controls the two towers only from index max(1, s) upwards. When s < 1 the missing initial stretch can do two things at log scale: a class born in [s, 1) either dies at or before index 1, contributing a bar of length at most log(1/s), or survives to index 1 and merely stretches an existing bar leftwards by at most log(1/s). Either way the bottleneck distance moves by at most that.

Definition 9.10 (intrinsic Čech complex). Given a (pseudo)metric space (Y, dY), its intrinsic Čech complex ℂr(Y) at scale r is the nerve complex of the set of intrinsic r-balls {B(y; r)}y∈Y. — Dey & Wang §9.4.3, Definition 9.10, p. 280

Definition 9.11 (intrinsic Čech filtration). The intrinsic Čech filtration of (Y, dY) is 𝓒(Y) = {ℂr(Y) ↪ ℂr′(Y)}0<r<r′; at resolution s it is 𝓒s(Y) = {ℂr(Y) ↪ ℂr′(Y)}s≤r<r′. — Dey & Wang §9.4.3, Definition 9.11, p. 280

Theorem 9.23. Let 𝓒s(X) be the intrinsic Čech filtration of (X, df) starting with resolution s, and 𝐔 a (c,s)-good cover tower of the compact connected metric space Z. Then MM(𝐔, f) and 𝓒s(X) are multiplicatively 2c-interleaved. — Dey & Wang §9.4.3, Theorem 9.23, p. 280

Corollary 9.24. Given a continuous f : X → Z and a (c,s)-good cover tower 𝐔 of Z, db(DgmlogMM(𝐔, f), Dgmlog𝓒s) ≤ 2c. — Dey & Wang §9.4.3, Corollary 9.24, p. 280

The comparison is with an object you might reasonably have built instead. Given the pullback pseudometric df of Definition 9.6 you could skip the nerve of the pullback covers entirely and take the intrinsic Čech filtration of (X, df), which is connected by inclusions rather than by simplicial maps and is easier to handle computationally. Theorem 9.23 says the two are within a factor 2c of each other, so nothing essential turns on the choice.

Paying only for the 1-skeleton

Computing a pullback cover exactly means shattering f−1(W) into connected components, which for a general map produces partial edges, partial triangles and worse. §9.6 sidesteps that by insisting each component consist only of vertices.

Definition 9.13 (G-induced connected component). Given a graph G and a set of vertices O ⊆ V(G), the set of connected components of O induced by G, denoted ccG(O), is the partition of O into maximal subsets of vertices connected in the subgraph GO spanned by O. — Dey & Wang §9.6.1, Definition 9.13, p. 284

Definition 9.14 (G-induced multiscale mapper). The G-induced mapper is the nerve complex MG(𝓦, f) := N(f∗G(𝓦)), and for a cover tower 𝐖 of Z the G-induced multiscale mapper MMG(𝐖, f) is the tower of these nerves. Taking G = K1, the 1-skeleton of a simplicial complex K, and fV the restriction of f to V(K), gives the combinatorial multiscale mapper. — Dey & Wang §9.6.1, Definition 9.14, p. 285

Theorem 9.28. Assume (Z, dZ) is a compact and connected metric space. Given f : |K| → Z with fV its restriction to V(K), and a (c,s)-good cover tower 𝐖 of Z such that (K, f, 𝐖) satisfies the minimum diameter condition (Condition 9.1), the bottleneck distance between DgmlogMM(𝐖, f) and DgmlogMMK1(𝐖, fV) is at most 3·log(3c) + 3·max(0, log(1/s)). — Dey & Wang §9.6.2, Theorem 9.28, p. 286

Condition 9.1 belongs to §9.5: writing κ(𝐖) for the minimum diameter of any element of any cover in the tower, the triple (K, f, 𝐖) satisfies the minimum diameter condition if diam(f(σ)) ≤ κ(𝐖) for every simplex σ ∈ K — for a real-valued PL f, simply |f(u) − f(v)| ≤ κ(𝐖) on every edge. The factor 3 in the bound is not decoration either: the two towers interleave only in the weak sense of Definition 6.6, from a chapter this unit does not read, and that weakening costs exactly a factor of three. Algorithm 17, MMAPPER, is the resulting procedure: for each cover in the tower, collect VW ⊆ V(K) with f(VW) = f(V(K)) ∩ W, split it by ccK1, build the nerve, then compute the diagram of the resulting filtration.

What the mission strip claims, and what §9.1–9.6 establish. The strip calls the nerve theorem "the guarantee lab-08's Mapper output was implicitly leaning on". That is the one thing this chapter is at pains to deny. Dey and Wang quote the Nerve Theorem from Theorem 2.1 — a result of Chapter 2, not proved here — and immediately write that we cannot hope for such a good cover all the time; nothing establishes that a Mapper pullback cover has contractible intersections, and Figure 9.3 exhibits a pullback nerve failing even surjectivity in H2. What Mapper's output actually leans on is weaker and provable: the pullback cover is path connected by construction, so Theorem 9.5 and Theorem 9.9 give surjectivity in H1 — loops in the picture come from loops in the data, and loops in the data may vanish. Everything in the strip's second sentence does hold: Definition 9.5 makes the cover a parameter and Corollaries 9.19, 9.22 and 9.24 are the error bars, in bottleneck distance, obtained from interleaving.

The pattern to carry out of this unit. Every bound after Corollary 9.19 is conditional on the cover tower being (c,s)-good. That is a property of an object the analyst designs, not of the data — balls of radius ε/2 give c = 2 — so unlike a noise model it can actually be checked, and c is then the error bar. Perturb the lens and Corollary 9.19 bounds the bottleneck distance by the interleaving parameter η; perturb the map by δ in sup-norm and Corollary 9.22 bounds it by log(2c·max(s,δ) + c) + max(0, log(1/s)); compare against the Čech filtration you might have built instead and Corollary 9.24 bounds it by 2c; compute on the 1-skeleton only and Theorem 9.28 costs a factor of 3 plus Condition 9.1. A Mapper picture reported without naming which of these applies is still a heuristic.

Self-check 5. Corollary 9.19 holds for arbitrary interleaved cover towers, but Corollary 9.22 requires the tower to be (c,s)-good. What is the difference in what is being perturbed?

Dey and Wang state the split explicitly: the diagram is stable under perturbation of the covers, but not necessarily under perturbation of the map, and (c,s)-good covers are introduced to address exactly that. The additive-versus-multiplicative distinction is real but secondary — it is why Corollary 9.22 is stated at log scale. The third option is invented: bottleneck distance is defined for the diagrams of any pointwise finite dimensional module here.

Self-check 6. Why does Theorem 9.28 carry a factor of 3 that Theorem 9.23 does not?

Dey and Wang say it plainly: the towers arising here interleave not in the strong sense but in a weaker one, and this limitation worsens the approximation by a factor of 3. The first option confuses a size saving with an error bound — the 1-skeleton being smaller is the benefit, not the cost. The third misreads Condition 9.1, which is a single requirement, diam(f(σ)) ≤ κ(𝐖) for every simplex.

(1) Theorem 9.9. Let τ : N(𝓤) → N(𝓥) be induced by a cover map 𝓤 → 𝓥 with both covers path connected. Consider

H1(X) → φ̄𝓤∗ H1(N(𝓤)) → τ∗ H1(N(𝓥)) and H1(X) → φ̄𝓥∗ H1(N(𝓥)).

By Proposition 9.8, τ∗ ∘ φ̄𝓤∗ = φ̄𝓥∗. Since 𝓥 is path connected, Theorem 9.5 says φ̄𝓥∗ is surjective. Now take any h ∈ H1(N(𝓥)); surjectivity gives some c ∈ H1(X) with φ̄𝓥∗(c) = h, and then τ∗(φ̄𝓤∗(c)) = h, so h is in the image of τ∗. Hence τ∗ is surjective. ∎

What it says: along a tower of nerves whose maps come from cover maps between path connected covers, every class of a later nerve is the image of a class of the earlier one. In persistence terms, a one-dimensional class can die as the cover coarsens, but no class is ever born. Note the direction — this is not stability, and it says nothing about how much dies; it says that whatever you see was there.

(2) Claim 9.2. Let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be (c,s)-good cover towers. Fix ε ≥ s and take any U ∈ 𝓤ε. By clause (i) of Definition 9.8 for 𝐔, smax(𝓤ε) ≤ ε, so diam(U) ≤ ε. By clause (ii) for 𝐕, λ(𝓥cε) ≥ ε, and by the definition of the Lebesgue number any set of size at most λ(𝓥cε) lies inside some element of 𝓥cε. Since size is diameter, U qualifies, so there is V ∈ 𝓥cε with U ⊆ V; set ζε(U) = V, choosing arbitrarily if several V work. Doing this for every U ∈ 𝓤ε gives a map of covers ζε : 𝓤ε → 𝓥cε, since a map of covers is exactly an assignment with U ⊆ Vζ(U). The same argument with the roles of 𝐔 and 𝐕 exchanged gives ξε′ : 𝓥ε′ → 𝓤cε′. By Definition 9.7 in its multiplicative form, 𝐔 and 𝐕 are multiplicatively c-interleaved. ∎

(3) Theorem 9.20. Let 𝐔 and 𝐕 be (c,s)-good cover towers of Z and f : X → Z. By (2) they are multiplicatively c-interleaved as cover towers of Z. Apply Proposition 9.17(b), which says pullback along a continuous map preserves interleaving: f∗(𝐔) and f∗(𝐕) are multiplicatively c-interleaved cover towers of X. Now apply Proposition 9.18, which passes interleaving of cover towers to interleaving of their nerve towers: N(f∗(𝐔)) and N(f∗(𝐕)) are multiplicatively c-interleaved. By Definition 9.5 those are precisely MM(𝐔, f) and MM(𝐕, f). ∎

What this does and does not license. The chain uses Proposition 9.17(b) only; part (a), which composes two interleavings, is what one needs when chaining perturbations of the cover with perturbations of the map. Note also what is not concluded: the two multiscale mappers are not isomorphic and their diagrams are not equal — Theorem 9.20 bounds their discrepancy by the single constant c of the goodness hypothesis, and Dey and Wang's gloss, that any two (c,s)-good towers carry roughly the same information, should be read with that constant attached.
