Sparsification and approximate persistence (tda2-08)

Fifteen thousand points from a natural-image dataset. The 2-skeleton of their Rips complex has 1 329 672 867 simplices. Now compute its persistence.

That number is Dey and Wang's, measured on the primary circle in ℝ25 at a scale of 0.6. It is not a pathological example — it is what the exact object costs. Once the scale exceeds the diameter of the sample, every subset spans a simplex, so the d-skeleton has Θ(nd+1) simplices and there is nothing clever to be done about it.

The response of this chapter is not a faster algorithm. It is to build a different, smaller filtration and prove how far its barcode can be from the true one. The error turns out to be a single constant you choose in advance, and the whole subject is the exchange rate between that constant and the size you pay.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The sparse Rips complex Qα is built on a vertex set that shrinks as α grows: points that have become redundant are thrown away. Predict what that does to the sequence {Qα}α.

A filtration needs each complex to sit inside the next, and deleting vertices does the opposite of that — the first option has the direction backwards. Dey and Wang note it explicitly: for α < β, Qα need not be contained in Qβ, nor Q̄α in Q̄β; what is always true is 𝕊α ⊆ 𝕊β for the cumulative complex 𝕊α := ⋃β≤α Q̄β, and it is 𝕊 that is called the sparse Rips filtration. The hypothesis ε < 1/3 is real but does other work — it is what makes the packing bound in Theorem 6.4(b) come out.

Why this matters for the mission: Sparse Rips filtrations, witness complexes and graph-induced complexes produce complexes of size linear in the sample while staying multiplicatively interleaved with the exact filtration, so tda1-07's stability bound still governs the approximation. This is the difference between lab-03 finishing and lab-03 exhausting memory.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The two filtrations, and the wall

Fix a finite P in a metric space (Z, dZ). The Čech complex at scale r is the nerve of the union of balls Pr = ⋃p∈PBZ(p,r):

ℂrZ(P) = {σ = {p0,…,pk} | ⋂i BZ(pi, r) ≠ ∅},

while the Vietoris–Rips complex uses pairwise distances alone:

𝕍ℝr(P) = {σ = {p0,…,pk} | dZ(pi, pj) ≤ 2r for all i, j}.

Corollary 6.1. For a fixed r ≥ 0, if the metric ball BZ(x, r) is convex for every x ∈ P, then ℂr(P) is homotopy equivalent to Pr, and thus Hk(ℂr(P)) ≅ Hk(Pr) for any dimension k ≥ 0. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Corollary 6.1, p. 180

Proposition 6.2. If the metric ball B(x, r) is convex for every x ∈ P and all r ≥ 0, then the persistence module Hk𝒫 is isomorphic to Hk𝒞(P); their persistence diagrams are identical, Dgmk𝒫 = Dgmk𝒞(P), for any k ≥ 0. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Proposition 6.2, p. 180

Corollary 6.1 is the nerve theorem applied at one radius; Proposition 6.2 is the upgrade a persistence statement actually needs — the whole filtration of unions of balls and the whole Čech filtration have the same diagram — and it pays for the upgrade with convexity at every radius rather than at one. Between the Čech and Rips filtrations, meanwhile, Dey and Wang record the standard comparison: they are multiplicatively 2-interleaved, so at log scale their modules are log 2-interleaved and

db(Dgmlog𝒞(P), Dgmlogℛ(P)) ≤ log 2   (Corollary 4.4).

One subtlety before the cost. If the ambient space is dropped and P is treated as a discrete metric space (P, dP), the balls change — BP(p,r) holds only points of P — and the two Čech complexes differ:

ℂrP(P) ⊆ ℂrZ(P) ⊆ ℂ2rP(P).

The Rips complexes, by contrast, coincide exactly, because the definition never mentions a ball. Remember the factor 2: it will reappear in Theorem 6.3.

Definition 6.1 (Čech distance; Rips distance). For finite point sets P and Q with appropriate metrics, dCech(P,Q) = maxk dB(Dgmk𝒞(P), Dgmk𝒞(Q)) and dRips(P,Q) = maxk dB(Dgmkℛ(P), Dgmkℛ(Q)). — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.1, p. 181

Definition 6.2 (Hausdorff distance). For compact A, B ⊆ (Z, dZ), dH(A,B) = max{maxa∈A dZ(a,B), maxb∈B dZ(b,A)}. — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.2, p. 181

Definition 6.3 (Gromov–Hausdorff distance). For metric spaces (X, dX) and (Y, dY), a correspondence is a C ⊆ X × Y meeting every x ∈ X and every y ∈ Y; its distortion is distortC(X,Y) = ½ sup(x,y),(x′,y′)∈C |dX(x,x′) − dY(y,y′)|; and dGH(X,Y) = infC distortC(X,Y). — Dey & Wang §6.1, Definition 6.3, p. 182

Definition 6.1's distances are only pseudodistances: two different point sets can share every diagram, and then the distance is zero without the sets being equal. Definition 6.2 needs a common ambient space; Definition 6.3 does not, which is what makes it the right tool for two point clouds that merely came from somewhere.

Theorem 6.3. (a) For finite P, Q ⊆ (Z, dZ): dCech(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q) and dRips(P,Q) ≤ dH(P,Q). (b) For finite metric spaces (P, dP) and (Q, dQ): dCech(P,Q) ≤ 2dGH((P,dP),(Q,dQ)) and dRips(P,Q) ≤ dGH((P,dP),(Q,dQ)). — Dey & Wang §6.1, Theorem 6.3, p. 182

There is the factor 2 again, on the Čech bound in part (b) and nowhere else. Dey and Wang say where it comes from: the difference in metric balls recorded above. The Rips distance escapes it because the Rips complex never looked at a ball. They also note that part (b) extends to totally bounded metric spaces — those admitting a finite ε-sample for every ε > 0, hence approximable by a finite metric space at any resolution — which need not be finite themselves.

The wall. When the scale r exceeds the diameter of P, every subset of P spans a simplex of both complexes, so the d-skeleton of ℂr(P) or 𝕍ℝr(P) has size Θ(nd+1) for n = |P|. This is not an implementation problem. A few thousand points already produce triangles in the millions, and the exact filtration on a realistic sample cannot be written down, let alone reduced. Everything after this point is a trade: a smaller object, and a theorem saying how far its barcode can be from the true one.

Self-check 1. Why does Theorem 6.3(b) carry a factor 2 for the Čech distance but not for the Rips distance?

The factor traces to Eq. (6.5): dropping the ambient space changes the balls, and the two Čech complexes are only related up to a doubling of the radius. The ½ in the distortion is present in both bounds and so cannot explain a difference between them. The third option has the containment backwards in any case — a Rips complex contains its Čech complex at the same scale — and size is not what stability keys on.

Self-check 2. What exactly does Proposition 6.2 add to Corollary 6.1?

Corollary 6.1 gives one isomorphism per radius; that is not enough for persistence, which is a statement about the maps between scales as much as about the spaces. Proposition 6.2 asserts the isomorphism of modules and deduces Dgmk𝒫 = Dgmk𝒞(P) — and its hypothesis is strictly stronger, convexity of B(x,r) for all r rather than for one. Nothing here upgrades homotopy equivalence to homeomorphism, which would be false.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Sparse by subsampling: Delaunay, witness, graph-induced

The oldest answer to the size problem is to build a complex that is sparse by construction. Chapter 2 offers three, and it is worth being precise about what each one is proved to do.

Definition 2.11 (Delaunay simplex; complex). For a finite P ⊂ ℝd, a k-simplex σ is Delaunay if its vertices are in P and there is an open d-ball whose boundary contains its vertices and which is empty — contains no point of P. A Delaunay complex Del P is a geometric simplicial complex with vertices in P in which every simplex is Delaunay and whose underlying space is the convex hull of P. — Dey & Wang §2.3.1, Definition 2.11, p. 34

Definition 2.12 (Voronoi diagram). For a finite P ⊂ ℝd in generic position, Vor(P) tessellates ℝd into convex cells Vp = {x ∈ ℝd | d(x,p) ≤ d(x,q) for all q ∈ P}; a k-face of Vor(P) is the intersection of (d − k + 1) cells. — Dey & Wang §2.3.1, Definition 2.12, p. 35

Fact 2.3 says a nondegenerate point set — no d + 2 points cospherical — has a unique Delaunay complex, and Fact 2.4 identifies Del(P) as the nerve of the closed cover {Vp}, which is the source of the Delaunay–Voronoi duality sending a Delaunay k-simplex to a Voronoi (d − k)-face. Fact 2.5 records three optimality properties: in ℝ2, Del P maximises the minimum angle and minimises the largest circumcircle; in all dimensions it minimises the largest min-ball. The alpha complex Delα(P) is the subcomplex of simplices with a circumscribing ball of radius at most α, equivalently the nerve of the truncated Voronoi cells Dpα = {x ∈ B(p,α) | d(x,p) ≤ d(x,q) ∀q}.

The catch is arithmetic. Del P has size Θ(n) in ℝ2, Θ(n2) in ℝ3, and Θ(n⌈d/2⌉) in ℝd — computable in that same time, but for high-dimensional data that is no help at all.

Definition 2.13 (weak witness). Let P be a point set with d : P × P → ℝ and Q ⊆ P finite. A simplex σ = {q1,…,qk} with qi ∈ Q is weakly witnessed by x ∈ P ∖ Q if d(q,x) ≤ d(p,x) for every q ∈ σ and every p ∈ Q ∖ σ. — Dey & Wang §2.3.2, Definition 2.13, p. 36

Definition 2.14 (witness complex). With P and Q as above, the witness complex 𝒲(Q,P) is the collection of all simplices all of whose faces are weakly witnessed by a point in P ∖ Q. — Dey & Wang §2.3.2, Definition 2.14, p. 37

Definition 2.15 (strong witness). For finite Q ⊂ ℝd, σ = {q1,…,qd} is strongly witnessed by x ∈ ℝd if the weak-witness inequality holds and in addition d(q1,x) = ⋯ = d(qd,x). — Dey & Wang §2.3.2, Definition 2.15, p. 37

The "all of whose faces" clause in Definition 2.14 is not fussiness. Dey and Wang note that a weakly witnessed simplex need not have all its faces weakly witnessed, so without the clause the collection would fail to be closed under faces and would not be a simplicial complex at all. Proposition 2.3 then says σ is strongly witnessed exactly when every face of σ is weakly witnessed; Fact 2.6 says σ ∈ Del Q exactly when σ is strongly witnessed by points of ℝd; and combining them gives Proposition 2.4: for finite P ⊂ ℝd and Q ⊆ P, 𝒲(Q,P) ⊆ Del Q. That containment has a pleasant consequence — a witness complex of a Euclidean sample embeds in that Euclidean space — and an unpleasant one, which is the next definition's reason for existing. Proposition 2.5 pins down when the containment is an equality: 𝒲(Q,ℝd) = Del Q; 𝒲(Q,M) is the restricted Delaunay complex Del|MQ for a smooth 1- or 2-manifold M; and 𝒲(Q,P) = Del|MQ for sufficiently dense samples of a 1-manifold in ℝ2, with no extension to other submanifolds.

Definition 2.16 (graph induced complex). Let (P, d) be a finite metric space, G(P) a graph with vertices in P, Q ⊆ P, and ν : P → Q with ν(p) any point of argmin d(p,Q). The graph induced complex 𝒢(G(P), Q, d) contains the k-simplex σ = {q1,…,qk+1}, qi ∈ Q, if and only if there is a (k+1)-clique in G(P) spanned by {p1,…,pk+1} ⊆ P with qi ∈ ν(pi) for each i. — Dey & Wang §2.3.3, Definition 2.16, p. 39

Definition 2.17 (δ-sample; δ-sparse). Q ⊆ P is a δ-sample of (P, d) if every p ∈ P has some q ∈ Q with d(p,q) ≤ δ, and δ-sparse if d(q,r) ≥ δ for all distinct q, r ∈ Q. — Dey & Wang §2.3.3, Definition 2.17, pp. 39–40

It is a simplicial complex because a subset of a clique is a clique. The motivation Dey and Wang give is blunt: the witness complex does not capture the topology of a manifold even for dense input, except for smooth curves in the plane. The GIC's advantage is that it is not forced to be a subcomplex of Del Q, so it carries extra simplices that help inference; its disadvantage is the same sentence read backwards — it may not embed in the Euclidean space its vertices live in. Two inference results are stated.

Theorem 2.6. Let M ⊂ ℝ3 be a smooth, compact, connected surface. If 8ε ≤ δ ≤ (2/27)ρ, α ≥ 8ε, P is an ε-sample of (M, dE), and Q ⊆ P is a δ-sparse δ-sample of (P, dE), then a triangulation T of M exists as a subcomplex of 𝒢α(P,Q,dE) and can be computed efficiently. — Dey & Wang §2.3.3, Theorem 2.6, p. 40

Theorem 2.7. Let P be an ε-sample of an embedded smooth compact manifold M with reach ρ, and Q a δ-sample of (P, dG). For 4ε ≤ α and δ ≤ (1/3)√(3/5) ρ, the map h∗ : H1(𝕍ℝα(P)) → H1(𝒢α(P,Q,dG)) is an isomorphism, where h is the simplicial map induced by the nearest-point map νdG : P → Q. — Dey & Wang §2.3.3, Theorem 2.7, p. 40

What the mission strip claims, and what §2.3 establishes. The strip says that sparse Rips filtrations, witness complexes and graph-induced complexes all "produce complexes of size linear in the sample while staying multiplicatively interleaved with the exact filtration". For the sparse Rips filtration both halves are theorems, and Segment 3 proves them: Theorem 6.4(b) gives Θ((1/ε)kdn) simplices and Theorem 6.4(a) gives multiplicative 1/(1−ε)-interleaving. For witness complexes and graph-induced complexes, §2.3 proves neither. It states no bound on the number of simplices of either — what it offers instead is the qualitative claim that they are "much sparser", the remark that a GIC "retains the simplicity of the Rips complex as well as the sparsity of the witness complex", and the empirical comparison of Figure 2.8 on a Klein bottle sample in ℝ4 and a primary-circle sample in ℝ25. And it proves no interleaving with the Rips filtration; what it proves instead is of a different type — Theorem 2.6's triangulation-as-subcomplex and Theorem 2.7's H1-isomorphism, both under sampling hypotheses on a hidden manifold, and both about a single scale α rather than about a filtration. The strip's last clause is right in substance but worth restating precisely: what converts interleaving into a diagram bound here is Chapter 4's Corollary 4.4, and the resulting errors are logarithmic and multiplicative, not additive.

Self-check 3. Why must Definition 2.14 require every face to be weakly witnessed?

Dey and Wang state the reason exactly: the clause is there to force a simplicial complex, since being weakly witnessed is not inherited by faces. The containment in Del Q is a consequence proved afterwards, via Proposition 2.3 and Fact 2.6, not the motivation. And nothing in the definition requires distinct witnesses; one point may witness many simplices, as Figure 2.6 shows.

Self-check 4. Theorem 2.7 concerns H1 only. What should you conclude about H2 of a graph induced complex?

The map h being defined in all dimensions is not an argument that it is an isomorphism in all of them; Theorem 2.7 says H1 and stops, and Dey and Wang explicitly decline to state further homology inference results precisely, offering empirical evidence instead. The third option misreads Definition 2.16: a (k+1)-clique of G(P) yields a k-simplex, so a GIC certainly has simplices above dimension 1 — that is exactly why it is not a graph.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Sparsifying the filtration itself, and inferring a hidden space

The second strategy does not subsample once. It sparsifies as the scale grows, which is the right thing to do because Figure 6.2's observation is that redundancy is scale-dependent: two points needed at a small radius become superfluous at a large one.

Definition 6.4 (nets; net-tower). For finite P ⊂ (ℝd, d) and γ, γ′ ≥ 0, a subset Q ⊆ P is a (γ,γ′)-net of P if (covering) Q is a γ-sample, d(p,Q) ≤ γ for every p ∈ P, and (packing) Q is γ′-sparse, d(q,q′) ≥ γ′ for distinct q,q′ ∈ Q; if γ = γ′ it is a γ-net. A family {Nγ}γ is a net-tower of P if (i) there is a constant c > 0 with Nγ a (γ, γ/c)-net for all γ, and (ii) Nγ ⊇ Nγ′ for γ ≤ γ′. — Dey & Wang §6.2.1, Definition 6.4, pp. 183–184

Farthest point sampling builds one. Choose p1 ∈ P arbitrarily, set P1 = {p1}, and recursively take pi ∈ argmaxp∈P∖Pi−1 d(p, Pi−1) with Pi = Pi−1 ∪ {pi}. The exit-time of pi is tpi := d(pi, Pi−1) — how far it was from everything already chosen. The open and closed net-towers are then Nγ = {p ∈ P | tp > γ} and N̄γ = {p ∈ P | tp ≥ γ}: as γ grows, only the points that were far from their predecessors survive.

Fix 0 < ε < 1. Each point now carries a weight that switches on when the scale outgrows it:

wp(α) = 0 if tp/ε ≥ α;   α − tp/ε if tp/ε < α < tp/(ε(1−ε));   εα if tp/(ε(1−ε)) ≤ α.

Claim 6.1 records that wp is continuous, 1-Lipschitz and nondecreasing — all three matter, and the Lipschitz property does the most work. The net-induced distance at scale α inflates the metric by the weights,

d̂α(p,q) := d(p,q) + wp(α) + wq(α),

so a point becomes progressively harder to connect to as it ages out.

Definition 6.5 (sparse Rips). For P ⊂ ℝd, a constant 0 < ε < 1 and the net-towers above, the open sparse Rips complex at scale α is Qα := {σ ⊆ Nε(1−ε)α | d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α for all p,q ∈ σ}, and the closed one is Q̄α := {σ ⊆ N̄ε(1−ε)α | d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α for all p,q ∈ σ}. The cumulative complex is 𝕊α := ⋃β≤α Q̄β, and the ε-sparse Rips filtration is 𝕊 = {𝕊α ↪ 𝕊β}α≤β. — Dey & Wang §6.2.1, Definition 6.5, pp. 185–186

This settles the prediction gate. Neither Qα ⊆ Qβ nor Q̄α ⊆ Q̄β holds in general for α < β, because the vertex set shrinks; only 𝕊α ⊆ 𝕊β is automatic, and the cumulative union is what makes the object a filtration at all.

Visual: the sparse complex against the exact one

The grey graph is the 1-skeleton of the exact Rips complex at scale α; the blue one is the sparse complex of Definition 6.5, on the vertices that survive Nε(1−ε)α. At small α they agree. As α grows the grey graph races towards the complete graph while the blue one sheds vertices and keeps its edge count under control — and that gap, extended to k-simplices, is Theorem 6.4(b).

Read that bound carefully before believing it solves the problem. Θ((1/ε)kdn) is linear in n, which is the whole point, but the constant is exponential in kd. With ε = 0.1, k = 2 and d = 3 the constant alone is 106. What has been bought is the removal of the nd+1 growth, not smallness in any absolute sense — and the price of shrinking the error by taking ε → 0 is that the constant explodes. That trade is the subject.

Worked example: the chain that proves Theorem 6.4

The statement. Theorem 6.4: for P ⊂ ℝd with |P| = n and d constant, ℛ(P) the Rips filtration and 𝕊(P) the ε-sparse Rips filtration, and fixed 0 < ε < 1/3: (a) 𝕊(P) and ℛ(P) are multiplicatively 1/(1−ε)-interleaved at the homology level, so Dgmk𝕊(P) is a log(1/(1−ε))-approximation of Dgmkℛ(P) at log scale; (b) for fixed k ≥ 0, the total number of k-simplices ever appearing in 𝕊(P) is Θ((1/ε)kdn).

Step 1 — an intermediate object. Define the relaxed Rips complex 𝕍ℝ̂α(P) := {σ ⊆ P | d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α for all p,q ∈ σ}: same inflated distance, but on all of P. Claim 6.2 says that if d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α ≤ 2β then d̂β(p,q) ≤ 2β, so the relaxed complexes really do form a filtration under inclusion — unlike the sparse ones.

Step 2 — sandwich the intermediate object. Proposition 6.5: with C = 1/(1−ε), 𝕍ℝα/C ⊆ 𝕍ℝ̂α ⊆ 𝕍ℝα. This is the entire source of the approximation constant, and the guided proof below asks you to supply it.

Step 3 — identify the sparse complex with the relaxed one. Proposition 6.6: the inclusion i : Qα ↪ 𝕍ℝ̂α induces an isomorphism on homology. The mechanism is a projection πα onto the net — the identity on Nε(1−ε)α and the nearest net point otherwise — shown to be a simplicial retraction with i ∘ πα contiguous to the identity. Fact 6.2, applied twice, is what makes πα simplicial: d̂α(πα(p), πα(q)) ≤ d̂α(p, πα(q)) ≤ d̂α(p,q). Fact 6.1, together with wp(α) ≤ εα, is what supplies the contiguity: d̂α(p, πα(p)) ≤ 2wp(α) ≤ 2εα < 2α.

Step 4 — climb to the cumulative complex. The same argument covers Qα ↪ Q̄α. Proposition 6.7 then handles Q̄α ↪ 𝕊α, factoring the inclusion through the intermediate complexes Ti,k = ⋃j=ik Q̄αj and repeating retraction-plus-contiguity at each step. Combining, {𝕊α} and {𝕍ℝ̂α} induce isomorphic persistence modules, and with Step 2 that is Theorem 6.4(a).

Step 5 — the size bound, by packing. Charge each simplex of 𝕊(P) to its vertex of smallest exit-time. A point p contributes no new edge beyond β = tp/(ε(1−ε)), so it is enough to count in Q̄αp with αp = tp/(ε(1−ε)). Put E(p) = {q | (p,q) ∈ Q̄αp, tp ≤ tq} ∪ {p}. Every pair in E(p) is at distance at least tp, by the packing condition of the net; and E(p) ⊆ B(p, 2αp). A packing argument in ℝd gives |E(p)| = O((2αp/tp)d) = O((2/(ε(1−ε)))d) = O((1/ε)d), the last step using ε < 1/3 so that 1 − ε ≥ 2/3. So at most O((1/ε)kd) k-simplices are charged to each point, and O((1/ε)kdn) in total. That is where ε < 1/3 earns its place.

A second sparsification, and a weaker interleaving

Dey and Wang give an alternative that is conceptually simpler and provably worse. Start from the geometric subsequence of the Rips filtration, 𝕍ℝα(P) ↪ 𝕍ℝα(1+ε)(P) ↪ ⋯ ↪ 𝕍ℝα(1+ε)m(P). Then set P0 = P and let Pk+1 be an (αε2)(1+ε)k−1-net of Pk, stopping when Pm has constant size; the nearest-neighbour map πk : Pk → Pk+1 induces a simplicial map, and one obtains a tower connected by simplicial maps rather than a filtration connected by inclusions. Claim 6.3 says the triangles relating the two sequences commute at the homology level — but only at the discrete indices.

Definition 6.6 (weak interleaving of vector space towers). Towers 𝕌 and 𝕍 over {a ∈ ℝ | a ≥ a0} are weakly ε-interleaved if there are linear maps φi : Ua0+iε → Va0+(i+1)ε and ψi : Va0+iε → Ua0+(i+1)ε for every integer i ≥ 0 such that any subdiagram of the resulting diagram commutes. — Dey & Wang §6.2.2, Definition 6.6, p. 191

The difference from the strong notion of Definition 4.4 is that the relations hold only at discrete index values, and the price is exactly a factor of three: weakly ε-interleaved modules satisfy db(Dgm 𝕌, Dgm 𝕍) ≤ 3ε rather than ε.

Theorem 6.8. Given n points P ⊂ ℝd, one can 3log(1+ε)-approximate the persistence diagram of the discrete Rips filtration by that of the sparsified tower at the log scale. The number of k-simplices ever appearing in the tower is O((1/ε)O(kd)n). — Dey & Wang §6.2.2, Theorem 6.8, p. 192

What any of this says about a hidden space

So far everything has been about approximating the diagram of a filtration built from P. The inference question is different: P is a sample of some hidden X, and one wants H∗(X).

Definition 6.7 (distance field). For compact X ⊂ ℝd, the distance field is dX : ℝd → ℝ, x ↦ d(x,X); the α-offset is Xα := {x | dX(x) ≤ α}, the sublevel set dX−1((−∞,α]). — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.7, p. 193

Definition 6.8 (local feature size; reach). The local feature size at x ∈ X is its distance to the medial axis of X; the reach ρ(X) is the minimum local feature size over X. — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.8, pp. 193–194

Definition 6.9 (generalized vector field). With Π(x) the set of closest points of x in X and c(x) the centre of the minimal enclosing ball of Π(x), define ∇d : ℝd ∖ X → ℝd by ∇d(x) = (x − c(x))/dX(x). Its critical points are the x with ∇d(x) = 0. — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.9, p. 194

Definition 6.10 (weak feature size). With C the set of critical points of ∇d, wfs(X) = minx∈X infc∈C d(x,c). — Dey & Wang §6.3.1, Definition 6.10, p. 195

Why two feature sizes? Because the reach is fragile. It can be zero at a nonsmooth point — a polygon's medial axis reaches its vertices — and Dey and Wang note that even a smoothly embedded manifold can have reach zero. The reach is used for smooth manifolds, the weak feature size for general compact sets. Proposition 6.9 is the reason the second one is useful: if 0 < α < α′ and dX has no critical value in [α,α′], then Xα′ deformation retracts onto Xα, so H∗(Xα) ≅ H∗(Xα′).

Fact 6.3 (rank sandwich). Given a sequence A → B → C → D → E → F of linear maps between finite-dimensional vector spaces, if rank(A → F) = rank(C → D), then this quantity also equals rank(B → E). Specifically, if A → B → C → E → F has rank(A → F) = dim C, then rank(B → E) = dim C. — Dey & Wang §6.3.2, Fact 6.3, p. 195

Worked example (faded): pinning H∗(X) between two Rips complexes. Let X ⊂ ℝd be a smooth manifold of reach ρ(X) and P a finite sample with dH(P,X) ≤ ε.

Step 1 — from the sample to the space. Proposition 6.10: if 3ε ≤ α ≤ (3/4)√(3/5) ρ(X) then H∗(Pα) ≅ H∗(X). Note this is about the union of balls, not about any complex.

Step 2 — from the union of balls to the Čech complex. Euclidean balls are convex, so the Nerve Theorem applies and ℂα(P) is homotopy equivalent to Pα. Combining with Step 1, H∗(X) ≅ H∗(Pα) ≅ H∗(ℂα(P)) ≅ H∗(ℂβ(P)) for any β in the same window, the last isomorphism being induced by ⟨your step: which map?⟩.

Step 3 — interleave Čech with Rips. Write the chain ℂα(P) ⊆ 𝕍ℝα(P) ⊆ ℂ2α(P) ⊆ 𝕍ℝ2α(P) ⊆ ℂ4α(P) and apply H∗. By Step 2 the three Čech groups are all isomorphic under the inclusion-induced maps, so rank(H∗(ℂα) → H∗(ℂ4α)) = ⟨your step: equal to what?⟩.

Step 4 — squeeze. Apply the second part of Fact 6.3 to that five-term sequence. The middle rank is pinned, which is Theorem 6.11: for 3ε ≤ α ≤ (3/16)√(3/5) ρ(X) and i∗ : H∗(𝕍ℝα) → H∗(𝕍ℝ2α) the inclusion-induced map, rank(im(i∗)) = dim(H∗(ℂα(P))) = dim(H∗(X)).

Why the image and not the group. Read what the sandwich actually controls. Fact 6.3 pins a rank along a composite, so what the argument delivers is the rank of the image of the map between two scales — not the dimension of H∗(𝕍ℝα) for either one on its own, about which the chain says nothing. That is the persistence idea used as an inference tool.

Gap solutions: the inclusion ℂα(P) ⊆ ℂβ(P); and the rank equals dim(H∗(ℂα(P))).

For a general compact X the first step fails, and Dey and Wang say why: there are compact sets for which H∗(Xλ) is not isomorphic to H∗(X) however small λ > 0 is. So one gives up on X and targets an offset instead, using Proposition 6.9 to know that all small offsets agree.

Proposition 6.12. Let X be compact in ℝd and P finite with dH(X,P) < ε for some ε < ¼wfs(X). Then for all α, α′ ∈ (ε, wfs(X) − ε) with α′ − α ≥ 2ε, and any λ ∈ (0, wfs(X)), H∗(Xλ) ≅ im(i∗), where i∗ : H∗(ℂα(P)) → H∗(ℂα′(P)) is induced by inclusion. — Dey & Wang §6.3.3, Proposition 6.12, p. 198

Theorem 6.13. Let X be compact in ℝd and P a finite point set with dH(X,P) < ε for some ε < (1/9)wfs(X). Then for all α ∈ (2ε, ¼(wfs(X) − ε)) and all λ ∈ (0, wfs(X)), H∗(Xλ) ≅ im(j∗), where j∗ is induced by the inclusion j : 𝕍ℝα/2(P) ↪ 𝕍ℝ2α(P). — Dey & Wang §6.3.3, Theorem 6.13, p. 198

Theorem 6.11. Given a smooth manifold X embedded in ℝd with reach ρ(X) and a finite sample P with dH(P,X) ≤ ε, for any 3ε ≤ α ≤ (3/16)√(3/5) ρ(X) and i∗ : H∗(𝕍ℝα) → H∗(𝕍ℝ2α) induced by inclusion, rank(im(i∗)) = dim(H∗(ℂα(P))) = dim(H∗(X)). — Dey & Wang §6.3.2, Theorem 6.11, pp. 196–197

The pattern to carry out of this unit. Two errors, of different kinds, and both must be reported. The first is the sparsification error: log(1/(1−ε)) at log scale for Definition 6.5 by Theorem 6.4(a), or 3log(1+ε) for the tower by Theorem 6.8. It is entirely under your control — you chose ε — and it is multiplicative, so it lives on the log axis, not the linear one. The second is the inference error: Theorem 6.11 and Theorem 6.13 say nothing at all unless dH(P,X) ≤ ε for a hidden X and the scale α lies inside a window set by ρ(X) or wfs(X). Neither the sampling density nor the feature size is computable from P, so that half is a hypothesis about the data, argued for from how the data were acquired. Reporting a sparse barcode as if it were a fact about a manifold conflates the two.

Self-check 5. Where is the hypothesis ε < 1/3 actually used in Theorem 6.4?

Part (b) is where the constant is spent. Proposition 6.5 holds for any 0 < ε < 1 — its proof needs only wp(α) ≤ εα and non-negativity — and Claim 6.2 needs nothing but the 1-Lipschitz property from Claim 6.1. The bound ε < 1/3 is what lets Dey and Wang absorb the factor (1 − ε) into the constant and state the count as Θ((1/ε)kdn).

Self-check 6. Why does the compact-set case need a pair of Čech complexes where the manifold case needed one?

Proposition 6.10 is the step that dies: it is stated for smooth manifolds via the reach, and Dey and Wang record compact sets for which no offset has the homology of X. The Nerve Theorem is unaffected — it depends on the balls being convex, which they still are. And the third option invents a comparison; the window in Proposition 6.12 is stated in terms of wfs(X) and is perfectly capable of being non-empty.

Guided proof

Prompt. (1) Prove the intermediate bound wp(α) ≤ εα for every p and every α ≥ 0, directly from the three-piece definition. (2) Prove Proposition 6.5: with C = 1/(1−ε), 𝕍ℝα/C ⊆ 𝕍ℝ̂α ⊆ 𝕍ℝα. (3) Deduce that the relaxed Rips filtration and the Rips filtration are multiplicatively C-interleaved, and say which further results are then needed to reach Theorem 6.4(a).

Hint: for (1) the only case with any content is the middle one, where α < tp/(ε(1−ε)). Dey and Wang state Proposition 6.5 without proof; part (2) is the argument they leave out.

Click to reveal model answer

(1) The weight never exceeds εα. Take the three cases of the definition. If tp/ε ≥ α then wp(α) = 0 ≤ εα, since ε, α ≥ 0. If tp/(ε(1−ε)) ≤ α then wp(α) = εα exactly. The middle case is the one to check: there tp/ε < α < tp/(ε(1−ε)), and the right-hand inequality rearranges to tp > ε(1−ε)α, hence tp/ε > (1−ε)α. Therefore

wp(α) = α − tp/ε < α − (1−ε)α = εα.

So wp(α) ≤ εα in all three cases, with equality only in the third. ∎

(2) Proposition 6.5. Both complexes are determined by their edges, being clique complexes, so it suffices to compare the conditions on pairs.

Right inclusion, 𝕍ℝ̂α ⊆ 𝕍ℝα. Let σ ∈ 𝕍ℝ̂α and p, q ∈ σ. Weights are non-negative, so

d(p,q) ≤ d(p,q) + wp(α) + wq(α) = d̂α(p,q) ≤ 2α,

which is the membership condition for 𝕍ℝα. Hence σ ∈ 𝕍ℝα.

Left inclusion, 𝕍ℝα/C ⊆ 𝕍ℝ̂α. Let σ ∈ 𝕍ℝα/C and p, q ∈ σ, so d(p,q) ≤ 2α/C = 2α(1−ε). Using part (1) twice,

d̂α(p,q) = d(p,q) + wp(α) + wq(α) ≤ 2α(1−ε) + εα + εα = 2α − 2εα + 2εα = 2α.

Hence σ ∈ 𝕍ℝ̂α. ∎

(3) The interleaving. Replacing α by Cα in the left inclusion gives 𝕍ℝα ⊆ 𝕍ℝ̂Cα; together with the right inclusion this is the chain

𝕍ℝ̂α ⊆ 𝕍ℝα ⊆ 𝕍ℝ̂Cα ⊆ 𝕍ℝCα,

valid for every α ≥ 0. Every map in sight is an inclusion, so every composite is again an inclusion and therefore agrees with the internal map of the relevant filtration at that scale factor — the commutativity an interleaving demands is automatic. Applying H∗ exhibits the two filtrations as multiplicatively C-interleaved with C = 1/(1−ε), and by Corollary 4.4 that bounds the bottleneck distance between the log-scaled diagrams by log C = log(1/(1−ε)).

To reach Theorem 6.4(a) one still needs the sparse filtration 𝕊 to be interchangeable with the relaxed one, which is Proposition 6.6 (Qα ↪ 𝕍ℝ̂α induces an isomorphism, and likewise Qα ↪ Q̄α) together with Proposition 6.7 (Q̄α ↪ 𝕊α induces an isomorphism). Both are proved by the same device: exhibit a simplicial retraction onto the smaller complex and show the composite is contiguous to the identity.

What this does and does not license. The interleaving constant is C = 1/(1−ε) and nothing smaller comes out of this argument; the approximation cannot be made exact by any choice of ε > 0, and driving ε to 0 destroys the size bound, since Theorem 6.4(b) is Θ((1/ε)kdn). The trade is genuine and quantified, which is the whole point — but note that it says nothing whatever about how close either diagram is to a hidden space. That is a separate hypothesis and a separate theorem.
