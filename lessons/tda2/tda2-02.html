Multiparameter persistence modules (tda2-02)

Imagine screening a cloud of points with two knobs: the allowed radius and the density threshold. Turning one knob at a time gives a line of snapshots; turning both gives a grid, and a connected component can be born on one route and merge on the other. The puzzle is whether those two routes tell the same algebraic story.

Self-check 1. Prediction gate: Put a one-dimensional class at grade (0,0). Let the route through t₁ then t₂ send it to 0 at (1,1), while the route through t₂ then t₁ sends it to a nonzero class. Does this still define a ℤ²-graded module?

The order of coordinate moves cannot matter for a ring action. Definition 11.2 requires the two composites around every square to be equal; the lesson will make this condition concrete and then connect it to filtrations and homology.

Why this matters for the mission: A d-parameter persistence module is a ℤᵈ-graded module over the polynomial ring k[t₁,…,t_d] (aa-13’s rings acting on aa-15’s modules); for d ≥ 2 the interval decomposition behind a barcode is unavailable—the structural fact that separates TDA II from TDA I.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The grid is algebra: grades, actions, and commuting squares

For one parameter, adjacent stages form a chain. With d parameters, the stages sit at grades u = (u₁,…,u_d) ∈ ℤᵈ and each coordinate supplies its own step. The polynomial ring records those steps algebraically.

Definition 11.1 (Polynomial ring). Given a variable t and a field k, the set of polynomials k[t] = {a₀ + a₁t + a₂t² + ⋯ + aₙtⁿ : n ≥ 0, aᵢ ∈ k} forms a ring under the usual polynomial addition and multiplication. In the multiparameter setting we use R = k[t₁,…,t_d], whose elements are finite sums of monomials ai₁,…,i_dt₁i₁⋯tdi_d with every exponent nonnegative and every coefficient in k. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.1, p. 325

Throughout this chapter k is a field, so the homology groups used as graded pieces are k-vector spaces. Write ei for the i-th standard basis vector of ℤᵈ.

Definition 11.2 (Graded module). A ℤᵈ-graded R-module is an R-module M that is a direct sum of k-vector spaces indexed by grades, M = ⊕u∈ℤᵈ Mu, and whose ring action satisfies ti · Mu ⊆ Mu+ei for every i and every u. The indices u are called grades. Equivalently, multiplication by ti is a linear map ti• : Mu → Mu+ei, and these maps commute: (tj•) ∘ (ti•) = (ti•) ∘ (tj•). — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.2, p. 325; equivalent map description, p. 326

The direct sum says what lives at each grid point. The action condition says which neighbouring point receives it. The commutative-square condition says that moving one step in direction i and then one in direction j has the same result as taking those steps in the opposite order.

The diagram is the map interpretation of Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.2, p. 326.

Worked Example 1 · Problem 1(c). Let M(0,0), M(1,0), M(0,1), and M(1,1) each be k, with all other grades 0. Let t₁• be the identity on the two horizontal nonzero maps and let t₂• be the zero map on the two vertical nonzero maps; maps involving a zero source or target are the unique zero maps.

Clause 1: direct sum. The vector space at each grade has been specified, and M is their direct sum, so the decomposition clause holds.

Clause 2: grade shift. Every t₁• map goes from Mu to Mu+e₁, and every t₂• map goes from Mu to Mu+e₂. The action lands in the required grade.

Clause 3: commutativity. At the only square with a nonzero source, the route through t₁ and then t₂ is zero because the second map is zero. The route through t₂ and then t₁ is zero because the first map is zero. Thus both composites M(0,0) → M(1,1) are the same zero map. The construction is therefore a ℤ²-graded R-module. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.2, p. 325

Worked Example 2 · Faded, Problem 1(b). Let Mu = k for u ≥ (0,0), coordinatewise, and Mu = 0 otherwise. Let each ti• be the identity whenever both source and target are nonzero.

The direct-sum clause is satisfied because M is specified as ⟨your step⟩. The maps have the correct grades. For a square based at u ≥ (0,0), both routes are ⟨your step⟩. If a route reaches a zero graded piece, both composites are zero because ⟨your step⟩. Hence the commutativity check is ⟨your step⟩.

Self-check 2. Which condition is the easy-to-miss extra check in Definition 11.2?

The definition allows arbitrary k-vector spaces and linear maps, including zero maps. What makes the separate coordinate actions a single R-action is their commutativity.

Self-check 3. In Problem 1(c), why do the two routes from (0,0) to (1,1) agree?

One route applies t₁• then the zero t₂•; the other applies the zero t₂• then t₁•. They are equal even though the horizontal maps are identities.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

From a bifiltration to modules, then to morphisms and shifts

Now put topology back into the square. A multiparameter filtration supplies the spaces and the inclusions; homology turns those inclusions into the linear maps that the graded-module language requires.

Definition 11.4 (d-parameter filtration). A d-parameter simplicial filtration is a family of simplicial complexes {Xu}u∈ℤᵈ such that Xu ⊆ Xu+ei for each grade u and each i = 1,…,d. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.4, p. 326

Definition 11.5 (d-parameter persistence module). A ℤᵈ-graded R-module M is a d-parameter persistence module when each Mu is a homology group defined over a field and the linear maps coming from the ring actions are induced by inclusions in a d-parameter simplicial filtration. A module is finitely generated when a finite set {g₁,…,gₙ} ⊆ M generates every element by an R-linear combination m = ∑ αᵢgᵢ, with αᵢ ∈ R; the R-linear combinations that equal zero are relations. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.5, pp. 326–327

Prerequisite bridge: from the earlier homology unit, H₀ records connected components. We will use that fact without reproving cellular or singular homology here.

Worked Example 1 · Problem 2. Consider the filtration on vertices {a,b}: X(0,0) = {a}, X(1,0) = {a,b}, X(0,1) = {a}, and X(1,1) = {a,b,ab}, constant beyond these grades.

(a) Filtration check. The four generating inclusions are {a} ⊆ {a,b}, {a} ⊆ {a}, {a,b} ⊆ {a,b,ab}, and {a} ⊆ {a,b,ab}. Each holds, so the family satisfies Definition 11.4.

(b) Homology pieces and maps. The component bases are [a] at (0,0), [a],[b] at (1,0), [a] at (0,1), and [a] at (1,1), because the edge ab joins a to b at the final grade.

Thus dim M(0,0) = 1, dim M(1,0) = 2, dim M(0,1) = 1, and dim M(1,1) = 1.

(c) Square check. The t₁-then-t₂ route sends [a] to [a]. The t₂-then-t₁ route also sends [a] to [a]. Since M(0,0) is generated by [a], the two linear maps are equal. The H₀ module therefore has the required commutativity. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definitions 11.4–11.5, pp. 326–327

Definition 11.6 (Morphism). A graded-module morphism f : M → N is an R-linear map preserving grades: f(Mu) ⊆ Nu for every u ∈ ℤᵈ. Equivalently, it is a collection of linear maps fu : Mu → Nu for which every square with a structure map commutes. Two graded modules are isomorphic when there are morphisms in both directions whose composites are identity maps; we write M ≅ N. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.6, p. 328

Definition 11.3 (The special graded module R). There is a special graded module whose piece at grade u is the k-vector space generated by the monomial tu, with the ring action given by R. We denote this graded module by R, keeping it distinct from the polynomial ring used to define it. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.3, p. 326

Definition 11.7 (Shifted module). For a graded module M and u ∈ ℤᵈ, define M→u by (M→u)v = Mv−u for each v. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.7, p. 328

Definition 11.8 (Free module). A graded module is free if it is isomorphic to a direct sum of shifted copies of the special graded module R: F ≅ ⊕j Rj, where Rj = R→uj. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.8, p. 328

Definition 11.9 (Homogeneous element). An element m ∈ M is homogeneous when m ∈ Mu for some grade u; write gr(m) = u. A minimal generating set of a free module is a basis, usually chosen to consist of homogeneous elements. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.9, p. 328

Two small lemmas for the problem set

Lemma A (the shifted quadrant). For the special graded module R, Rw is one-dimensional when w ≥ (0,…,0) coordinatewise and is zero otherwise. Consequently, (R→u)v = Rv−u is one-dimensional exactly when v ≥ u and is zero otherwise.

Proof. Definition 11.3 says that Rw is generated by the monomial tw. The polynomial-ring description allows only nonnegative exponents, so that monomial exists precisely when every coordinate of w is nonnegative; in that case its scalar multiples form a one-dimensional k-vector space. Substitute w = v − u and use Definition 11.7. — Follows directly from Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.3, p. 326, and Definition 11.7, p. 328

Lemma B (free structure maps are injective). In a free graded module, each structure map ti• : Fv → Fv+ei is injective.

Proof. On a shifted copy R→uⱼ, multiplication by ti sends each monomial basis element to a distinct monomial basis element one grade higher, so it has no nonzero kernel. A direct sum of these maps is injective. If F is only presented as isomorphic to that direct sum, the grade-preserving isomorphism conjugates the structure maps, so injectivity is preserved. — Follows from Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definitions 11.3, 11.7–11.8, pp. 326 and 328, with elementary linear algebra

Worked Example 2 · Faded, Problem 3(a) and (c). For R→(2,1), Definition 11.7 gives (R→(2,1))v = Rv−(2,1). By Lemma A this is k exactly when ⟨your step: the two coordinate inequalities⟩, and is zero elsewhere. Thus the module is a shifted quadrant with apex at (2,1).

For R→(2,1) ⊕ R→(0,0), take the identity e₁ in the first summand and e₂ in the second. The homogeneous basis is {e₁,e₂}, with gr(e₁) = ⟨your step⟩ and gr(e₂) = ⟨your step⟩. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definitions 11.7–11.9, p. 328

Application Worked Example · Problem 4. Let Nu = k for u ≥ (0,0), Nu = 0 otherwise, with identity structure maps wherever both pieces are nonzero. Define f : M → N on the module from Problem 2 by sending every component generator in Mu to the generator 1 ∈ Nu; in particular, f(1,0)([a]) = f(1,0)([b]) = 1.

This is grade-preserving because fu lands in Nu. It is R-linear because the two possible routes around the only nontrivial square both send [a] to 1, and at (1,0) the map t₂• sends both [a] and [b] to [a], which f also sends to 1. Thus every structure-map square commutes.

For any morphism g : N → M, the component g(1,0) has domain dimension 1 and codomain dimension 2. Its image has dimension at most 1, so it cannot be surjective.

A map Mu → Nu+e₁ is not grade-preserving in the sense of Definition 11.6. The construction that handles this is a shift. With Definition 11.7’s convention, such a map is an ordinary morphism M → N→(−e₁), because (N→(−e₁))u = Nu+e₁. The minus sign is forced by the source’s shift convention. — Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definitions 11.6–11.7, p. 328

Why the barcode stops being the right summary

The earlier one-parameter unit supplies the familiar chain: a finitely generated graded k[t]-module is controlled by the structure theorem over the PID k[t]; its graded cyclic summands appear as interval modules, and their multiset is the barcode. The two-parameter obstruction is a change in the coefficient ring, not a failure of the graded-module language.

Auxiliary algebra bridge · Problem 5(a). Use the standard ideal notation from aa-13: (t₁,t₂) = {t₁a + t₂b : a,b ∈ R}. We prove directly from the polynomial-ring description that this ideal is not principal.

Suppose, for contradiction, that (t₁,t₂) = (f). Then f divides both t₁ and t₂. Regard the polynomials first as polynomials in t₂ with coefficients in k[t₁]. Since t₁ has t₂-degree 0, f also has t₂-degree 0, so f ∈ k[t₁]. Now regard the equation f h = t₂ as an equation in t₁. Since t₂ has t₁-degree 0, f has t₁-degree 0 as well. Thus f is a nonzero constant, hence a unit in R.

But a unit generates the whole ring, so (f) contains 1. Every element of (t₁,t₂) has zero constant term, so 1 ∉ (t₁,t₂). This contradiction proves that (t₁,t₂) is not principal. Since a PID requires every ideal to be principal, R = k[t₁,t₂] is not a PID. This is an auxiliary proof, not a numbered Dey & Wang theorem; it is the elementary polynomial-ring bridge needed to unpack the problem-set comparison. — Uses Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.1, p. 325; the ideal and PID notions are prerequisite ring language from aa-13

Problem 5(b)–(c), faded explanation. The one-parameter chain is: graded k[t]-module → PID structure theorem → cyclic summands → intervals → barcode. Already in the first multiparameter case d = 2, the ring k[t₁,t₂] has the nonprincipal ideal above, so the step that is unavailable is ⟨your step: name the broken link⟩. What remains is the graded-module framework and, as the chapter opening states, existence of indecomposable summands for finitely generated persistence modules over ℤᵈ via Krull–Schmidt. What does not remain is a complete classification of those summands by a simple list of intervals. A claimed “two-parameter barcode” would therefore contradict the claim about ⟨your step: existence or classification?⟩, not the claim about ⟨your step⟩.

The structural contrast is Dey & Wang, Chapter 11 opening, pp. 321–322; the ring is Dey & Wang, §11.1.1, Definition 11.1, p. 325. The one-parameter chain is a prerequisite result from tda1-04.

Self-check 4. In the H₀ module from Problem 2, which map witnesses non-freeness?

At (1,0), the two component classes both map to [a] after the edge appears, so [a] − [b] maps to zero. Lemma B says free modules have injective structure maps.

Self-check 5. With (N→u)v = Nv−u, which shifted target represents a map Mv → Nv+e₁ as an ordinary grade-preserving morphism?

Substitute u = −e₁ into Definition 11.7: the target’s grade-v piece becomes Nv−(−e₁) = Nv+e₁. The shift is a bookkeeping device that restores grade preservation.

Exit Ritual: Blank-Page Reconstruction

Look away from the page. From nothing, reconstruct the three load-bearing claims of this lesson and explain why each forces the next:

  1. A ℤᵈ-graded R-module is a direct sum of graded pieces, with ti moving one step in direction ei; commutativity makes the route around every grid square independent of order.
  2. A d-parameter filtration supplies inclusions, and homology turns them into those commuting maps. Grade-preserving morphisms compare such modules; shifts move the bookkeeping of grades, and free modules have injective coordinate maps.
  3. Already for d = 2, the polynomial ring has a nonprincipal ideal, so the one-parameter PID-to-interval-to-barcode route is unavailable. Indecomposable pieces may still exist, but the chapter’s warning is about their classification, not their existence.
