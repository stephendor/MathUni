Kernels, vectorisation, and topological loss functions (tda2-09)

You cannot average two persistence diagrams. There is no sum, no scalar multiple, no inner product — and every machine learning pipeline you own assumes all three.

The space of diagrams with the bottleneck or Wasserstein distance is a perfectly good metric space and nothing more. Dey and Wang open the chapter by naming exactly that defect: it "lacks (e.g., inner product) structure, which can pose challenges when used within a machine learning framework". Everything in §13.1 is a way of mapping diagrams somewhere that does have the missing structure, and every such map is a lossy bargain.

The second half of the chapter reverses the arrow. Instead of measuring topology after training, put a topological term in the loss and differentiate through it — which requires knowing when a persistence diagram is a differentiable function of a model's parameters, and that is a more delicate question than it sounds.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. To do gradient descent on a persistence-based loss you must differentiate the birth and death values with respect to the model parameters ω. Predict what §13.2 actually proves about that.

Proposition 13.9 is the whole content and it is a statement about a neighbourhood of a good ω, under a genericity hypothesis on vertex values. It licenses the chain rule at such parameters and says nothing at all about the size or structure of the set where two vertex values collide. The third option correctly notes that the map from fω to Dgm fω has no inverse — Dey and Wang say so — but the repair is not a surrogate: it is to map birth and death values back to vertices rather than to functions, which Theorem 3.16 makes possible for a PL-function.

Why this matters for the mission: The space of diagrams (tda1-05) is a metric space but not a Hilbert space, which is precisely why lab-06's landscapes, images and kernels exist — and each carries its own stability constant against the bottleneck or Wasserstein distance. Persistence-based loss functions are differentiable almost everywhere, which is what allows topology to be optimised inside a model rather than only measured after it.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Landscapes: a diagram as a function

Write 𝔻 for the space of bounded, finite persistence diagrams. The first construction, and still the cleanest, turns a diagram into a function.

Definition 13.1 (persistence landscape). Given a finite persistence diagram D = {(bi, di)}i∈[1,n] from 𝔻, the persistence landscape with respect to D is the function λD : ℕ × ℝ → ℝ where

λD(k, t) := k-th largest value of [min{t − bi, di − t}]+ for i ∈ [1, n],

with [c]+ = max(c, 0). — Dey & Wang §13.1.1, Definition 13.1, p. 391

The picture is the definition. Each point (bi, di) traces out a tent: the curve {(t, [min{t − bi, di − t}]+) | t ∈ ℝ} rises from (bi, 0) to an apex at the midpoint of the interval, at height half its persistence, and falls back to (di, 0). There are n such tents, and λD(k, ·) is the k-th upper envelope of their arrangement — the boundary of the k-th layer. For k > n no point of the line is covered k times, so λD(k, ·) ≡ 0.

Visual: the tents and their envelopes

Watch the red point and the envelopes together. Moving one death coordinate by δ moves its tent by δ and therefore moves every envelope by at most δ — which is Theorem 13.1 below, seen rather than proved. Note also how a point close to the diagonal makes a short flat tent that contributes almost nothing: low-persistence noise is quietly demoted, without anyone having thresholded it away.

Landscapes live in a normed space, which is the entire point. Put

‖λD‖pp = Σk=1∞ ‖λD(k, ·)‖pp,    Λp(D1, D2) = ‖λD1 − λD2‖p.

Landscapes of finite diagrams lie in the Lp-space 𝓛p(ℕ × ℝ), and for p = 2 that is a Hilbert space — the missing structure, recovered.

Claim 13.1. Given a persistence diagram D, let λD be its persistence landscape. Then from λD one can uniquely recover the persistence diagram D. — Dey & Wang §13.1.1, Claim 13.1, p. 392

Theorem 13.1. For persistence diagrams D and D′, Λ∞(D, D′) ≤ dB(D, D′). — Dey & Wang §13.1.1, Theorem 13.1, p. 393

So the map D ↦ λD is injective and 1-Lipschitz against the bottleneck distance — lossless and stable, which is as good as this subject gets. Dey and Wang note further results for Λp against the p-th Wasserstein distance, and for diagrams induced by tame Lipschitz functions.

Worked example (faded): a landscape by hand. Take D = {(1, 7), (2, 5)} and compute λD(1, t) and λD(2, t) at t = 3 and t = 4.

At t = 3. The first point contributes [min{3 − 1, 7 − 3}]+ = [min{2, 4}]+ = 2. The second contributes [min{3 − 2, 5 − 3}]+ = [min{1, 2}]+ = 1. Sorting descending, λD(1, 3) = 2 and λD(2, 3) = ⟨your step: what value?⟩.

At t = 4. The first gives [min{3, 3}]+ = 3; the second gives [min{2, 1}]+ = 1. So λD(1, 4) = 3, λD(2, 4) = 1.

Outside the intervals. At t = 0 both minima are negative, so both are clipped by [·]+ to 0 and λD(k, 0) = 0 for every k. And λD(k, ·) ≡ 0 for every k > ⟨your step: which threshold?⟩, since there are only that many tents.

Recovering D. Claim 13.1 says this is reversible. λD(1, ·) has apex 3 at t = 4, and a tent with apex h at t = m came from the point (m − h, m + h) = (1, 7). Peel that tent off and repeat on the rest.

Gap solutions: λD(2, 3) = 1; and the threshold is k > 2, the number of points of D.

One caveat, and it recurs. The landscape map is injective but far from surjective: a function λ : ℕ × ℝ → ℝ need not be the image of any valid diagram. In particular the mean landscape of D1, …, Dℓ,

λ̄(k, t) = (1/ℓ) Σi=1ℓ λDi(k, t),

is easy to compute and may correspond to no persistence diagram at all. That is the cost of leaving 𝔻 for a linear space: you gain arithmetic and lose the guarantee that your answers are diagrams. Segment 3's Fréchet mean is the opposite bargain — it stays inside 𝔻 and gives up uniqueness.

Self-check 1. Why does the mean landscape not contradict Claim 13.1?

Injectivity constrains the map on its image and says nothing about which functions are in that image. A convex combination of landscapes is perfectly good as a function — it is in 𝓛p, so the second option is false — and it simply need not be a landscape. Nothing in Claim 13.1 assumes distinct persistence values.

Self-check 2. A diagram point moves distance δ. By how much can λD(3, ·) change in sup-norm?

Λ∞(D, D′) = ‖λD − λD′‖∞ is a supremum over every layer k and every t, so Theorem 13.1's bound applies to λD(3, ·) as much as to λD(1, ·) — with no factor of k. The third option names something real, that the identity of the third-largest tent can switch, but that switch happens exactly where two values are equal, so the envelope itself does not jump.

Kernels: a diagram as an inner product

Definition 13.2 (positive, negative semidefinite kernel). Given a topological space X, a function k : X × X → ℝ is a positive semidefinite kernel if it is symmetric and for any integer n > 0, any x1,…,xn ∈ X and any a1,…,an ∈ ℝ with Σi ai = 0, it holds that Σi,j aiaj k(xi,xj) ≥ 0. Analogously k is negative semidefinite if under the same conditions Σi,j aiaj k(xi,xj) ≤ 0. — Dey & Wang §13.1.2, Definition 13.2, p. 393

The apparatus around it is standard and worth stating once. Given a set X and a Hilbert space ℋ of real-valued functions on X, the evaluation functional at x is Lx : ℋ → ℝ, Lx(f) = f(x); ℋ is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space if Lx is continuous for every x. Given a positive semidefinite kernel k there is a unique RKHS ℋk with k(x,y) = ⟨k(·,x), k(·,y)⟩ℋk, and k is its reproducing kernel. Equivalently a kernel is an inner product after a feature map Φ : X → ℋ, and it induces

dk2(x,y) := k(x,x) + k(y,y) − 2k(x,y),    equivalently dk(x,y) = ‖x − y‖ℋ,

a pseudometric: d(x,y) = 0 need not force x = y, since a feature map need not be injective.

Worked example: the persistence scale space kernel, end to end

Step 1 — where the features live. Let Ω = {x = (x1,x2) ∈ ℝ2 | x2 ≥ x1} be the closed half-plane on or above the diagonal, and recall that 𝓛2(Ω) is a Hilbert space. Diagrams will be mapped to functions on Ω.

Definition 13.3 (persistence scale space kernel). Define the feature map Φσ : 𝔻 → 𝓛2(Ω) at scale σ > 0 by

Φσ(D)(x) = (1/4πσ) Σy∈D [e−‖x−y‖²/4σ − e−‖x−ȳ‖²/4σ],

where ȳ = (y2, y1) is the reflection of y across the diagonal. The induced kernel is

kσ(D,E) = ⟨Φσ(D), Φσ(E)⟩𝓛²(Ω) = (1/8πσ) Σy∈D; z∈E [e−‖y−z‖²/8σ − e−‖y−z̄‖²/8σ].

— Dey & Wang §13.1.2, Definition 13.3 and Eq. (13.2), p. 394

Step 2 — read the reflected term. Each diagram point places a Gaussian bump at y and a negative bump at its mirror image ȳ. On the diagonal the two are equidistant, so they cancel exactly and the feature function vanishes there. A point of tiny persistence sits near the diagonal, so its positive and negative bumps nearly cancel and it contributes almost nothing. Low-persistence noise is suppressed by the geometry of the construction rather than by a threshold.

Step 3 — positive definiteness is free. kσ was defined as an inner product of feature vectors in a Hilbert space, so Definition 13.2's condition holds automatically: Σi,j aiaj⟨Φ(Di), Φ(Dj)⟩ = ‖Σi aiΦ(Di)‖² ≥ 0. This is the general lesson about feature-map constructions and it is why Segment 2's later kernels, which are not built from a feature map, need Theorem 13.4 instead.

Step 4 — the price, and what it is measured against.

Theorem 13.2. Given two persistence diagrams D, E ∈ 𝔻, ‖Φσ(D) − Φσ(E)‖𝓛²(Ω) ≤ (1/2πσ) dW,1(D,E). — Dey & Wang §13.1.2, Theorem 13.2, p. 394

Step 5 — the trade the constant encodes. The Lipschitz constant is 1/(2πσ), and it is against the 1-Wasserstein distance, not the bottleneck distance. Both facts matter. Small σ means sharp Gaussians and a feature that distinguishes nearby points well — and a Lipschitz constant that blows up, so the same feature reacts violently to a small perturbation of the diagram. Resolution and stability are the two ends of one parameter, and choosing σ is choosing where on that line to sit.

Definition 13.4 (persistence image). Let T(x,y) = (x, y − x) and T(D) the transformed diagram; let φu be a differentiable probability distribution with mean u. For a nonnegative weight function ω : ℝ2 → ℝ, the persistence surface of D is μD(z) := Σu∈T(D) ω(u)φu(z). Fixing a grid of N pixels 𝖯 on a rectangle, the persistence image is ID = {ID[𝗉]}𝗉∈𝖯 with ID[𝗉] := ∫𝗉 μD dx dy, a vector in ℝN. — Dey & Wang §13.1.3, Definition 13.4, p. 395. The displayed integrand there reads ρD; Eq. (13.3) defines the surface as μD, which is what is integrated.

Theorem 13.3. Suppose persistence images are computed with the normalised Gaussian of variance σ² and weight function ω. Then for finite bounded diagrams D and E,

‖ID − IE‖1 ≤ (√5 |∇ω| + √(10/π) ‖ω‖∞/σ) · dW,1(D, E),

where |∇ω| = supz∈ℝ²‖∇ω‖2; the same bound holds for ‖·‖2 and ‖·‖∞. — Dey & Wang §13.1.3, Theorem 13.3, p. 395

Again the constant degrades as σ → 0, and now it also depends on the weight function twice over — through its sup-norm and through its gradient. Dey and Wang's suggested choice for ω(u) is the persistence |b − d| of the point u = (b,d), which lets high-persistence features dominate the picture.

Definition 13.5 (persistence weighted kernel). Let k : Ω → ℝ be a C0-universal kernel on Ω and ω : Ω → ℝ+ strictly positive. The feature map Ψk,ω : 𝔻 → ℋk is Ψk,ω(D) = Σx∈D ω(x)k(·,x), and it induces

Kk,ω(D,E) = ⟨Ψk,ω(D), Ψk,ω(E)⟩ℋk = Σx∈D; y∈E ω(x)ω(y)k(x,y).

— Dey & Wang §13.1.4, Definition 13.5, p. 396

A kernel on a locally compact Hausdorff X is a C0-kernel if k(·,x) lies in C0(X), the continuous functions vanishing at infinity, and C0-universal if additionally ℋk is dense in C0(X); the Gaussian kernel is C0-universal on ℝd. Universality earns something concrete: viewing D as the weighted discrete measure μDω = Σx∈D ω(x)δx, the map Ψk,ω is the kernel mean embedding, and for a C0-universal k that embedding is injective — so the induced distance is a proper metric, not merely a pseudometric. Taking ωarc(x) = arctan(C·pers(x)p) and the 2D Gaussian gives the persistence weighted Gaussian kernel; putting a Gaussian on top of the RKHS representation instead of a linear kernel gives the (k,ω)-Gaussian kernel.

Kernels built from a distance instead of a feature map

Theorem 13.4. Given X and φ : X × X → ℝ, the kernel φ is negative semidefinite if and only if e−tφ is positive semidefinite for all t > 0. — Dey & Wang §13.1.5, Theorem 13.4, p. 398

Definition 13.6 (sliced Wasserstein distance). For a unit vector θ ∈ 𝕊1 let L(θ) = {λθ} and πθ the orthogonal projection onto it. For diagrams D and E set μDθ := Σp∈D δπθ(p) and μ̄Dθ := Σp∈D δπθ∘πΔ(p), where πΔ is the orthogonal projection onto the diagonal, and symmetrically for E. Then

dSW(D,E) := (1/2π) ∫𝕊¹ 𝒲(μDθ + μ̄Eθ, μEθ + μ̄Dθ) dθ.

— Dey & Wang §13.1.5, Definition 13.6, p. 398

Here 𝒲 is the 1-Wasserstein distance between measures on the line. The crossed sums are not decoration: 𝒲 compares measures of equal total mass, and D and E may have different cardinalities, so each is padded with the diagonal projections of the other. Proposition 13.5 then says dSW is negative semidefinite on 𝔻, and Theorem 13.4 converts it into the positive semidefinite sliced Wasserstein kernel kSW(D,E) := e−dSW(D,E)/2σ².

Theorem 13.6. Let 𝔻N be the set of bounded persistence diagrams with cardinalities at most N. For any D, E ∈ 𝔻N,

dW,1(D,E) / (4N(4N−1) + 2) ≤ dSW(D,E) ≤ 2√2 · dW,1(D,E).

— Dey & Wang §13.1.5, Theorem 13.6, p. 399

This is stronger than stability: each distance bounds the other, so dSW and dW,1 are strongly equivalent and a statement in one transfers to the other up to constants. But read the lower bound. Its denominator is quadratic in N, so the guarantee that dSW cannot collapse relative to dW,1 weakens fast as one allows larger diagrams.

Definition 13.7 (persistence Fisher kernel). Map D to μD(x) := (1/Z) Σu∈D φG,σ(x,u) with φG,σ(x,u) = e−‖x−u‖²/2σ and Z the normalising integral, and put DΔ := {πΔ(u) | u ∈ D}. The Fisher information metric is

dFIM(D,E) := arccos( ∫ℝ² √(μD∪EΔ(x) · μE∪DΔ(x)) dx ),

and the persistence Fisher kernel is kPF(D,E) := e−t·dFIM(D,E) for some t > 0. — Dey & Wang §13.1.6, Definition 13.7, pp. 399–400

Corollary 13.8. The persistence Fisher kernel kPF is positive definite on 𝔻. — Dey & Wang §13.1.6, Corollary 13.8, p. 400

Proposition 13.7 supplies what Theorem 13.4 needs — (dFIM − τ) is negative definite for any τ ≥ π/2 — and the guided proof asks you to finish the argument. Dey and Wang add that both kSW and kPF are infinitely divisible, which can bring computational advantages in kernel machines, and that the PSSK and PWGK are not.

What the mission strip claims, and what §13.1 establishes. The strip says each of these constructions "carries its own stability constant against the bottleneck or Wasserstein distance". For three of them that is exactly right and the constants are displayed above: Theorem 13.1 gives landscapes a constant of 1 against dB; Theorem 13.2 gives the PSSK 1/(2πσ) against dW,1; Theorem 13.3 gives persistence images an explicit constant in ω and σ against dW,1. For the sliced Wasserstein distance the situation is better than stability — Theorem 13.6 is a two-sided equivalence. But for the other two the strip outruns the section. For the PWGK, Dey and Wang state that stability results with respect to both dB and dW,1 exist in the cited work with bounds depending on ω and kG, and then write that the precise statements are somewhat involved and they omit the details — so no constant is available here, only the remark that bottleneck stability is provided and is usually harder to obtain. And for the persistence Fisher kernel §13.1.6 states no stability result at all; what it proves is positive definiteness (Corollary 13.8) and records infinite divisibility. Positive definiteness is a statement about being a legitimate kernel, not about being robust to a perturbation of the diagram, and the two must not be conflated.

Self-check 3. Why does the PSSK need no separate proof of positive definiteness, while the sliced Wasserstein kernel does?

Σi,jaiaj⟨Φ(Di),Φ(Dj)⟩ = ‖ΣiaiΦ(Di)‖² ≥ 0 with no work at all — that is what a feature-map construction buys, and it is why Dey and Wang say the PSSK is positive definite "by construction". The PSSK's feature space 𝓛²(Ω) is infinite-dimensional, so the second option is false. The third names a real subtlety but not the reason: the route taken is negative semidefiniteness of dSW followed by exponentiation.

Self-check 4. Both Theorem 13.2 and Theorem 13.3 have constants that grow as σ → 0. What is the general lesson?

A large Lipschitz constant is not a defect of the analysis, it is a description of the map: as σ shrinks, the Gaussians concentrate and moving a diagram point a little moves the feature a lot. The first option overshoots — a small σ may be exactly right when the diagrams are known to be clean — and the third is an assertion about proofs that the source does not make and that the σ-dependence of the construction contradicts.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Optimising topology, and averaging diagrams

Now reverse the arrow. A classifier is a smooth f : 𝐗 → ℝ on a d-dimensional hypercube, predicting by sign(f(x)); its classification boundary is the zero level set Sf = f−1(0). One wants to penalise a boundary that is topologically fussy — but the number of components of Sf is a bad penalty, because it counts features without asking how real they are.

The fix uses the whole function, not just its zero set. Let Dgm f be the levelset zigzag persistence diagram of f, and set

ΠSf := {(b, d) ∈ Dgm f | b ≤ 0; d ≥ 0},

the features whose lifetime passes through the zero level, in one-to-one correspondence with the topological features of Sf. For c = (b, d) ∈ ΠSf define the robustness ρ(c) = min{|b|, |d|}: the least L∞ perturbation of f needed to push c out of ΠSf and so delete the feature from Sf. Then

Ltopo(f) := Σc∈ΠSf ρ²(c),

and with any standard loss ℓ and a parameterisation fω, ω ∈ ℝm,

L(fω, Xn) = Σxi∈Xn ℓ(fω(xi), yi) + λ Ltopo(fω).

Why the robustness rather than the size of the feature? Dey and Wang's illustration is two small components of Sf of similar size, one of which is destroyed by pushing a single saddle q1 down a little so that it merges with the large component, while the other needs a much larger move of q2 or p2. How big a feature looks inside Sf says nothing about how deep the surrounding function must be moved to erase it. The persistence pairing knows; the level set alone does not.

Differentiating through a persistence diagram

To run gradient descent on L one needs ∂𝒯/∂ω for 𝒯(ω) := 𝒯(Dgm fω) = 𝒯({(bi, di)}). The terms ∂𝒯/∂bi are easy when 𝒯 is given analytically, as Ltopo is. What is hard is ∂bi/∂ω, which would seem to need an inverse for the map sending fω to its diagram — and that inverse does not exist, since many functions share a diagram.

The repair is to map back not to functions but to vertices. For a PL-function f on a finite complex K, Theorem 3.16 says each persistent point of Dgm f has the form (bi, di) = (f(vℓi), f(vri)) with vℓi and vri homological critical points, giving a map ρ : Dgm f → V × V with ρ(bi, di) = (vℓi, vri). Writing ξ(ω) := ρω(Dgm fω) ⊆ V × V, the question becomes whether ξ moves as ω moves.

Proposition 13.9. Suppose fω : |K| → ℝ is a PL-function with distinct values on all vertices V of K, and K is a finite simplicial complex. Then there exists a neighbourhood of ω in the parameter space such that ξ remains constant within this neighbourhood; that is, the image set ξ(ω) = ρω(Dgm fω) remains the same for all parameters within this neighbourhood. — Dey & Wang §13.2.2, Proposition 13.9, p. 405

What the mission strip claims, and what §13.2 establishes. The strip says persistence-based loss functions are "differentiable almost everywhere". Proposition 13.9 does not say that, and neither does anything else in §13.2. What it says is local and conditional: at a parameter ω where fω is PL on a finite complex and takes distinct values on all vertices, the vertex assignment is constant on a neighbourhood, so bi = fω(vℓi) with a fixed vertex and the chain rule applies there. No claim is made about the set of ω where two vertex values coincide — not that it is small, not that it has measure zero, not that the loss extends differentiably across it. "Almost everywhere" is a measure-theoretic assertion, and §13.2 contains no measure. What one may say honestly is: differentiable at generic parameters, in the precise sense Proposition 13.9 names, and silent elsewhere. The strip's conclusion — that this is what lets topology be optimised inside a model rather than only measured after it — stands unaltered; it is the quantifier that overreaches.

Statistics on the space of diagrams

The other way to keep arithmetic is to refuse to leave 𝔻 and redefine what a mean is.

Definition 13.8 ((p,q)-Wasserstein distance). For persistence diagrams P and Q, dW,qp(P,Q) := infΠ : P→Q [ Σx∈P ‖x − Π(x)‖pq ]1/q, where Π ranges over all bijections from P to Q. — Dey & Wang §13.3, Definition 13.8, p. 406

Definition 13.9 (space of persistence diagrams). With D∅ the trivial diagram containing only infinite copies of the diagonal, 𝔻qp := {P | dW,qp(P, D∅) < ∞}. — Dey & Wang §13.3, Definition 13.9, p. 406

The q-Wasserstein distance of Definition 3.10, met in tda1-05, is dW,q∞ in this notation. What is known about these spaces is exactly what statistics needs and no more: 𝔻q∞ is Polish — complete and separable — so probability measures can be defined on it, and 𝔻22 is a nonnegatively curved Alexandrov space, a geodesic space with curvature bounded below by zero. Neither is a Hilbert space, which is where this lesson began.

Definition 13.10 (Fréchet function, variance, and expectation). Given a probability distribution ρ on (𝒟, ℬ(𝒟)), its Fréchet function is ℱρ(X) := ∫𝒟 d𝒟2(X,Y) dρ(Y). The Fréchet variance is Varρ := infX∈𝒟 ℱρ(X), and the set at which it is attained, 𝔼(ρ) = {X | ℱρ(X) = Varρ}, is the Fréchet expectation, or Fréchet mean set. — Dey & Wang §13.3, Definition 13.10, pp. 406–407

Theorem 13.10. Let ρ be a probability measure on (𝒟, ℬ(𝒟)) with a finite second moment, that is ℱρ(X) < ∞ for any X ∈ 𝒟. If ρ has compact support, then 𝔼(ρ) ≠ ∅. — Dey & Wang §13.3, Theorem 13.10, p. 407

Note precisely what is bought and what is not. Theorem 13.10 says the mean set is non-empty, under two hypotheses. It does not say the mean is unique — Dey and Wang state plainly that in general it is not, which is awkward as soon as one wants to track a mean across a family of diagrams, and the probabilistic Fréchet mean is the proposed repair. An iterative algorithm computing a local minimum exists for 𝔻22, and the computational question remains open. So the two routes trade off exactly against each other: the mean landscape of Segment 1 always exists, is trivial to compute, and may be no diagram at all; the Fréchet mean is always a diagram, may not be unique, and may not be computable.

Self-check 5. Where exactly does the hypothesis "distinct values on all vertices" do its work in Proposition 13.9?

The vertex map ρ exists because fω is PL on a finite complex, via Theorem 3.16 and the lower-star filtration; distinctness is a separate hypothesis, and what it buys is that small changes in ω cannot reorder the vertices and so cannot repair the pairing to different vertices. Being PL is assumed outright, not derived from distinctness. And distinctness is about vertex values, not about diagonal points of the diagram.

Self-check 6. Which statement about averaging diagrams is correct?

Theorem 13.10 concludes 𝔼(ρ) ≠ ∅ and nothing more; Dey and Wang note the mean is in general not unique and describe the probabilistic Fréchet mean as the repair for settings where uniqueness matters. The third option overstates the motivation for landscapes: what 𝔻 lacks is inner-product structure, and the Fréchet construction shows a notion of mean is available in a metric space — it is just set-valued.

(1) Corollary 13.8. Fix t > 0 and τ ≥ π/2. By Proposition 13.7 the function (dFIM − τ) is negative definite on 𝔻, so by Theorem 13.4 the function e−t(dFIM−τ) is positive definite. Now

kPF(D,E) = e−t·dFIM(D,E) = e−tτ · e−t(dFIM(D,E) − τ) = α · e−t(dFIM(D,E) − τ),   α := e−tτ > 0.

Multiplying by a positive constant preserves positive definiteness: for any a1,…,an with Σai = 0, Σi,jaiaj·αk′(Di,Dj) = α·Σi,jaiajk′(Di,Dj) ≥ 0 since α > 0, and symmetry is untouched. Hence kPF is positive definite on 𝔻. ∎

(2) The gradient. Fix ω at which the hypotheses of Proposition 13.9 hold: K a finite simplicial complex, fω PL on |K| with distinct values on all vertices. Two facts are in play. First, unconditionally, bi = fω(ρω(bi)) = fω(vℓi): this is Theorem 3.16's identification of persistent points with pairs of vertices, available because fω is PL on a finite complex, so that the sublevel-set filtration is simulated by the lower star filtration. Second, by Proposition 13.9 there is a neighbourhood U of ω on which ξ = ρω(Dgm fω) is constant — the identity of the vertex vℓi does not change as the parameter varies within U.

So on U the function ω′ ↦ bi(ω′) is literally ω′ ↦ fω′(v) for one fixed vertex v = vℓi, and the composite that looked unmanageable — differentiate through a diagram — is just the evaluation of the parameterised function at a fixed point of the domain. Therefore

∂bi/∂ω = ∂fω(ρω(bi))/∂ω = ∂fω(vℓi)/∂ω,

and symmetrically ∂di/∂ω = ∂fω(vri)/∂ω. Combining with the analytic derivatives ∂𝒯/∂bi and ∂𝒯/∂di by the chain rule gives ∂𝒯/∂ω. Where each hypothesis is used: finiteness of K and fω PL give the vertex map ρ at all; distinct vertex values give the local constancy, since the lower-star filtration is built by sorting vertices by function value and a tie is exactly where that order — and hence the pairing — can change. ∎

(3) What is and is not established. Established: at any parameter ω satisfying the hypotheses, and provided ω′ ↦ fω′(v) is itself differentiable at ω for each vertex v, the birth and death coordinates are differentiable at ω and their derivatives are given by the displayed formula. That is a statement about one point of parameter space and a neighbourhood of it.

Not established: anything about the set B of parameters at which two vertices share a function value. Proposition 13.9 says nothing about B — not that it is closed, not that it is small, not that it is Lebesgue-null, and not that 𝒯 extends differentiably across it. To justify "differentiable almost everywhere" one would have to prove that B has Lebesgue measure zero in ℝm, which is a statement about the particular parameterisation ω ↦ fω and not about persistence at all: for a parameterisation in which two vertices are constrained to share a value, B is everything. The honest formulation is differentiable at generic parameters, in the sense of Proposition 13.9.

What this does and does not license. It licenses running gradient descent and reading the gradient formula as exact wherever the hypotheses hold — which is the whole engineering point, since it means no surrogate or smoothed persistence is needed. It does not license any claim about what happens at a tie, and in a pipeline where ties are structurally forced, the argument gives nothing.
