Cellular sheaves and sheaf cohomology (tda2-10)

A robot in a corridor measures distance in two directions. At a junction, the number of directions jumps. The data has no fixed dimension — so no coefficient group can hold it.

That is Ghrist's opening example, and it is a real defect in everything you have done so far. Cellular cohomology puts the same group on every cell. Fibre bundles do better but insist the fibre be locally constant. Neither can express "two numbers along a corridor, three at a junction, and here is how they must agree where the corridor meets the junction".

A cellular sheaf drops both restrictions: a group on each cell, chosen freely, and a homomorphism for each incidence saying how a value on a face is required to appear on the cell above it. The whole subject is then one question — which local assignments fit together globally — and the answer turns out to be a cohomology group you can compute with the linear algebra of la-06.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Take a cell complex X and put the same group 𝐆 on every cell, once with all restriction maps the identity (the constant sheaf 𝐆X) and once with all of them zero (the sum of skyscrapers ⊕σ𝐆σ). Every stalk agrees. Predict how the two compare.

Ghrist makes the point explicitly: ⊕σ𝐆σ ≠ 𝐆X "even though all the stalks agree: the restriction maps of this city full of skyscrapers are all zero". The sum-of-skyscrapers has value ⊕v𝐆 on X — an anarchic choice at each vertex, since the zero restrictions demand nothing on higher cells — while 𝐆X(X) ≅ 𝐆, because the identity maps force a perfect consensus among cells. The disagreement is loudest in grading zero, which is precisely where the third option says it cannot be. Restriction maps are not bookkeeping; they are the entire content.

Why this matters for the mission: A cellular sheaf assigns data to cells and restriction maps to incidences — cat-03's functors, now indexed by a cell complex. Its H^0 is exactly the space of global sections, the globally consistent assignments, so sheaf cohomology measures the obstruction to consistency and la-06's kernel/range theorem is literally the computation.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Data over a complex

Cellular sheaf. Fix X a regular cell complex with ◁ denoting the face relation: σ ◁ τ iff σ ⊂ τ̄. A cellular sheaf ℱ over X is generated by an assignment to each cell σ of an abelian group ℱ(σ), and to each face σ ◁ τ a restriction map — a homomorphism ℱ(σ ◁ τ) : ℱ(σ) → ℱ(τ) — such that faces of faces satisfy the composition rule

ρ ◁ σ ◁ τ   ⇒   ℱ(ρ ◁ τ) = ℱ(σ ◁ τ) ∘ ℱ(ρ ◁ σ).   (9.1)

The trivial face τ ◁ τ induces the identity, ℱ(τ ◁ τ) = Id. — Ghrist §9.1, pp. 180–181

Two remarks on the name and the shape. Ghrist footnotes that it does seem backwards to call this a restriction, the terminology coming from the topological perspective of §9.6 for which the cellular case acts as a nerve. And the composition rule (9.1) is exactly functoriality on the face poset — that is the sense in which cat-03's functors are being indexed by a cell complex — though Ghrist's own phrase is that this is "a sheaf as a representation of the face structure". The rule looks like bookkeeping. Segment 2's guided proof shows it is the only reason cohomology exists here at all.

The value ℱ(τ) is the group of local sections over τ, and one writes sτ ∈ ℱ(τ). The value on all of X collects the local sections that continue consistently:

ℱ(X) := { (sτ)τ∈X : sσ = ℱ(ρ ◁ σ)(sρ) ∀ ρ ◁ σ } ⊂ ∏τ ℱ(τ).   (9.2)

These are the global sections. For a point x ∈ X the stalk ℱx is simply ℱ(σ) for the unique cell σ in whose interior x lies.

The two extremes, and why they differ

The simplest sheaf is the constant sheaf 𝐆X: the group 𝐆 on every cell and the identity on every face relation. Every point has stalk 𝐆 and the global sections match the local ones, 𝐆X(X) ≅ 𝐆. The antipode is the skyscraper sheaf 𝐆σ: the group 𝐆 on the cell σ and zero on every other cell, with every restriction map zero apart from the identity σ ◁ σ. It has no nonzero global sections if dim σ > 0, but for a vertex v, 𝐆v(X) ≅ 𝐆.

Now the prediction gate. Summing skyscrapers over every cell gives ⊕σ∈X𝐆σ ≠ 𝐆X, even though the stalks all agree — the restriction maps of this city full of skyscrapers are all zero. Its value on X is ⊕v𝐆, since an arbitrary element at each vertex agrees, via zero maps, with the null on higher cells; whereas 𝐆X(X) ≅ 𝐆, the identities forcing consensus. Ghrist adds a third case worth keeping: if all stalks are ℤ, the restriction maps are multiplications by constants, which must factor according to (9.1); if none is zero and X is connected there are always nonzero global sections — a ℤ's worth — yet it is still not the case that local sections can always be patched into a global one.

Four sheaves that are not toys

Example 9.1 (recurrence equations). For un+1 = Anun with un ∈ ℝk, take the cell structure on ℝ with ℤ as vertices, put ℝk on every cell, let ℱ({n} ◁ (n, n+1)) be u ↦ Anu and ℱ({n+1} ◁ (n, n+1)) be the identity. Global sections then correspond precisely to solutions of the recurrence. Linearity is not optional: to be a sheaf of vector spaces the dynamics must be linear, so the solution space is a linear subspace.

Example 9.2 (local cohomology). Assign to σ the local cohomology Hk(X, X − σ̄), with restriction ℱ(σ ◁ τ) = H(ι) induced by the inclusion ι : (X, X − τ̄) ↪ (X, X − σ̄). On an n-manifold this yields a constant sheaf of dimension one, the orientation sheaf, and the manifold is orientable if and only if that sheaf has a global section. On a finite graph the local H1 sheaf has stalk dimension 1 on edges and deg(v) − 1 on each vertex v.

Example 9.3 (fibre homology). For a cellular f : X → Y, put ℱ(σ) = Hk(f−1σ̄) on each simplex σ of Y, with restrictions induced by f−1σ̄ ⊂ f−1τ̄. For a cellular height function h : X → ℝ this records the homology of level sets as stalks over the vertices of ℝ — the same level-set data that tda2-06 organised into a Reeb graph, here recorded instead as stalks along the line.

Example 9.4 (logic gates). Topologise an XOR gate as a directed Y-graph. Take 𝔽2-vector spaces with stalk dimension one everywhere except the central vertex, where it is two. The restrictions from the centre to the two input edges are the projections to the first and second factors; the restriction to the output edge is addition, + : 𝔽22 → 𝔽2. The global sections are then precisely the truth table. Ghrist notes the same trick fails for AND, since 𝔽22 → 𝔽2 encoded by AND is not a homomorphism — nor are NOT, OR, NOR or NAND.

Visual: the XOR sheaf and its global sections

Step through all four. A global section is determined by the value at the central vertex, and every one of the four values extends — so ℱ(X) ≅ 𝔽22, which is the truth table read as a vector space. Nothing here is an accident of the gate: it works because projection and addition are homomorphisms, and it is exactly what fails for AND.

Self-check 1. Why does the XOR construction fail for an AND gate?

A cellular sheaf's restriction maps are homomorphisms by definition, and AND is not additive — Ghrist lists NOT, OR, NOR and NAND as failing for the same reason. AND is surjective onto 𝔽2, so the second option is false on its own terms. Stalk dimension is not the obstruction: two inputs need dimension two whichever gate one wants.

Self-check 2. A sheaf on a connected X has every stalk ℤ and every restriction map multiplication by a nonzero integer. What follows?

Ghrist states both halves: the constants must factor according to the composition rule, and given that none is zero and X is connected there is a ℤ's worth of global sections, yet local sections need not patch. Injectivity of each map does not make extension automatic — a local section must satisfy every constraint along every path, and the multipliers can be incompatible. Nor does it make the sheaf constant, which would need every multiplier to be 1.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Cohomology with coefficients that move

Read a sheaf as a coefficient system: ℱ(σ) is the coefficient group over σ, and the restriction maps encode the switching required to glue local cochains into global cocycles. Then the construction is the one from at2-03 with the coefficients allowed to vary. For ℱ over a compact cell complex X, let Cn(X; ℱ) be the product of ℱ(σ) over all n-cells:

0 ⟶ ∏dim σ=0 ℱ(σ) —d→ ∏dim σ=1 ℱ(σ) —d→ ∏dim σ=2 ℱ(σ) —d→ ⋯   (9.3)

with coboundary

d(σ) := Σσ◁τ [σ : τ] ℱ(σ ◁ τ).   (9.4)

Since [σ : τ] = 0 unless σ is a codimension-1 face of τ, this maps Cn to Cn+1. The resulting cellular sheaf cohomology is H•(X; ℱ). Two sanity checks are immediate and both were the prediction gate's subject: the cohomology of the constant sheaf 𝐆X is H•cell(X; 𝐆), and a skyscraper 𝐆σ has a cochain complex with d = 0 throughout, so its cohomology equals its cochain complex — vanishing except in grading dim σ, where it has rank one.

Lemma 9.5. Sheaf cohomology in grading zero classifies global sections:

H0(X; ℱ) = ℱ(X).   (9.5)

— Ghrist §9.3, Lemma 9.5, p. 184

Ghrist anticipates the beginner's objection: is the data on higher-dimensional cells ignored, since ℱ(X) is determined by vertices and edges? No — the compatibility on higher cells is built into the definition of a sheaf. His analogy is a planar tiling by square tiles with coloured edges: place tiles at the vertices of a cubical complex and demand that incident tiles have compatible edges, and the legal tilings of the plane are classified completely by vertex and edge data, with no additional constraints where four corners meet.

Worked example: the sheaf that is a matrix

Example 9.6 (matrix equations). Take the closed interval with vertices a, b and edge e. Set ℱ(a) = ℝm, ℱ(b) = 0, ℱ(e) = ℝn, with ℱ(b ◁ e) = 0 and ℱ(a ◁ e) = A a linear transformation.

Step 1 — write the complex. C0(X; ℱ) = ℱ(a) × ℱ(b) = ℝm and C1(X; ℱ) = ℱ(e) = ℝn; everything above grading 1 is zero because X has no 2-cells. By (9.4) the coboundary of a 0-cochain x is [a:e]Ax + [b:e]·0 = ±Ax, the sign coming from the incidence number and the orientation of e.

Step 2 — read off the cohomology. A two-term complex has H0 = ker d and H1 = coker d, and the sign changes neither. So, by definition, H0 ≅ ker A and H1 ≅ coker A.

Step 3 — say what that means. H0 = ker A is the failure of uniqueness for Ax = y: the homogeneous solutions. H1 = coker A is the obstruction to solvability: Ax = y has a solution exactly when y vanishes in coker A. This is the sense in which sheaf cohomology is an obstruction theory, and it is the mission strip's claim made literal — la-06's kernel and range are not an analogy for H0 and H1 here, they are the same two spaces.

Step 4 — two vertices instead of one. Ghrist continues: replace ℱ(b) = 0 by ℝp with ℱ(b ◁ e) = B. Then the complex is

0 ⟶ ℝm × ℝp —[A | −B]→ ℝn ⟶ 0 ⟶ ⋯

and H0 is precisely the set of solutions to Ax = By for x ∈ ℝm and y ∈ ℝp — the global sections over the closed edge. H1 measures the degree to which Ax − By fails to span ℝn. All higher groups vanish.

Step 5 — the general moral. Cellular sheaf cohomology valued in vector spaces is, in Ghrist's words, really a characterisation of solutions to complex networks of linear equations. Each cell contributes unknowns, each incidence contributes an equation, and the complex assembles them. What cohomology adds to plain linear algebra is that the assembly respects the topology of X — which is why Segment 3's flow theorem can be read off a long exact sequence.

Relative sections

Since sheaves are a coefficient system, the machinery of exact sequences carries over. A short exact sequence of sheaves 0 → ℱ → 𝒢 → ℋ → 0, exactness enforced cell-by-cell, yields a connecting homomorphism and a long exact sequence

⟶ Hn−1(X; ℋ) —δ→ Hn(X; ℱ) ⟶ Hn(X; 𝒢) ⟶ Hn(X; ℋ) ⟶   (9.6)

For a closed subcomplex A ⊂ X let ℱA be the restriction of ℱ to A — stalks zero off A, and all restriction maps set to zero unless both cells lie in A — and ℱX−A the complementary restriction, vanishing on A. Then 0 → ℱX−A → ℱ → ℱA → 0 is exact, and (9.6) gives the long exact sequence of the pair, with the notation H•(A; ℱ) := H•(X; ℱA) and H•(X, A; ℱ) := H•(X; ℱX−A).

Corollary 9.7. For A a subcomplex of X, H0(X, A; ℱ) classifies global sections of ℱ on X which vanish on A. — Ghrist §9.3, Corollary 9.7, p. 186

Ghrist's gloss is the one to remember: this interpretation is in keeping with the notion of cohomology as an obstruction to solving certain problems of extension and restriction.

Worked example (faded): a relative computation. Take X the interval with a, b, e and the sheaf with all stalks ℝ and both restrictions the identity, and let A = {a}.

Absolute first. C0 = ℝ², C1 = ℝ, and orienting e from a to b, d(xa, xb) = xb − xa with matrix (−1   1). So H0 = {(x,x)} ≅ ℝ and H1 = ⟨your step: what group?⟩. By Lemma 9.5 the H0 answer says a global section is a single real number, agreed at both ends.

Now relative. ℱX−A has stalk 0 at a and ℝ at b and at e, so C0(X, A; ℱ) = ℱ(b) = ℝ and C1(X, A; ℱ) = ℱ(e) = ℝ, with d(xb) = xb, the a-contribution having been sent to zero. Hence H0(X, A; ℱ) = ker d = ⟨your step: what group?⟩.

Check against Corollary 9.7. A global section is a constant (x, x); requiring it to vanish at a forces x = 0, leaving only the zero section. The two computations agree, which is the point of doing both.

Gap solutions: H1 = coker d = ℝ/ℝ = 0; and H0(X, A; ℱ) = 0.

Guided proof

Prompt. Ghrist writes that (9.3) is a cochain complex because "in the computation of d², the incidence numbers factor from the restriction maps, and the computation from cellular co/homology suffices to yield 0". Unpack that. (1) Write out (d²x)υ for a cochain x and a cell υ of dimension n+2. (2) Show that the composition rule (9.1) is exactly what lets the restriction maps be pulled out of the inner sum, and finish the proof that d² = 0. (3) Say what would go wrong if (9.1) were dropped.

Hint: you may use the identity Στ[σ:τ][τ:υ] = 0 from cellular cohomology without proof — that is the "computation from cellular co/homology" Ghrist is invoking.

Click to reveal model answer

(1) Expand. Let x ∈ Cn(X; ℱ), so x assigns xσ ∈ ℱ(σ) to each n-cell σ. By (9.4), for an (n+1)-cell τ,

(dx)τ = Σσ ◁ τ [σ:τ] ℱ(σ ◁ τ)(xσ),

the sum over codimension-1 faces σ of τ. Applying d again, for an (n+2)-cell υ,

(d²x)υ = Στ ◁ υ [τ:υ] ℱ(τ ◁ υ)( Σσ ◁ τ [σ:τ] ℱ(σ ◁ τ)(xσ) ).

Since ℱ(τ ◁ υ) is a homomorphism it passes through the inner sum, and the double sum may be reordered over pairs (σ, τ) with σ ◁ τ ◁ υ:

(d²x)υ = Σσ Στ : σ◁τ◁υ [σ:τ][τ:υ] · ( ℱ(τ ◁ υ) ∘ ℱ(σ ◁ τ) )(xσ).

(2) Factor, using (9.1). The obstruction to simplifying is that the operator in the summand still depends on the intermediate cell τ. This is exactly what the composition rule removes: σ ◁ τ ◁ υ gives ℱ(τ ◁ υ) ∘ ℱ(σ ◁ τ) = ℱ(σ ◁ υ), which does not mention τ. So the operator is constant across the inner sum and factors out:

(d²x)υ = Σσ ( Στ : σ◁τ◁υ [σ:τ][τ:υ] ) · ℱ(σ ◁ υ)(xσ).

The bracketed scalar is the incidence sum of cellular cohomology, which vanishes for every pair σ ◁ υ of codimension 2. Every term is therefore ℱ(σ ◁ υ)(xσ) multiplied by 0, so (d²x)υ = 0 for every υ, and d² = 0. ∎

(3) Without (9.1). Drop the composition rule and the summand keeps its τ-dependence. One would be summing operators ℱ(τ ◁ υ) ∘ ℱ(σ ◁ τ) that differ from one τ to the next, weighted by [σ:τ][τ:υ]; those weights sum to zero, but a zero-sum combination of different operators is not the zero operator. Concretely, on a square with two paths from a vertex to the 2-cell, the two composites could be any two homomorphisms whatever, and their difference would be an arbitrary element of ℱ(υ). So d² would not vanish, (9.3) would not be a cochain complex, and there would be no cohomology to compute.

What this does and does not license. It shows (9.1) is load-bearing, not hygiene: it is precisely the hypothesis that makes the incidence numbers separate from the algebra. It does not say the sheaf axioms are the weakest possible — one only needs the composites to agree along codimension-2 pairs — but agreement for all faces of faces is the clean statement, and it is what Ghrist takes as the definition.

Self-check 3. Which statement about the constant sheaf is correct?

Identical complexes have identical cohomology in every grading, so the agreement is not confined to H0 — this is the precise sense in which at2-03's theory is the special case of the present one in which the coefficients do not vary. The third option is the prediction gate's trap: those two sheaves have the same stalks and different restriction maps, and Ghrist writes the inequality out.

Self-check 4. H0(X, A; ℱ) = 0 for some subcomplex A. What does that say?

Corollary 9.7 identifies H0(X, A; ℱ) with the sections vanishing on A, so its vanishing says only that no nonzero section does — equivalently that the restriction map to A is injective on global sections. It says nothing about whether sections exist: the faded example has H0(X; ℱ) ≅ ℝ and H0(X, A; ℱ) = 0 simultaneously. And for A empty the relative cohomology is the absolute one, which was ℝ, not 0.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Flow sheaves, cuts, and network coding

Even a sheaf over a network — a 1-dimensional complex — has real applications. Take a directed acyclic network X from a source s to a target t with capacities cap(e) ∈ ℕ. A flow φ : E(X) → ℕ assigns rates 0 ≤ φ(e) ≤ cap(e) conservatively: at each node v,

Σe→v φ(e) = Σv→e′ φ(e′) =: φv,   (9.7)

and the flow value val(φ) is the net flow out of s, equivalently into t.

To turn this into a sheaf, fix a capacity-respecting φ and choose a consistent routing protocol: at each vertex v a binary matrix Ψv : ⊕e→v ℝcap(e) → ⊕v→e′ ℝcap(e′) in which (Ψv)i,j = 1 means the jth unit of incoming flow is sent to the ith unit of outgoing flow, conservation being imposed by requiring Ψv to be a permutation matrix padded with zero rows and columns. Then subdivide X to X̃: split each edge in two with an extra middle vertex, give each such vertex trivial routing Ψv = Id, and add a single feedback edge from t to s of sufficiently large capacity.

The flow sheaf ℱ on X̃ has ℱ(e) = ℝcap(e) on each edge and ℱ(v) = ℝcap(v) at each vertex, where cap(v) is the sum of cap(e′) over incoming edges e′ → v. The restrictions enforce conservation: for an incoming e → v, ℱ(v ◁ e) is the projection to the ℝcap(e)-factor of ℱ(v); for an outgoing v → e, it is the projection of the image of Ψv to the ℝcap(e)-factor.

Lemma 9.8. For a flow-sheaf ℱ as above, H0(X̃; ℱ) ≅ H1(X̃; ℱ) and dim H0(X̃; ℱ) equals the net flow value val(φ). — Ghrist §9.4, Lemma 9.8, p. 187

Ghrist's proof. The simplest proof of this duality is by counting: C0(X̃; ℱ) ≅ C1(X̃; ℱ), since each vertex has stalk isomorphic to the sum of the stalks of its incoming edges, and this enumeration exhausts all cells of X̃. Passing to cohomology, the isomorphism comes from exactness — dim ker d = dim coker d. For the second clause, Lemma 9.5 identifies H0(X̃; ℱ) with the space of global sections, and by conservation each independent section counts a unit of flow circulating from source to target. ∎ (Ghrist's sentence reads "dim H0 equals the number of global sections"; the dimension of the space of global sections is what is meant, and is what Lemma 9.5 supplies.)

What "Poincaré duality" is doing in that sentence. Ghrist introduces Lemma 9.8 by saying the flow sheaf "satisfies a type of Poincaré duality", and the phrase deserves care. The manifold duality theorem of at2-07 needs a closed R-orientable n-manifold and produces its isomorphism via cap product with a fundamental class. Lemma 9.8 needs none of that — no manifold, no orientation, no fundamental class — and its isomorphism is not induced by any pairing; it is a dimension count on a two-term complex. What the two share is the shape Hk ≅ Hn−k, not a mechanism. Note also where the counting step gets its force: X̃ is one-dimensional, so the complex stops at C1 and dim H0 − dim H1 = dim C0 − dim C1 by rank–nullity. For a longer complex, equal dimensions in two gradings would say nothing about their cohomology, because the missing terms could absorb the difference.

Max-flow min-cut, read off a long exact sequence

Let C ⊂ E(X) be a putative cut-set. C is not a subcomplex, so take instead C̃ ⊂ X̃, the subcomplex of refined midpoint vertices of C. The long exact sequence of the pair (X̃, C̃) in ℱ-cohomology is

0 ⟶ H0(X̃, C̃) ⟶ H0(X̃) ⟶ H0(C̃) —δ→ H1(X̃, C̃) ⟶ H1(X̃) ⟶ 0.

By definition dim H0(C̃; ℱ) = Σe∈C cap(e) =: val(C), the net capacity of C; and by Lemma 9.8 both H0(X̃; ℱ) and H1(X̃; ℱ) have dimension val(φ). By Corollary 9.7, H0(X̃, C̃) measures how much of the flow does not pass through C — so if C is a cut then H0(X̃, C̃) = 0, and this relative cohomology is the obstruction to being a cut. For C a cut the map H0(X̃) → H0(C̃) is injective by exactness, giving val(φ) ≤ val(C) for any flow and cut: the weak form of max-flow min-cut. Ghrist's summary is worth memorising as a table:

(1) dim H0(X̃; ℱ) = val(φ) is the flow value; (2) dim H0(C̃; ℱ) = val(C) is the cut value; (3) H0(X̃, C̃; ℱ) is the obstruction to being a cut; and (4) δ is the obstruction to val(φ) = val(C). — Ghrist §9.4, p. 188

When δ ≠ 0 the cut is not minimal or the flow is not maximal; when both obstructions vanish, dim H0(X̃; ℱ) = dim H0(C̃; ℱ) — maxflow equals mincut. Ghrist concedes that flow sheaves may look like an over-complicated restatement of "net inputs equal net outputs", and gives the reason to bother: adapting sheaves to this elementary setting is what lets one generalise properly. §9.5 is the generalisation.

Network coding

Example 9.9 (butterfly network). One source s, two targets t1, t2, every edge of unit capacity, and the goal of sending two packets a, b ∈ 𝔽q to both targets. If the nodes merely route — redirecting data unchanged — at least five time units are needed, since one central node must switch from transmitting a to transmitting b. If instead the central node receives both and transmits a + b, then in four steps t1 receives a and a + b while t2 receives b and a + b, and the originals are decoded algebraically at the targets.

In general, to each node v is associated a local coding map Ψv : ⊕e→v 𝔽cap(e) → ⊕v→e′ 𝔽cap(e′) taking the data entering v to the data leaving it; routing protocols are the zero-padded permutation matrices of §9.4, and the butterfly's central node has Ψv = [1|1]. Decoding at ti is given by a decoding edge ti → s with map Ψti. Building X̃ by subdividing and adding the decoding edges, the network coding sheaf is defined exactly as the flow sheaf was, with 𝔽 in place of ℝ.

Theorem 9.10. With a network coding sheaf ℱ for single-source-multi-target networks with decoding edges as above, H∗(X̃; ℱ) has the following interpretations: (1) the net information flow rate equals dim H0(X̃; ℱ); (2) the net information flow rate which persists if a subnetwork A becomes inoperative equals dim H0(X̃, Ã; ℱ); (3) the obstruction to extending an information flow on a subnetwork A to all of X respecting the coding and capacity constraints is δ : H1(X̃, Ã; ℱ) → H1(X̃; ℱ), and if δ = 0 all information flows on A are extendable to X. — Ghrist §9.5, Theorem 9.10, p. 189

Read clause (2) against clause (3). The first is a robustness statement — how much throughput survives losing part of the network — and it is a dimension, computable. The second is an extension statement, and it is a map: not a number but the connecting homomorphism itself, whose vanishing is the condition. That is the general shape of an obstruction theory, and it is why Corollary 9.7's gloss about extension and restriction was worth stating in Segment 2.

Self-check 5. In Lemma 9.8's proof, which feature of X̃ makes the passage from cochain groups to cohomology valid?

An isomorphism of cochain groups does not in general give an isomorphism of cohomology — for a longer complex the terms above could absorb the difference. Here the complex stops at C1, so the alternating sums of cochain and cohomology dimensions agree and equal cochain dimensions force equal cohomology dimensions. Surjectivity of d would make H1 = 0, which is not claimed; and capacity-respecting flow is a hypothesis on φ, not a statement about d.

Self-check 6. In the max-flow min-cut sequence, what is δ measuring?

Ghrist's four-item summary assigns the roles precisely: H0(X̃, C̃; ℱ) is the obstruction to being a cut, and δ is the obstruction to the flow and cut values agreeing. He adds the reading of a nonzero image class: it is a local section on C̃ that comes from no global flow. The cut value is a dimension already named in item (2), not something δ measures.

Exit ritual: blank-page reconstruction

Close the page, look away, and reconstruct the argument skeleton — three load-bearing claims and why each forces the next:

  1. What a cellular sheaf is, and which part of the data does the work the stalks cannot.
  2. How the cochain complex is built, why it is a complex at all, and what H0 turns out to be.
  3. What a flow sheaf makes computable, and which of its results is a genuine duality theorem.
Check your mental skeleton

If you can say: (1) a group on each cell and a homomorphism for each incidence, subject to the composition rule (9.1), with the global sections ℱ(X) of (9.2) the compatible assignments — and the restriction maps carrying everything, since the constant sheaf and the city of skyscrapers have identical stalks and utterly different sections; (2) Cn is the product of stalks over n-cells and d(σ) = Σ[σ:τ]ℱ(σ ◁ τ), which squares to zero precisely because (9.1) lets the restriction maps factor out of the incidence sum; the constant sheaf recovers at2-03's cellular cohomology, a skyscraper gives rank one in its own grading, Lemma 9.5 says H0 = ℱ(X), Example 9.6 makes H0 = ker A and H1 = coker A, and Corollary 9.7 reads relative H0 as the sections vanishing on A; (3) the flow sheaf on the subdivided X̃ has dim H0 = val(φ) by Lemma 9.8 — a counting argument valid because the complex is two-term, and a duality only by analogy of shape — with the long exact sequence of (X̃, C̃) giving the weak max-flow min-cut theorem and δ as the obstruction to equality, and Theorem 9.10 generalising all of it to network coding — then you have the lesson.
