Cosheaves, categorification, and interleaving revisited (tda2-11)

"These two persistence modules are within ε of each other" is an inequality. Category theory replaces it with a pair of actual maps — and the inequality becomes a theorem about them.

That substitution is the move the whole subject has been making. A number becomes an object; a comparison becomes a morphism; a claim that two things are close becomes a diagram that commutes. tda1-06 defined the interleaving distance by writing down shifted homomorphisms and checking their composites; Ghrist's §10.6 shows those homomorphisms were the components of natural transformations all along, and that the definition never needed persistence modules in the first place.

Before that, one piece of unfinished business from tda2-10. Sheaves are built for cohomology. Turn every arrow round and you get cosheaves, which are built for homology — and the skyscrapers, which behaved so simply for sheaves, flip over in a way worth watching.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. §10.6 recasts the ε-interleaving as a pair of natural transformations. Predict how far that reformulation generalises, as §10.6 actually states it.

Ghrist asks "what is the interleaving distance of arbitrary functors (ℝ, ≤) → ℂ for ℂ a category?" — the quantifier is on ℂ, and (ℝ, ≤) sits fixed on the left of the arrow, as it must, since the shift Tε and its natural transformation τε of Example 10.10 are constructions in (ℝ, ≤). That freedom in the target is exactly what the third option denies and is the substance of the reformulation: it is what lets the proof of Theorem 10.15 interleave spaces and then push the interleaving through Hk. The first option is the over-reach, and the warn block in Segment 3 says what would actually have to change.

Why this matters for the mission: Cosheaves dualise sheaves and recover homology-flavoured local-to-global statements. Recast categorically, an epsilon-interleaving is a pair of natural transformations (cat-04) between functors shifted by epsilon, which is what makes tda1-06's interleaving distance a definition rather than a construction — and it then generalises unchanged to sheaves, Reeb graphs and multiparameter modules.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Cosheaves: every arrow the other way

Ghrist opens §9.10 with the obvious suspicion: sheaf theory is built for cohomology, so the homological counterpart should exist and be by no means unimportant. That dual is the cosheaf.

Cosheaf. For a cell complex X, a cosheaf ℱ̂ assigns to each cell σ an abelian group ℱ̂(σ) and to each face pair σ ◁ τ an extension map — a homomorphism ℱ̂(σ ◁ τ) which sends ℱ̂(τ) → ℱ̂(σ) and composes properly: for ρ ◁ σ ◁ τ, ℱ̂(ρ ◁ τ) = ℱ̂(ρ ◁ σ) ∘ ℱ̂(σ ◁ τ). The data ℱ̂(σ) over a cell is the costalk. — Ghrist §9.10, p. 198

One imagines extending data from cells down to faces; note how this flips the directions in the definition of a sheaf, and that the co- prefix is connoting exactly that contravariance. The value on all of X is the analogue of tda2-10's Eq. (9.2):

ℱ̂(X) := ⊕τ ℱ̂(τ) / ∼  :   sρ ∼ ℱ̂(ρ ◁ σ)(sσ).   (9.14)

Ghrist concedes this is much harder to parse than the global-sections intuition for a sheaf, and the reason is structural rather than notational. A sheaf's ℱ(X) is a subgroup of the product ∏τℱ(τ), cut out by equations. A cosheaf's ℱ̂(X) is a quotient of the direct sum, cut out by an equivalence: an equivalence class of data assignments to cells, where the equivalence is up to compatible extensions that fall from higher-dimensional to lower-dimensional cells. Sub-of-a-product versus quotient-of-a-sum is the same dualisation that separates cohomology from homology, and it is worth carrying that sentence forward.

Worked example (faded): the skyscrapers flip. Recall tda2-10's skyscraper sheaf 𝐆σ — the group 𝐆 on σ, zero elsewhere, all restrictions zero. Its nonzero global sections exist only when σ is a ⟨your step: which extreme of the face poset?⟩, that is a vertex, and 𝐆v(X) ≅ 𝐆.

Now the skyscraper cosheaf. ℱ̂ = 𝐆̂σ assigns 𝐆 to σ and 0 to all other cells. Its value over X is zero unless σ has no cofaces — unless it is locally top-dimensional — in which case 𝐆̂σ(X) ≅ 𝐆.

Why the flip. For a sheaf, a global section must restrict upward consistently, and a nonzero value on σ is forced to land in the zero stalks above it unless there is nothing above; for a cosheaf the extension maps run downward, so the same argument runs in the opposite direction of the poset. In one sentence: for skyscraper sheaves only ⟨your step: which extrema?⟩ of the face poset contribute globally, and for skyscraper cosheaves only maxima.

Gap solutions: a minimum of the face poset; and minima.

Homology, as promised

The cellular cosheaf chain complex is the obvious one:

⋯ —∂→ ⊕dim σ=2 ℱ̂(σ) —∂→ ⊕dim σ=1 ℱ̂(σ) —∂→ ⊕dim σ=0 ℱ̂(σ) ⟶ 0   (9.15)

with boundary map

∂(τ) = Σσ ≤ τ [σ : τ] ℱ̂(σ ≤ τ).   (9.16)

Here ∂ decreases the grading by one and ∂² = 0, as a boundary operator must — the same factoring argument as in tda2-10's guided proof, now with the composition rule read the other way. The resulting cosheaf homology H•(X; ℱ̂) gives a collation and classification of homological features in the data structure.

The two calibration cases behave as they should. For the constant cosheaf 𝐆X, cosheaf homology agrees with cellular homology with 𝐆 coefficients. For a skyscraper cosheaf ℱ̂σ, the homology vanishes unless σ is a maximal cell in the face poset, in which case it is 𝐆 in grading dim σ. Ghrist's comment on the pair is worth keeping: this duality between dimensions and sheaf cohomology / cosheaf homology on skyscrapers is reminiscent of the delicacies of duality between homology and compactly supported cohomology.

Example 9.18 (precosheaves). In the topological setting a precosheaf assigns data to open sets U ⊂ X, with extension map ℱ̂(V) → ℱ̂(U) for V ⊂ U. To be a cosheaf it must behave well under limits of covers: the sequence

⊕i,j ℱ̂(Uij) —∂→ ⊕k ℱ̂(Uk) ⟶ ℱ̂(U) ⟶ 0   (9.17)

must be exact for any finite open cover 𝒰 = {Ui} of U — equivalently, ℱ̂(U) agrees with the cellular cosheaf homology H0(𝒩(𝒰); ℱ̂) for any cover 𝒰. — Ghrist §9.10, Example 9.18, p. 200

Ghrist attaches a warning that a practitioner should not skip: in practice this agreement cannot be assumed and can be challenging to confirm. It is the same shape of caution as tda2-07's good-cover hypothesis — the condition that makes the local-to-global machine work is exactly the one nobody checks.

Where do cosheaves come from? Taking a hint from Poincaré duality, from functions of compact support. On a manifold M the precosheaf ℱ̂(U) = Cc(U, ℝ) of compactly supported continuous functions, with extension-by-zero from V to U ⊃ V, is a cosheaf; and the sheaf of differential p-forms Ωp is complemented by the cosheaf of p-currents Ωp. Example 9.19 gives another: for f : X → Y with Y locally connected, the precosheaf assigning Hk(f−1(U)) to U ⊂ Y is a cosheaf, with extension from the induced homomorphism on Hk — the homological twin of tda2-10's fibre homology sheaf.

Finally, the example that names the duality. Example 9.17 (sensing and inference). For sensors xi ∈ 𝒬 each returning attributes S(xi) about a fixed object, take the simplex X on the sensor nodes. The sheaf assigns to each vertex the vector space with basis S(xi) — the target is grey and large; the target has a long nose — with restriction maps the inclusions into the unions over the vertices of a simplex, and its global sections give the full sensorium. Dually, the cosheaf assigns to each vertex the vector space whose basis is all objects consistent with S(xi) — rhinoceros, elephant, whale; anteater, elephant — with extension maps the projections onto the subbasis generated by intersection: elephant. Its global sections return the constraint satisfactions consistent with the sensing. Sensing yields a sheaf; inference is a cosheaf.

Self-check 1. What is the structural difference between Eq. (9.2) and Eq. (9.14)?

Sub-of-a-product versus quotient-of-a-sum is the whole difference, and it is why Ghrist says (9.14) is harder to parse: there is no "the global sections are the tuples that agree" reading, only "data assignments up to compatible extension". The second option loses exactly the content. The third confuses a computation about skyscrapers with the definition — both formulas range over all cells τ.

Self-check 2. Why does ∂ in (9.16) lower the grading where the sheaf coboundary raised it?

A differential can only compose the maps it has. ℱ̂(σ ≤ τ) goes from ℱ̂(τ) to ℱ̂(σ), so summing over the faces of τ produces something in the lower grading. The convention in the second option is downstream of that fact, not a substitute for it, and the incidence numbers are the same combinatorial data in both settings.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Categories, and maps between functors

Category. A category ℂ consists of: (1) a collection of objects 𝒪; (2) for each ordered pair (a,b) in 𝒪, a set of morphisms ℳ(a,b); and (3) for each ordered triple (a,b,c) a composition ∘ : ℳ(a,b) × ℳ(b,c) → ℳ(a,c). These satisfy associativity — composition of morphisms is associative — and identity: for each a ∈ 𝒪 there is Id ∈ ℳ(a,a) with f ∘ Id = f for all f ∈ ℳ(a,b) and Id ∘ g = g for all g ∈ ℳ(b,a). — Ghrist §10.1, p. 208

Ghrist warns that the word is so generic as to be uninformative, is unrelated to the LS category of his Chapter 7, and was chosen to evoke an Aristotelian organisation — "deceptively unexciting". The redeeming picture is combinatorial: a small category is a directed graph whose vertices are objects and whose arrows from a to b are the elements of ℳ(a,b), with a loop-like identity at each vertex. Composition is then visualised by a 2-simplex whose boundary is the commutative triangle, and associativity by the wireframe of a 3-simplex. Seeing the algebraic laws in their simplicial guise is, as he puts it, the subject's first hint at its relevance to topology. The limitation is honest: very small categories are easily visualised, but singular chain complexes demand too much of the Apollonian seer.

The standard examples — Vect, Grp, Ab, GrAb, Top, hTop, Man, Set, FinSet, ChCo, Pos — are too large to draw. The smaller ones are the ones this semester has been using without saying so:

  • OpX: objects the open sets of a space X, including the empty set, with morphisms V → U the inclusions V ⊂ U.
  • FaceX: for a regular cell complex X, objects the cells, with a morphism σ → τ iff σ is a face of τ. This is tda2-10's index. A cellular sheaf is a functor FaceX → Ab, and the composition rule (9.1) that made d² = 0 possible is precisely functoriality; a cosheaf is a functor on the opposite category.
  • Any poset (P, ≤) is a category, with a morphism a → b iff a ≤ b — and the previous two are special cases. This is where (ℝ, ≤) is going to come from in Segment 3.
  • A group 𝐆 is a category with one object ⋆ whose morphisms ⋆ → ⋆ are the elements of 𝐆, composition being the group operation, with every morphism invertible. A groupoid is a category with invertible morphisms and a monoid is a category with a single object — so a group is a single-object groupoid, equally a monoid-with-inverses.

Example 10.1 supplies one more that will look familiar: the categorical n-simplex is the category [n] := 0 → 1 → 2 → ⋯ → n−1 → n, whose objects are the ordinals through n and whose morphisms come from the total order. There are more morphisms than the displayed generators, since composition must be applied.

Natural transformations

Ghrist says the reader may suspect a joke when told that the truly interesting objects of category theory are neither morphisms nor functors but correspondences between functors. It is asserted in all seriousness — such a construction was the original impetus for creating category theory at all.

Natural transformation. η : ℱ ⇒ ℱ′ connects a pair of functors ℱ, ℱ′ : ℂ → ℂ′ by sending each object a ∈ ℂ to a morphism η(a) : ℱ(a) → ℱ′(a) so that for each morphism h : a → b in ℂ the square

ℱ(a) —ℱ(h)→ ℱ(b)    with    η(a) ↓   η(b) ↓    over    ℱ′(a) —ℱ′(h)→ ℱ′(b)

commutes. — Ghrist §10.5, p. 217

His better formulation: a natural transformation is a functor in the Category of categories. Categories themselves are the Objects of a Category whose Morphisms are functors, under functor composition and with the identity functor playing the obvious role; the Functors of that Category relate a pair of Morphisms, and in the original category that is precisely a natural transformation. Natural transformations are composable, and one may also compose a natural transformation η with a functor ℋ on the left, ℋ·η, or on the right, η·ℋ — a fact Segment 3 will need in the very statement of an interleaving.

Worked example: four naturalities, and one failure.

Example 10.10 (translation). Regard (ℝ, ≤) as a category: one morphism a → b whenever a ≤ b. Translation by ε is a functor Tε : ℝ → ℝ sending a ↦ a + ε and preserving the order, hence morphisms. It does very little — just a shift in objects — so it is no surprise that there is a natural transformation τε : Id ⇒ Tε taking (a ≤ b) to (a + ε ≤ b + ε). Naturality here is the indifference to whether one ≤-compares objects before or after the translation. Keep this example; it is the whole of Segment 3's machinery.

Example 10.11 (snakes). The Snake Lemma gives a connecting homomorphism δ : H•(𝒞) → H•−1(𝒜) from a short exact sequence. What makes it powerful is the naturality of the resulting long exact sequence — and that is exactly the statement that δ is a natural transformation, on the category ChCoSES of short exact sequences of chain complexes, between the two homology functors to GrAb.

Example 10.12 (co/homology equivalences). The several homology theories are not merely isomorphic but naturally so: maps between cell complexes induce the same homomorphism whichever theory is used. A natural isomorphism between functors is an inverse pair of natural transformations whose composites both ways are the identity natural transformation. Ghrist: natural isomorphisms are fundamental.

Example 10.14 (duals and isomorphisms). The failure that makes the notion sharp. On FinVect the dual-space functor 𝒟 sends V ↦ V∨. Although V and V∨ are isomorphic as vector spaces, that isomorphism is not natural — and "not natural" is a precise statement, not a complaint about bases: there is no natural isomorphism 𝒟 ⇒ Id at all. It is true, however, that every V is naturally isomorphic to its double dual: there is a natural isomorphism η : 𝒟² ⇒ Id.

The payoff. Categories are isomorphic, ℂ ≅ 𝔻, when there are functors both ways whose composites equal the identities. Ghrist notes this is rarely satisfied even for categories that seem very closely related. More common and more useful is that there are not equalities but natural transformations 𝒢·ℱ ⇒ Idℂ and ℱ·𝒢 ⇒ Id𝔻, in which case ℂ ≈ 𝔻 are equivalent categories — analogous to the way homotopic spaces are topologically equivalent though not necessarily homeomorphic. That downgrade from equality to a natural transformation is the same move Segment 3 makes to define a distance.

Self-check 3. A cellular sheaf on X is a functor. From which category, and to which?

Ghrist lists FaceX among the smaller categories, and the cellular sheaves of Chapter 9 are exactly functors on it — the composition rule (9.1) is the functor axiom, though Ghrist words it as a sheaf being "a representation of the face structure" rather than using the term. OpX is the right index for a topological sheaf, which is his §9.6, not the cellular case. The third option has the arrow backwards: a sheaf assigns a group to each cell.

Self-check 4. Why is Example 10.14's failure the right way to understand naturality?

The example is instructive exactly because the objectwise statement is true — every finite-dimensional V is isomorphic to its dual — and the natural statement is nonetheless false. That is what a naturality square demands: one choice per object, compatible with every morphism simultaneously. The second option changes the example, since Ghrist states it on FinVect; the third is beside the point, and 𝒟² is covariant and does admit a natural isomorphism to Id.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Interleaving, said twice

Consider persistence over ℝ: a family {Xt : t ∈ ℝ} of spaces with inclusions Xa ⊂ Xb for a ≤ b, thought of as the lower excursion sets Xt = {h ≤ t} of a height function h : X → ℝ. The stability question is: for h′ close to h, how much can the topology of the excursion sets change? Any individual Xt can change dramatically under a small perturbation; the content of the Stability Theorem is that the impact on persistent homology is small. That needs a notion of proximity.

ε-interleaving (the concrete version). Given two ℝ-indexed homology sequences Hk(Xt) and Hk(X′t), they are ε-interleaved if there exist homomorphisms φt : Hk(Xt) → Hk(X′t+ε) and φ′t : Hk(X′t) → Hk(Xt+ε) such that φ′t ∘ φt and φt ∘ φ′t are each the inclusion maps on homology induced by the shift +2ε. — Ghrist §10.6, p. 219

Visual: the two triangles that make an interleaving

The gold arrow is not extra data: it is the map ℱ already had from t to t + 2ε. An interleaving is the demand that going up-and-over by η and back-and-over by η′ reproduces it exactly, and the same in the other order. Watch ε shrink: the two triangles flatten, and in the limit the two families are identified.

An ε-interleaving implies the homologies of the two sequences can differ substantially only over short parameter intervals, which motivates defining the distance d(Hk(Xt), Hk(X′t)) to be the infimum of ε over all ε-interleavings.

It is not a metric, for two separate reasons. Ghrist gives both and they are worth keeping apart. First, it may take the value +∞, when no interleaving exists at any ε. Second, two persistence diagrams at interleaving distance zero need not be identical, since the infimum may be 0 without a 0-interleaving existing — an infimum that is not attained. Reflexivity and the triangle inequality do hold, so what one has is a pseudometric. Neither defect is repaired by the stability theorem below; a bound on a pseudometric is a bound on a pseudometric.

Theorem 10.15. Given h, h′ : X → ℝ, the interleaving distance between the sublevel set persistent homology sequences is bounded by the L∞ norm of the difference of the height functions:

d(Hk(Xt), Hk(X′t)) ≤ ‖h − h′‖∞.

— Ghrist §10.6, Theorem 10.15, p. 220

The same thing, categorically

For a function h : X → ℝ and a ≤ b, the inclusion Xa → Xb of sublevel sets defines an excursion-set functor ℰh : (ℝ, ≤) → Top, and the sublevel set persistent homology is the composition of this functor with H•. The prevalence of functors motivates Ghrist's question: what is the interleaving distance of arbitrary functors (ℝ, ≤) → ℂ for ℂ a category? The answer involves natural transformations, and Example 10.10 supplies the ingredient — translation is a functor Tε with a natural transformation τε from the identity.

ε-interleaving (the categorical version). An ε-interleaving of two functors ℱ, ℱ′ : ℝ → ℂ is a pair of natural transformations η : ℱ ⇒ ℱ′·Tε and η′ : ℱ′ ⇒ ℱ·Tε such that

(η′·Tε) ∘ η = ℱ·τ2ε    and    (η·Tε) ∘ η′ = ℱ′·τ2ε.

The interleaving distance on functors, d(ℱ, ℱ′), is the infimal ε for an ε-interleaving — again a pseudometric that may take the value ∞ for non-interleavable functors. — Ghrist §10.6, p. 220

Every piece of the concrete definition has been replaced by a categorical one, and nothing has been added. The shifted homomorphisms φt became the components of a natural transformation; the requirement that the composites be "the maps induced by the shift +2ε" became the equation with ℱ·τ2ε on the right, where τ2ε is Example 10.10's translation natural transformation; and composing a natural transformation with a functor — η′·Tε — is the operation §10.5 introduced for no apparent reason two pages earlier.

What the mission strip claims, and what §10.6 establishes. The strip says the categorical definition "generalises unchanged to sheaves, Reeb graphs and multiparameter modules". Read the quantifier in Ghrist's own question: arbitrary functors (ℝ, ≤) → ℂ for ℂ a category. What is arbitrary is the target. That is a real and substantial generalisation — it is what lets the proof below interleave spaces in Top and then push the interleaving through Hk to Vect, and it is what makes tda1-06's interleaving distance a definition rather than a construction, exactly as the strip says. But the index is not arbitrary: it is (ℝ, ≤) throughout, and the shift is the specific Tε of Example 10.10 with its τε. A multiparameter module is indexed by (ℝd, ≤), so reaching it requires replacing the index category and replacing the one-parameter shift by translation along a vector — a change to the definition, not an instance of it; tda2-04's Definition 12.8 is where that version is actually set up. As for sheaves and Reeb graphs: §10.6 mentions neither. tda2-06's Definition 7.6 is a genuine interleaving distance and Dey's §7.4 notes an equivalent cosheaf-theoretic formulation of it, so the claim is true of the literature — but it is not established by the sections this unit reads, and the Reeb-graph case needs its own ε-smoothing functor to even state.

Guided proof

Prompt. Prove Theorem 10.15 in the categorical form. (1) With ε := ‖h − h′‖∞, prove the two set inclusions relating the excursion sets of h and h′, being explicit about where the sup-norm is used. (2) Check that the resulting inclusions really do satisfy the composite identity (η′·Tε) ∘ η = ℰh·τ2ε — this is the step Ghrist compresses to a single clause. (3) Complete the proof by pushing the interleaving through Hk, naming the property of functors that licenses it, and say why the theorem does not upgrade the pseudometric to a metric.

Hint: for (2), every map in sight is an inclusion of subsets of X; ask what a composite of inclusions is.

Click to reveal model answer

(1) The inclusions. Put ε = ‖h − h′‖∞ = supx|h(x) − h′(x)|. Fix t and let x ∈ ℰh(t) = {h ≤ t}, so h(x) ≤ t. By definition of the sup-norm, |h(x) − h′(x)| ≤ ε for every x — this is the only place the sup is used, and it is used pointwise — so h′(x) ≤ h(x) + ε ≤ t + ε. Hence x ∈ {h′ ≤ t + ε} = ℰh′(t + ε), giving

ℰh(t) = {h ≤ t} ⊂ {h′ ≤ t + ε} = ℰh′(t + ε),

and by the symmetric argument ℰh′(t) ⊂ ℰh(t + ε). Both hold for every t.

(2) The composite identity. Let η(t) : ℰh(t) → ℰh′(t + ε) and η′(t) : ℰh′(t) → ℰh(t + ε) be the inclusions just produced. Each is natural: for s ≤ t the square built from η and the inclusions ℰh(s) ⊂ ℰh(t), ℰh′(s+ε) ⊂ ℰh′(t+ε) commutes, because all four maps are inclusions of subsets of X and any two composites agree as functions.

Now compute the composite at an index t. The transformation η′·Tε has component at t equal to η′(t + ε) : ℰh′(t + ε) → ℰh(t + 2ε). So

((η′·Tε) ∘ η)(t) = η′(t + ε) ∘ η(t) : ℰh(t) ⟶ ℰh(t + 2ε),

a composite of two inclusions of subsets of X, which is the inclusion ℰh(t) ⊂ ℰh(t + 2ε). And that inclusion is exactly the component at t of ℰh·τ2ε, since τ2ε : Id ⇒ T2ε has component the morphism t ≤ t + 2ε and ℰh takes that morphism to the inclusion of sublevel sets. Hence (η′·Tε) ∘ η = ℰh·τ2ε, and symmetrically for the other. So η, η′ are an ε-interleaving of ℰh and ℰh′. ∎

(3) Push through homology. If ℱ and ℱ′ are ε-interleaved and 𝒢 is any functor, then 𝒢·ℱ and 𝒢·ℱ′ are ε-interleaved — the property is functoriality: applying 𝒢 to every component of η and η′ gives natural transformations again, and applying 𝒢 to the two composite identities gives the corresponding identities for 𝒢·ℱ, since a functor preserves composition. Taking 𝒢 = Hk turns the interleaving of excursion-set functors into an interleaving of the persistent homology sequences, at the same ε = ‖h − h′‖∞. Since d is the infimum over ε for which an interleaving exists, d ≤ ‖h − h′‖∞. ∎

Why this does not make d a metric: the theorem produces an upper bound and says nothing about the two defects. It does not exclude the case of no interleaving at any ε — for that, d = +∞ and the bound is vacuous unless the two functions are both defined and finitely far apart. And it does not exclude an infimum being attained by no interleaving, so d = 0 still fails to force ℱ = ℱ′. Reflexivity and the triangle inequality are the properties that do hold, and they hold independently of Theorem 10.15.

What this does and does not license. The proof interleaves spaces and only then applies homology, which is possible precisely because the definition was stated for an arbitrary target category. That is the payoff of the reformulation, and it is a genuine simplification: the geometry is one line of set inclusions and the algebra is one appeal to functoriality. It licenses nothing about index categories other than (ℝ, ≤).

Self-check 5. In the categorical definition, what is ℱ·τ2ε?

τ2ε : Id ⇒ T2ε lives in (ℝ, ≤) and has as its component at t the morphism t ≤ t + 2ε; composing with ℱ on the left applies ℱ to that morphism, giving ℱ's own structure map. The condition therefore says the round trip reproduces the map the functor already had — which is what the gold arrow in the visual was. The second option confuses a condition with an interleaving, and while τ2ε is indeed related to τε twice over, that observation does not say what ℱ·τ2ε is.

Self-check 6. Which step of the proof of Theorem 10.15 uses that the target category is arbitrary?

The concrete definition of §10.6 was stated for homology sequences; had that been the only definition available, one would have had to build the interleaving maps on homology directly. Because the categorical definition quantifies over the target, an interleaving of Top-valued functors is a legitimate object and functoriality transports it. The inclusions in the second option are indeed set-theoretic, but they are the raw material, not the step where the generality is spent.
