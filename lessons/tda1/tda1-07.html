The stability theorem (tda1-07)

Perturb the data by epsilon, the diagram moves by at most epsilon. The theorem that makes TDA a science, proven. (pw-04's craft.)

Predict before we start. Stability bounds the bottleneck distance between two diagrams by ε. Which of these numbers, read off a diagram, is therefore guaranteed to move by at most a bounded amount?

Only the first. A bound on db says every point moves by at most ε in the supremum norm or is destroyed against the diagonal, which controls the longest bar to within 2ε. It does not control a count across a threshold — arbitrarily many bars can sit within ε of it — and it does not control a sum, which is why Oudot's own Theorem 3.8 has to buy its Wasserstein bound with a quantity he calls unstable. Segment 3 does this properly.

Why this matters for the mission: The stability theorem is why persistence is trustworthy on noisy data — the mission's keystone; the isometry theorem (bottleneck = interleaving distance) is its algebraic partner.

Two things to fix in that strip before the theorem is stated, so that it can be read correctly when it is. First, stability is not the isometry theorem's partner; it is one of its two halves. Oudot: "the statement of the theorem falls into two parts: on the one hand, the 'stability' part … on the other hand, the 'converse stability' part". Second, "trustworthy on noisy data" is a claim about a chain of inferences from a data perturbation to a reported number, and the theorem is a bound on a bottleneck distance by a supremum norm. Segment 3 works out which links of that chain these pages close and which they do not — using Oudot's own remark that the original hypotheses "do have an impact on applications … especially in the presence of noise in the data" (p. 62).

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Three statements, and exactly what each says

Theorem 3.1 (Isometry) — Oudot, p. 49

For 𝔮-tame persistence modules 𝕍, 𝕎 over ℝ: db(dgm(𝕍), dgm(𝕎)) = di(𝕍, 𝕎).

Stability is the inequality db ≤ di: modules that are close have diagrams that are close, so a diagram cannot be thrown far by a small change. Converse stability is di ≤ db: diagrams that are close came from modules that are close, so the signature is not merely calm but discriminating. Oudot: the stability part "is reputed to be difficult to prove, whereas the converse stability part is easy once the stability part is given" — an ordering Segment 2 and Segment 3 follow.

Corollary 3.6 — Oudot, p. 61

Let f, g : X → ℝ be 𝔮-tame functions. Then db(dgm(f), dgm(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞.

The derivation is two steps, both from tda1-06, and Oudot puts it in a footnote: ‖f − g‖∞ ≤ ε makes the sublevel-set filtrations ε-interleaved by (3.3), hence the persistent homologies ε-interleaved by Definition 3.3, and Theorem 3.1 applies. Note what the corollary needs and note it now, because Segment 3 turns on it: the same space X, two functions on it, compared in the supremum norm.

Edelsbrunner has the inequality without the equality, twice. For monotonic f, g on a simplicial complex K: W∞(Dgmp(f), Dgmp(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞, and he proves it — via vineyards, by differentiating the straight-line homotopy and bounding the slope of every segment in the vineyard by ‖f − g‖∞. Bounding a slope bounds a distance because a vine that runs from t = 0 to t = 1 with slope at most s has moved at most s in the plane, and the vines are exactly the trajectories the matching follows. For tame functions on a triangulable space he states the same inequality "without proof" — Edelsbrunner §VIII.2, pp. 217–219.

Self-check 1. Oudot reports that the original Cohen-Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer statement needed a more restrictive tameness plus finite triangulability and continuity, because its authors interpolated between the functions rather than between the modules. What went wrong with that?

Oudot's word is exactly "wild": taking the persistent homology of an interpolating function gives a module you do not control, so the hypotheses on X, f and g had to be strengthened until it was forced to remain tame. The algebraic Interpolation Lemma removes the problem by interpolating in the category where the conclusion lives. This is a general moral about proofs, and it has a cost in applications that Oudot states plainly.

Segment 2 · ~30 min

Snapping, and a loose bound that is tight for something else

The first proof gives db ≤ 3di — not the theorem, but "very simple and geometrically flavored", and its looseness turns out to be informative. Everything rests on one construction. The ε-discretization 𝕍εℤ is the restriction of 𝕍 to the index set εℤ, extended back over ℝ by putting Vnε at every index in [nε, (n+1)ε) — Oudot §3.2.1, p. 54.

What discretizing does to a diagram is legible. A birth strictly between (n−1)ε and nε is not detected until nε; a death strictly between (m−1)ε and mε is not detected until mε. So a summand 𝕀⌈b±, d±⌋ inside that cell becomes 𝕀[nε, mε) — and the corresponding point is snapped to the upper-right corner of its grid cell. Both coordinates round up, because detection is always late. When a coordinate sits exactly on a cell boundary, it is the decoration that decides which cell the point belongs to, so two summands plotting at the same undecorated point can snap to different vertices.

Lemma 3.4 (Snapping) — Oudot, p. 55

If 𝕍 is 𝔮-tame then dgm(𝕍εℤ) is well defined and dgm(𝕍εℤ) ∖ Δ is localised at the grid vertices {(nε, mε)}n<m, with the multiplicity of each vertex given by the persistence measure μ𝕍 of its lower-left cell — mult(nε, mε) = μ𝕍([(n−1)ε, nε] × [(m−1)ε, mε]), and correspondingly at infinity. These rules define a partial matching of bottleneck cost at most ε between dgm(𝕍) and dgm(𝕍εℤ).

The multiplicity is read off the cell below and to the left of the vertex, because that is the cell whose points snap to it. The bound of ε is the cell's own side length: a point moves within one cell, so each coordinate moves by less than ε and the ℓ∞-distance is at most ε. And one observation Oudot flags as "instrumental": the grid may be shifted, so 𝕍 may equally be discretized over εℤ + ε/2, with the same bound.

Worked, then fading: from an interleaving to 3ε. — Oudot, pp. 55–56

Let φ, ψ be an ε-interleaving of 𝔮-tame 𝕍, 𝕎. Restrict attention to the indices 2nε in 𝕍 and (2n+1)ε in 𝕎. The interleaving maps run diagonally between the two rows, and commutativity makes the whole ladder commute. Three modules can be read off it: 𝕍2εℤ along the top row, 𝕎2εℤ+ε along the bottom row, and a mixed module 𝕌 obtained by following the diagonals, alternating spaces from 𝕍 and 𝕎.

The key observation: by commutativity, 𝕍2εℤ and 𝕎2εℤ+ε are both 2ε-discretizations of 𝕌 — the top row is 𝕌 restricted to its even positions and the bottom row to its odd ones. So Lemma 3.4 applies to four pairs, (𝕍, 𝕍2εℤ), (𝕌, 𝕍2εℤ), (𝕌, 𝕎2εℤ+ε) and (𝕎, 𝕎2εℤ+ε), each giving a bottleneck bound of ⟨ what, given a grid of spacing 2ε? ⟩. The triangle inequality then gives db(dgm 𝕍, dgm 𝕎) ≤ ⟨ what? ⟩.

That crude number comes down to 3ε by tracking one point rather than adding four worst cases: a point of dgm(𝕍) is matched successively into dgm(𝕍2εℤ), dgm(𝕌), dgm(𝕎2εℤ+ε) and dgm(𝕎), and the four moves are not independent — each snap lands on a grid vertex, so the next move starts from a constrained position. Following Figure 3.3, the total displacement is at most 3ε. What the crude sum double-counts is ⟨ what? ⟩.

Now the remark that makes the loose bound worth teaching. Oudot: 3di "is not a tight upper bound. However, it becomes so if one replaces the concept of interleaving as of Definition 3.3 by the weaker version given in (3.8)" — and one can build modules that are weakly ε-interleaved whose diagrams lie exactly 3ε apart. So this is not a weaker argument for the same theorem; it is a tight argument for a weaker hypothesis. What (3.8) records is the interleaving at the indices of one fixed grid and its shift. What Definition 3.3 gives, and (3.8) throws away, is the interleaving at every real index — equivalently, the freedom to shift the grid arbitrarily. So the missing 2ε is exactly the price of fixing a grid, and any proof of the tight bound must use shiftability — p. 57.

Lemma 3.5 (Interpolation) — Oudot, p. 57

Suppose 𝕍, 𝕎 are ε-interleaved. Then there is a 1-parameter family (𝕌x)x∈[0,ε] of persistence modules with 𝕌₀ = 𝕍, 𝕌ε = 𝕎, and 𝕌x, 𝕌y being |y − x|-interleaved for all x, y. This family is not unique in general.

Oudot calls it "certainly the most subtle part in the proof of the Isometry Theorem", and the picture behind it is worth having. Order the plane by (x, y) ⪯ (x′, y′) iff x ≤ x′ and y ≤ y′, and for each r let Δr = {(x, y) : y − x = 2r} be a shifted diagonal. As a poset Δr is isomorphic to ℝ, via t ↦ (t − r, t + r), so persistence modules over ℝ are the same thing as representations of Δr. Then (3.9): 𝕍 and 𝕎 are |y − x|-interleaved exactly when there is a representation 𝕌 of Δx ∪ Δy restricting to 𝕍 on one line and 𝕎 on the other. The module structures become the maps along the diagonals; φ and ψ become the vertical and horizontal maps between them; everything else is composition. Interpolating is then filling in the lines between.

The tight bound then runs in three steps — Oudot, p. 59. Both rest on the Box Lemma: writing δ = |y − x| and Rδ for the δ-thickening of a rectangle R = [p, q] × [r, s] with r > q + 2δ,

μ𝕌x(R) ≤ μ𝕌y(Rδ) and μ𝕌y(R) ≤ μ𝕌x(Rδ),

proved by discretizing both modules over the finite index set {p−δ, p, q, q+δ, r−δ, r, s, s+δ} and running the same point-tracking argument through Lemma 3.4. Step 1: letting R shrink to a point of dgm(𝕌x) forces μ𝕌y to be positive within a δ-thickening of it, so the Hausdorff distance between the two diagrams is at most |y − x|. Step 2: once δ is below a threshold δx — typically a fraction of the minimum inter-point distance in dgm(𝕌x) — the bottleneck and Hausdorff distances coincide. Step 3: the intervals (x − δx, x + δx) cover the compact [0, ε]; extract a finite subcover with centres 0 = x₀ < ⋯ < xk = ε, and on each consecutive pair db = dH ≤ |xi+1 − xi|. The triangle inequality sums these to ε.

Self-check 2. Why does interpolating give ε where §2.1 gave 3ε, when chaining the loose bound over N steps of ε/N would still give 3ε?

Chaining the loose bound really does give nothing better than 3ε — the constant does not improve by subdividing. The gain comes from Step 2: below a threshold depending on the diagram's own inter-point spacing, db and dH agree, and the Box Lemma bounds dH by the step size with constant 1. Compactness of [0, ε] is what makes finitely many such steps suffice. Discretization is not avoided — the Box Lemma is proved with it. And Oudot says explicitly the family is not unique; Figure 3.5 shows three constructions and notes the interpolated points "do not always follow shortest paths in the ℓ∞-distance".

Segment 3 · ~30 min

The converse, and what stability licenses

The converse is two steps. Interval-decomposable case. Take a matching of bottleneck cost at most ε between the two undecorated diagrams. It induces a partial matching of interval summands, so both modules split as ⊕j𝕍j and ⊕j𝕎j where each pair is either two matched intervals, or an unmatched summand of 𝕍 against 0, or 0 against an unmatched summand of 𝕎. An ε-interleaving is then built summand by summand and summed — Oudot §3.3, p. 60.

General case, and it is a nice argument. Let 𝓟𝔮 be the 𝔮-tame modules with the pseudometric di, 𝓟pfd the pointwise finite-dimensional ones, and f(𝕍, 𝕎) = di(𝕍, 𝕎) − db(dgm 𝕍, dgm 𝕎). Stability makes 𝕍 ↦ dgm(𝕍) 1-Lipschitz, so f is non-negative and continuous; the interval-decomposable case makes f = 0 on 𝓟pfd × 𝓟pfd. If 𝓟pfd is dense, continuity finishes it. And it is dense: the ε-smoothing 𝕍ε with Viε = im vi−εi+ε is pointwise finite-dimensional exactly because Definition 1.12 says those ranks are finite, and 𝕍 is ε-interleaved with 𝕍ε, so 𝕍 is the limit of the 𝕍1/n. Oudot's Remark adds that this continuity argument extends the full theorem, so it suffices to prove the Isometry Theorem for pointwise finite-dimensional modules — pp. 60–61.

Guided audit — what a bound on db does and does not control.

Corollary 3.6 gives db(dgm(f), dgm(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞ for 𝔮-tame f, g on the same space X. Almost nothing anyone reports is a bottleneck distance, so the operative question is which functionals of a diagram are Lipschitz for db.

Three candidates. (1) The length of the longest bar. (2) The number of bars longer than a fixed threshold 2δ. (3) The total persistence, Σ(py − px). For each, decide whether db(X, Y) ≤ ε bounds the difference, and prove it or give a counterexample. Then say what the answers imply about chaining stability results together.

Reveal the model answer

(1) Longest bar: controlled. If db(X, Y) ≤ ε then take a matching of cost at most ε′ for any ε′ > ε. The longest bar of X corresponds to a point p; its partner q satisfies ‖p − q‖∞ ≤ ε′, so each coordinate moves by at most ε′ and the bar length py − px changes by at most 2ε′. If p is unmatched, its length is at most 2ε′ to begin with. So the longest bar lengths differ by at most 2ε. Lipschitz, with constant 2.

(2) Count above a threshold: not controlled, and badly. Let X have n points all at (0, 2δ + ε/2) and Y have the same n points at (0, 2δ − ε/2). Then a matching of cost ε/2 exists, so db(X, Y) ≤ ε/2, and every bar of X exceeds 2δ while none of Y's does. The counts differ by n, for any n. No bound in terms of ε alone can exist, because thresholding is discontinuous and stability is a continuity statement.

(3) Total persistence: not controlled. Let X be empty off the diagonal and Y have n points at height ε above the diagonal. Every point of Y is within ε/2 of the diagonal in the ℓ∞ sense, so db(X, Y) ≤ ε/2, while the total persistence of Y is nε and of X is 0. Again unbounded in n. The trouble is that a sum over an unbounded number of small terms is not controlled by a supremum over them.

What this implies about chaining. Exactly what Oudot's Theorem 3.8 shows. That theorem bounds a Wasserstein distance by di — but it has to pay for it, and the price is a factor (Pers(𝕍) + Pers(𝕎))1/p, which he immediately calls a dependence on quantities "which are unstable quantities". Case (3) is why: total persistence is one of the functionals stability does not control, so a bound that contains it is only as good as a separate bound on it. Stability is a statement about one distance, and every step away from that distance needs its own argument.

Reveal the skeleton

1. The theorem is an equality with two halves, and the function-level statement is a corollary. Stability, db ≤ di, makes diagrams stable; converse stability, di ≤ db, makes them informative. Corollary 3.6 follows in two steps: sup-norm closeness gives interleaved sublevel sets, which give interleaved modules. Edelsbrunner proves the inequality for monotonic functions by bounding every vineyard slope by ‖f − g‖∞, and states the tame-function version without proof.

2. The loose bound is tight for a weaker hypothesis, and that is what says where the work must go. Discretizing snaps each diagram point to the upper-right corner of its cell, moving it by at most the cell width (Lemma 3.4). Restricting an ε-interleaving to alternating indices produces a mixed module 𝕌 of which both discretizations are 2ε-discretizations, so four applications of the lemma and the triangle inequality give 8ε, reduced to 3ε by tracking one point. And 3ε is exactly right for the weak interleaving of (3.8), which fixes one grid — so the missing factor is the freedom to shift the grid, and Lemma 3.5's interpolating family is what supplies it.

3. Converse stability is easy on decomposables and extends by density, and neither half licenses a pipeline. A cost-ε matching splits both modules summand by summand; ε-smoothing makes every 𝔮-tame module a di-limit of pointwise finite-dimensional ones, and continuity does the rest — which, as Oudot notes, extends the whole theorem, so pfd modules suffice. What a bound on db then controls is a Lipschitz functional of the diagram: the longest bar, yes; a count across a threshold, no; total persistence, no — which is why Theorem 3.8's Wasserstein bound has to carry a quantity Oudot himself calls unstable.
