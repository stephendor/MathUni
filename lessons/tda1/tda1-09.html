The persistence algorithm and duality (tda1-09)

The full algorithm, with its clever pairing and cohomological speed-up — why Ripser (lab-03) is so fast.

Predict before we start. tda1-03's persistence algorithm is cubic. For a piecewise linear function on a triangulated surface, Edelsbrunner gets both interesting diagrams in time proportional to mα(m) — essentially linear. Where does the saving come from?

Two ingredients, neither of them cohomological. Dimension 0 collapses to a connectivity question that union–find answers in near-linear time. Dimension 1 is then obtained without a second persistence computation, by reflecting the 0-th diagram of −f across the minor diagonal. The sparse representation helps but is still cubic in the worst case. Cohomology is Chapter V of this book and is not in this unit's resources at all — Segment 3 says what that does to the hook.

Why this matters for the mission: The theory behind the tool: the reduction algorithm's correctness and the duality that makes it efficient.

Two scope notes, one of which is a straightforward correction. Correctness was established in tda1-03, by the Pairing Lemma; §VII.2 makes the algorithm fast and changes nothing about whether it is right. And the hook's "cohomological speed-up" is not on these pages: cohomology is Chapter V, outside this unit's resources, and the cohomology-based persistence algorithm that Ripser uses postdates this book entirely. What Edelsbrunner does have is a genuine speed-up from a genuine duality — just a different one, stated in §VII.3 and used in §VII.2. Segment 3 sorts it out and writes the corrected sentence.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The same algorithm, stored sparsely

tda1-03's algorithm stores the whole boundary matrix. For a large complex that is prohibitive, so §VII.2 replaces it by a linear array ∂[1..m] with a linked list at each entry. The list at ∂[j] starts as the j-th column — the codimension-one faces of σj — and by the end the list at the j-th entry is the column of the reduced matrix whose lowest one is in row j. That relocation is the whole idea, and it is why Edelsbrunner renames the array from ∂ to R as the algorithm runs, and why "we see a general migration of the lists from right to left" — Edelsbrunner §VII.2, p. 186.

Lists are sorted by decreasing index, so the most recently added simplex is on top; write i = TOP(L). Call an entry occupied if it holds a non-empty list. Then the algorithm reads: for each j, take L = ∂[j], empty R[j], and while L is non-empty and R[i] with i = TOP(L) is occupied, replace L by L + R[i]; if L survives non-empty, deposit it at R[i]. Compare with tda1-03: TOP(L) is low(j), "occupied" is "a column already has that lowest one", and adding lists is adding columns. The dense and sparse algorithms do the same arithmetic.

Adding two lists is a merge that deletes both copies of every duplicate — the set-symmetric-difference reading of addition that tda1-02 established from 1 + 1 = 0 — and sorted order makes it a single parallel scan.

Worked, then fading: the analysis, by collisions. — Edelsbrunner, p. 187

Call a collision a failed attempt to deposit L because the entry is occupied; each one costs a merge. The loop ends either with L empty — σj gives ⟨ birth or death? ⟩ — or with a successful deposit at some entry i, in which case σj gives ⟨ which? ⟩ and σi is ⟨ what? ⟩.

The inner loop runs right to left. When L collides with R[k], the simplex σk is topmost in both lists, so in the merge it ⟨ what happens to it, and by what arithmetic? ⟩. Hence the topmost simplex of the result has index below k, and successive collisions have strictly decreasing indices.

So collisions are confined. They happen only at entries between i and j, where i = 1 if σj gives birth and i is the index of the corresponding birth if it gives death — and in that second case j − i is exactly tda1-03's ⟨ which quantity? ⟩. A collision at k requires σk to have given birth to a class that died at some σl with i < k < l < j, and inductively the collisions for σl are nested inside [k, l]. So every list added contains only faces of simplices with index in [i, j].

The bound. A p-simplex has at most ⟨ how many? ⟩ codimension-one faces, so the faces available number at most (p+1)(j−i), which bounds the cost of one merge; and the number of collisions is bounded by ⟨ what? ⟩. Multiplying gives (p+1)(j−i)² for the inner loop at σj.

Knowing in advance which simplices give birth — which the Incremental Betti Number Algorithm supplies for complexes in ℝ³ — lets the births be skipped, making the total output-sensitive: the dimension times the sum of squares of the index persistences. That is at most proportional to m³, and much less whenever the bars are short. This is the first bound in the module that improves on favourable data rather than on favourable dimensions.

Self-check 1. Compare with tda1-02's Smith normal form bound of a constant times 2np−1npmin{np−1, np}. Both are cubic in the worst case. What is genuinely different?

The Smith bound is a function of how many simplices there are in two adjacent dimensions and of nothing else — no input of a given size can beat it. The persistence bound is a sum of squares of index persistences, which is a property of the output: a filtration in which everything dies quickly is fast, and one long bar is what costs. It is the Smith bound that is per dimension, incidentally; the persistence algorithm handles all dimensions in one matrix.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Dimension zero collapses, and dimension one is its reflection

The 0-th diagram depends only on the vertices and edges and their order, since nothing of higher dimension can create or destroy a component. A vertex has empty boundary and always gives birth. An edge σj has ∂σj = u + w with u = σi first and w = σk second; the algorithm tries to deposit the two-element list at R[k], and if that entry is occupied the occupant is another two-element list, so the merge again has length two. Every list in sight has length exactly two, each addition is constant time, and the total is at most m² — p. 188.

"But we can do even better." Look at what the two cases mean. The edge gives birth to a 1-cycle exactly when u and w already lie in the same component of Kj−1 — the algorithm walks a path along the collisions with L tracking its two endpoints, and eventually the ends meet and L empties. It gives death exactly when they lie in different components; the loop then ends when a path-end reaches the oldest vertex σi of one component, and because the loop runs monotonically right to left, the oldest vertex of the other component is older still. That is the Elder Rule of tda1-03 appearing as a property of the loop's direction rather than as a definition: the survivor is the older component because the loop cannot reach past it.

So the outcome is predictable from connectivity alone. Whether u and w are in different components, and if so which vertices are oldest, is exactly what union–find answers — store each component as a tree and name it by the index of its oldest vertex. The 0-th diagram therefore costs mα(m), with α the inverse Ackermann function, "which, for all practical purposes, is bounded from above by a constant" — p. 189.

Dimension one has no comparable structure — there is no union–find for 1-cycles. Edelsbrunner's move is not to compute it at all.

The reflection — Edelsbrunner, pp. 190–191

A point (a, b) belongs to Dgm₁(f) if and only if (−b, −a) belongs to Dgm₀(−f). So the finite portion of Dgm₁(f) is the finite portion of Dgm₀(−f) reflected across the minor diagonal.

The reasoning is one sentence: "Looking at −f is like taking the complement and going backward." A point (a, b) of Dgm₁(f) marks a 1-class born at a and dying at b; under −f the order reverses and the values negate, so the same event is a 0-class born at −b and dying at −a. Edelsbrunner omits the proof, "since the relations are consequences of the more general theorems given in the next section" — that is §VII.3's Persistence Duality and Symmetry Theorems, which tda1-05 read, and which are consequences of Lefschetz duality.

The infinite points are not obtained by reflection and need their own argument. Partition the edges of K into three sets: those that give death in the lower star filtration of f, those that give death in the lower star filtration of −f, and the rest. The first two contribute the finite portions of the 0-th and 1-st diagrams. Each edge in the third set gives a class born when it is added and living on even when K is complete — a point at infinity in the 1-st diagram. The result is a three-pass algorithm computing both diagrams of a PL function on a triangulated 2-manifold in time proportional to mα(m) — p. 191.

Self-check 2. The reflection computes a 1-dimensional diagram from a 0-dimensional computation. Why is that a speed-up and not merely a convenience?

The saving is not in the reflection, which is trivial — it is in what the reflection lets you avoid. Dimension 0 reduces to connectivity, which union–find answers in mα(m); dimension 1 has no such reduction, and running the general algorithm there is cubic. Reflecting exports the 1-dimensional problem into the dimension where the fast method lives. That is what makes a duality worth having computationally: not that it is elegant, but that the two sides have different costs.

Self-check 3. An edge in the third part of the partition gives death in neither the filtration of f nor that of −f. Why does that make it a point at infinity in Dgm₁(f)?

Giving death for f means killing a component on the way up; giving death for −f means, by the reflection, killing a 1-class of f. An edge doing neither must give birth to a 1-class for f — its endpoints are already connected — and that class survives to the complete complex. On a surface there are no 2-cells beyond K itself to kill it, so its death coordinate is infinite. The third option describes an edge that gives death, which is the opposite case.

One prerequisite has been passed over and is worth stating, because it is what makes the whole surface computation legitimate. The diagrams of f are defined from the sublevel sets |K|a; the algorithm computes with the lower star filtration, which is the filtration of the monotonic g(σ) = maxx∈σ f(x) — the function tda1-08 constructed, and which Edelsbrunner gives explicitly here. The inclusion |Ka| ⊆ |K|a is a homotopy equivalence by §VI.3, so homotopy invariance of homology from at1-07 makes the vertical maps in the square relating the two sequences isomorphisms, and all four maps being induced by inclusion makes it commute. Those are exactly the two hypotheses of the Persistence Equivalence Theorem, so the two sequences define the same diagram — p. 189.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

Where long exact sequences come from, and what the hook claims

§IV.4 supplies the machinery that at1-09 and tda1-08 have been using. Free the definitions from simplicial complexes: a chain complex is a sequence 𝒰 = (Up, up) with upup+1 = 0, and that vanishing is all that is needed to define Zp, Bp and Hp. A chain map φ commutes with the boundary maps, vφ = φu, which forces φp(Zp(𝒰)) ⊆ Zp(𝒱) and φp(Bp(𝒰)) ⊆ Bp(𝒱) and hence induces a map on homology — §IV.4, p. 114.

Worth noticing: those two containments are stated correctly here, where §IV.1's version — flagged in tda1-02 — put a stray f# on the right-hand side and failed to typecheck. The same book, the same claim, one chapter apart, and this is the right one.

Snake Lemma — Edelsbrunner, p. 115

Let 0 → 𝒰 →φ 𝒱 →ψ 𝒲 → 0 be a short exact sequence of chain complexes. Then there is a well-defined map D : Hp(𝒲) → Hp−1(𝒰), the connecting homomorphism, such that ⋯ → Hp(𝒰) → Hp(𝒱) → Hp(𝒲) →D Hp−1(𝒰) → ⋯ is a long exact sequence.

Every other map in the long sequence is induced by a chain map; D is the only new object, and it is the only thing that has to be built. The exact homology sequence of a pair falls straight out: inclusions give 0 → 𝒞(K₀) → 𝒞(K) → 𝒞(K, K₀) → 0, injective on the left, surjective on the right, and exact in the middle because a chain of K is carried by K₀ exactly when it is zero in the quotient.

Guided construction — the connecting homomorphism, and why two of its four steps are the real work.

Edelsbrunner builds D in four steps from the ladder of short exact sequences. Steps 1 and 2 construct; steps 3 and 4 check.

Your task: carry out the construction. Step 1: start from a cycle α ∈ Wp and produce γ ∈ Up−1 — say which map you invert and why you may, and which map you apply next. Step 2: show γ is a cycle, using commutativity and vv = 0. Steps 3 and 4: say what is chosen in each, what must be shown about it, and — the point of the exercise — why the construction would be worthless without them.

Reveal the model answer

Step 1. Take a cycle α ∈ Wp representing a class in Hp(𝒲). Since ψp is surjective — that is what exactness at 𝒲 gives — there is β ∈ Vp with ψβ = α. Apply the boundary map: vβ ∈ Vp−1. Now ψ(vβ) = w(ψβ) = wα = 0 since α is a cycle, so vβ lies in ker ψp−1 = im φp−1, and since φ is injective there is a unique γ ∈ Up−1 with φγ = vβ. Set D[α] = [γ].

Step 2. φ(uγ) = v(φγ) = v(vβ) = 0 by commutativity and vv = 0, and φ is injective, so uγ = 0 and γ is a cycle. Without this there would be no class [γ] to land in.

Step 3: the choice of β. Another β′ with ψβ′ = α differs from β by something in ker ψp = im φp, so β′ = β + φδ. Then vβ′ = vβ + vφδ = vβ + φ(uδ), so γ′ = γ + uδ — which differs from γ by a boundary, hence [γ′] = [γ].

Step 4: the choice of α. A different representative of the same class in Hp(𝒲) is α + wε, and running the construction on it changes γ by a boundary in the same way, so [γ] is unchanged.

Why steps 3 and 4 are the work. Steps 1 and 2 produce a class from a pair of choices; nothing in them says the result is a function of the input. D is defined on homology classes, and the construction reaches into chains twice — once to pick a preimage β, once to pick a representative α. If either choice mattered, D would not be a map at all and the long sequence would not exist. This is the general shape of every diagram-chase definition: the construction is short and the well-definedness is the theorem.

Self-check 4. In the long exact sequence the Snake Lemma produces, every map except D is induced by a chain map. Why does that make D the only thing the lemma has to construct?

The maps Hp(𝒰) → Hp(𝒱) → Hp(𝒲) come free from φ and ψ, by the argument that a chain map sends cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries. D is the one arrow that drops a dimension, and nothing in the given data points that way — it has to be manufactured, which is why the entire content of the construction is the two well-definedness steps. D is a homomorphism, and the induced maps are certainly not isomorphisms in general; the sequence being exact is exactly the statement that they are not.

Edelsbrunner uses the Snake Lemma to derive the Mayer–Vietoris Sequence Theorem for a complex K with subcomplexes K′, K″ covering it and A = K′ ∩ K″ — the divide-and-conquer sequence at1-09 taught and tda1-08's PL Morse proof ran on — p. 117. He omits the proof that the long sequence is exact, "leaving that as an exercise to the interested reader".

The audit, and the corrected sentences.

Cohomology. Searching both sources: §VII.2 and §IV.4 contain none. Edelsbrunner defines cohomology in §V.1, outside this unit's resources, and the one place Chapter VII mentions it is §VII.3's remark that modulo 2 the homology and cohomology groups are isomorphic — used to state Lefschetz duality, not to compute anything. There is no cohomological persistence algorithm in this book. The algorithm the hook is reaching for is a later development, and Ripser is not mentioned anywhere in these pages.

Correctness. tda1-03's Pairing Lemma. §VII.2 adds no correctness argument and needs none: the sparse implementation performs the same operations as the dense one, so it inherits the guarantee.

The duality that does make it efficient. Dgm₁(f) is the reflection of Dgm₀(−f) across the minor diagonal, stated in §VII.2 and proved in §VII.3 from Lefschetz duality. It earns the word "duality" because it relates complementary dimensions of complementary constructions, and it earns "efficient" because dimension 0 has union–find and dimension 1 has nothing comparable — the whole saving is that the two sides of the duality cost different amounts.

Reveal the skeleton

1. The sparse implementation is the same algorithm with a different data structure, and its analysis is output-sensitive. An array of linked lists, migrating right to left; TOP(L) is low(j) and occupancy is the collision test. The inner loop is monotone because the colliding simplex cancels, so collisions are confined to [i, j] and nest; a merge costs at most (p+1)(j−i) and there are at most j−i of them, giving (p+1)(j−i)². Skipping known births makes the total the dimension times the sum of squares of index persistences — a bound the data can beat, unlike tda1-02's.

2. Dimension 0 reduces to connectivity and dimension 1 reflects into it. All lists in dimension 0 have length two; an edge gives birth iff its endpoints are already connected, and death otherwise, with the Elder Rule appearing as a consequence of the loop's direction. Union–find then gives mα(m). Dimension 1 has no analogue, so instead Dgm₁(f) is the reflection of Dgm₀(−f) across the minor diagonal, with the points at infinity coming from a three-way partition of the edges. The whole surface computation is legitimate because the lower star filtration is the filtration of g(σ) = max f, homotopy equivalent to the sublevel sets, so the Persistence Equivalence Theorem applies.

3. Long exact sequences all come from one lemma, and the hook names the wrong duality. A chain complex needs only uu = 0; a chain map needs only commutation; the Snake Lemma turns a short exact sequence of complexes into a long exact sequence of homology, with the connecting homomorphism the sole construction — and its two well-definedness steps are the actual content. Mayer–Vietoris follows. Meanwhile these pages contain no cohomology and no Ripser: the speed-up is union–find plus reflection, and the duality behind the reflection is §VII.3's Lefschetz-based one.
