Persistent homology and the persistence module (tda1-03)

Track a hole from the level it is born to the level it dies — persistence is homology with a lifespan.

Predict before we start. tda1-02 derived that a filtered reduction may only let a basis element absorb elements of grade no larger — so adding an earlier column to a later one is admissible and free pivot exchange is not. Edelsbrunner's persistence algorithm is on p. 182 of this unit's source. What will it turn out to use?

Exactly that one. The pseudocode's only statement inside the loop is "add column j₀ to column j" with j₀ < j. Segment 3 checks the prediction properly, including the more interesting question of where it was looser than what the book does.

Why this matters for the mission: The persistence module — one vector space per level, one map per step — is the mission's central algebraic object.

Two books, one object, and neither of them says so. Edelsbrunner §VII.1 builds a filtration, takes homology, and studies the images of the induced maps. He never writes the phrase "persistence module" — the object is present and unnamed. Oudot's Chapter 1 defines the persistence module in Definition 1.7 and never builds one from a filtration; his filtrations open Chapter 2, which tda1-01 read. So the identification that gives this unit its title is made here, from the two halves, and Segment 3 does it explicitly rather than by assertion.

And "one map per step" is not what Definition 1.7 says. It supplies a map vij for every pair i ≤ j in T, not only for adjacent ones. For Edelsbrunner's T = {0, 1, …, n} the two descriptions agree, because functoriality makes every map a composite of steps. Over T = ℝ they do not agree and cannot: no two reals are adjacent, so there are no steps at all, and every map in the module is one that "one map per step" fails to account for.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The Elder Rule, and turning it into groups

Edelsbrunner starts before the algebra. Let 𝕏 be connected and f : 𝕏 → ℝ continuous. Sublevel sets are nested, and drawing each component of 𝕏a as a point and letting a increase gives a graph: components merge, and — this is the asymmetry the whole subject rests on — they never split. So the graph is a tree, the merge tree G(f) — Edelsbrunner §VII.1, p. 178.

Decompose that tree into monotone paths, drawn upward together. When two paths meet, one of them must end. Which?

Elder Rule — Edelsbrunner, p. 179

At a juncture, the older of the two merging paths continues and the younger path ends.

The rule is not a convention. Let β(a, b) be the number of components of 𝕏b meeting 𝕏a. Read on the tree, that is the number of subtrees topping out at b and reaching down to a — and each such subtree has exactly one longest path spanning [a, b]. So β(a, b) is the number of decomposition paths spanning [a, b], and Edelsbrunner adds that any decomposition not generated by the Elder Rule fails this. If f is Morse the Elder Rule gives a unique decomposition, and it is the only one whose spanning counts are the β(a, b) for all a ≤ b. The rule is forced by wanting the counts to come out right.

Now the algebra. Take a simplicial complex K and f : K → ℝ that is monotonic: f(σ) ≤ f(τ) whenever σ is a face of τ. That is exactly the condition making every sublevel set K(a) = f−1(−∞, a] a subcomplex, since a face of a simplex in K(a) has value at most that simplex's. With a₁ < ⋯ < an the distinct values and a₀ = −∞, the Ki = K(ai) form

∅ = K₀ ⊆ K₁ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Kn = K,

which Edelsbrunner calls the filtration of f — p. 179. Compare tda1-01's Definition 2.1: this is a finitely simplicial filtration over the finite index set {0, 1, …, n}, and note that here K₀ = ∅ by construction, so it is a filtration of K in the strict sense the octahedron example failed.

Each inclusion induces fpi,j : Hp(Ki) → Hp(Kj), and the filtration becomes a sequence of homology groups strung together by homomorphisms, one sequence per dimension. Classes appear; classes stop being new.

Persistent homology groups — Edelsbrunner, p. 180

The p-th persistent homology groups are the images of the homomorphisms induced by inclusion, Hpi,j = im fpi,j, for 0 ≤ i ≤ j ≤ n. The corresponding p-th persistent Betti numbers are the ranks, βpi,j = rank Hpi,j.

Two readings of the same group, and it is worth having both. As an image it is a subgroup of Hp(Kj) — the classes of Kj that were already there at Ki. As a subquotient it is Hpi,j = Zp(Ki) / (Bp(Kj) ∩ Zp(Ki)) — the cycles present at time i, modulo those that have become boundaries by time j. The first is where the classes live; the second is what has happened to them.

Worked, then fading: birth and death, made precise. — Edelsbrunner, p. 180

Let γ be a class in Hp(Ki). It is born at Ki if γ ∉ Hpi−1,i — read aloud: γ is not the image of anything that was already there one step earlier, so it is genuinely new at i.

If γ is born at Ki then it dies entering Kj if fpi,j−1(γ) ∉ Hpi−1,j−1 but fpi,j(γ) ∈ Hpi−1,j. In words: up to time j−1 the class was still ⟨ what? ⟩, and at time j it becomes ⟨ what? ⟩. Note that the condition is membership in the image of what was already there, and that image may be zero — so a class becoming a boundary is the special case of dying into ⟨ which subgroup? ⟩, not a case the definition excludes. Edelsbrunner's gloss "it merges with an older class" is exact for H₀, where deaths really are merges of components and the Elder Rule names which one survives. In higher degree the other way happens too, and Segment 3's triangle shows it: the 1-cycle born at edge 6 dies when the triangle makes it a boundary, with no older class to merge with at all.

The persistence of γ is pers(γ) = aj − ai, the difference in function value; the index persistence is j − i. A class born and never dying gets ∞ for both. Two classes can share an index persistence and differ wildly in persistence, so a stability theorem — which is about perturbing f — must be about ⟨ which of the two? ⟩.

Self-check 1. Edelsbrunner remarks that births and deaths "can also be defined for a sequence of vector spaces that are not necessarily homology groups. All we need is a finite sequence and homomorphisms from left to right." What does that remark give up?

It gives up the topology and keeps everything else. Birth, death and multiplicity were defined from images of composites and nothing else, so nothing in them knew that the vector spaces were homology groups. This remark is the whole of Definition 1.7 in one sentence, three chapters early and without the name — except that Oudot will also drop "finite", which Edelsbrunner keeps.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The diagram, and why it loses nothing

The persistent Betti numbers form a two-parameter family, which is awkward. The diagram compresses them to a multiset of points. Let μpi,j be the number of p-dimensional classes born at Ki and dying entering Kj. Then

μpi,j = (βpi,j−1 − βpi,j) − (βpi−1,j−1 − βpi−1,j)

for all i < j and all p. The first bracket counts classes born at or before Ki that die entering Kj; the second counts those born at or before Ki−1 that do the same; the difference is those born exactly at Ki. Drawing each point (ai, aj) with multiplicity μpi,j gives the p-th persistence diagram Dgmp(f). Multiplicities are defined only for i < j, so every point lies strictly above the diagonal, and the vertical distance to the diagonal is the persistence. Edelsbrunner then adds the diagonal itself, each point with infinite multiplicity, "for technical reasons which will become clear in the next chapter" — those reasons are tda1-06's, where the bottleneck matching needs somewhere to send unmatched points — p. 181.

Reading β back off the diagram is a quadrant count: βpk,l is the number of points in the upper-left quadrant with corner (ak, al). A class born at Ki and dying entering Kj is counted exactly when ai ≤ ak and aj > al — so the quadrant is closed along its vertical right side and open along its horizontal lower side. The asymmetry is not decoration; it is the difference between "born by then" and "not yet dead by then".

Fundamental Lemma of Persistent Homology — Edelsbrunner, p. 181

Let ∅ = K₀ ⊆ K₁ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Kn = K be a filtration. For every pair of indices 0 ≤ k ≤ l ≤ n and every dimension p, the p-th persistent Betti number is βpk,l = Σi≤k Σj>l μpi,j.

Edelsbrunner's gloss is one sentence: "the diagram encodes the entire information about persistent homology groups." Worth being exact about the scope of that. It says the multiset of points determines every βpk,l, which is a genuine completeness statement and the reason diagrams are used at all. It does not say the diagram determines the filtration — different filtrations routinely share a diagram — nor that it determines the homology classes, which have been discarded in favour of counting them.

Self-check 2. Why is the Fundamental Lemma of Persistent Homology a lemma about inverting the definition of μ?

μpi,j is a second difference of the persistent Betti numbers, and the lemma is the corresponding double summation. The content is that the alternating cancellation goes through — a discrete inclusion–exclusion. Non-negativity of μ is true and is a separate matter, and finiteness is automatic for a finite filtration.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

One reduction, one pass, and the module it computes

All of this is computed by a single matrix reduction, which is the payoff of tda1-02 and the test of its prediction. Order the simplices σ₁, …, σm compatibly: f(σi) < f(σj) implies i < j, and so does σi being a face of σj. Monotonicity of f is exactly what makes such an ordering exist, and every initial subsequence is then a subcomplex. Build one boundary matrix ∂ over all dimensions at once, with ∂[i, j] = 1 exactly when σi is a codimension-one face of σj — pp. 181–182.

The persistence algorithm — Edelsbrunner, p. 182

Let low(j) be the row index of the lowest one in column j, undefined if the column is zero. Call R reduced if low(j) ≠ low(j′) for any two distinct non-zero columns. Then: R = ∂; for j = 1 to m, while there exists j′ < j with low(j′) = low(j), add column j′ to column j.

Worked, then fading: the prediction, checked.

tda1-02 derived, from tda1-01's Definition 2.1 and the description of REDUCE, that a filtered reduction may only let a basis element absorb elements of grade no larger. Adding an earlier column to a later one is admissible on that test; free pivot exchange is not. The algorithm above performs exactly one operation, and it is the admissible one. The prediction holds.

Where it was looser than the book is the more useful question. The constraint permitted adding any earlier column to any later one; the algorithm adds only when the lowest ones collide, and always sweeps left to right. That extra discipline is what buys the two structural facts below, neither of which follows from the constraint alone.

And the last part of the prediction: a one-sided reduction cannot produce a diagonal, so something else must carry the answer. It is the lowest ones. Since ∂ is upper triangular — every simplex is preceded by its proper faces — and additions run left to right, V and R are upper triangular too, and R = ∂ · V. Counting zero columns and lowest ones by dimension gives #Zerop(R) = rank Zp and #Lowp(R) = rank Bp, so βp = #Zerop − #Lowp — tda1-02's formula, read off a matrix that is nowhere near normal form. And the pairing of a row to a column by a lowest one is ⟨ what, in the language of Segment 1? ⟩.

The example drawn above is Edelsbrunner's own — pp. 184–185. K is a triangle with its faces; the filtration adds the three vertices, then the three edges, then the triangle, numbered 1 to 7 — and a dummy simplex of index 0, the non-zero element of the (−1)-st reduced chain group, so that the computation is of reduced homology. The augmentation makes the boundary of each vertex that dummy simplex. Reduction leaves four non-zero columns. The lowest ones are at (0, 1), (2, 4), (3, 5) and (6, 7): vertex 1 kills the (−1)-dimensional class; vertices 2 and 3 give birth to 0-cycles killed by edges 4 and 5; edge 6 kills nothing, its column reducing to zero, and gives birth to the 1-cycle that the triangle 7 kills.

Guided proof — the Pairing Lemma.

The reduced matrix R is not unique: one may stop as soon as it is reduced or keep going. Yet the pairing had better not depend on that. Edelsbrunner's fix is to characterise the lowest ones by something manifestly independent of the reduction. Write Rij for the lower-left submatrix of R with corner R[i, j], and

rR(i, j) = rank Rij − rank Ri+1j + rank Ri+1j−1 − rank Rij−1.

Pairing Lemma. i = low(j) if and only if r∂(i, j) = 1. In particular the pairing defined by the lowest ones does not depend on R.

Your task: prove it. Establish first that rank Rij equals the number of non-zero columns of Rij, then evaluate rR(i, j) in the case where R[i, j] is a lowest one and in the two sub-cases where it is not. Finally say why rR = r∂.

Reveal the model answer

Rank equals the number of non-zero columns. In a reduced matrix distinct non-zero columns have distinct lowest ones. In any non-trivial linear combination of them, look at the column whose lowest one sits lowest: its entry in that row is 1 and every other column in the combination is zero there, so nothing can cancel it and the combination is non-zero. Hence the non-zero columns are independent, and they span, so they are a basis. The same holds for every submatrix Rij, since deleting rows above i and columns after j leaves a reduced matrix.

Book-keeping that makes all three cases short. A column is non-zero in Rij exactly when its index is at most j and its lowest one lies in row i or below — if the lowest one were above row i, every entry from row i down would be zero. Let c be the number of columns among 1, …, j−1 whose lowest one is in row i or below.

Case R[i, j] is a lowest one. Then rank Rij = c + 1, counting column j as well. Removing row i kills column j and nothing else, since lowest ones are distinct, so rank Ri+1j = c; and both j−1 submatrices have rank c, for the same reason. So rR(i, j) = (c+1) − c + c − c = 1.

Case R[i, j] is not a lowest one, sub-case A: no column among 1, …, j−1 has its lowest one in row i. Then Rij and Ri+1j have the same number of non-zero columns, and so do Rij−1 and Ri+1j−1. The four terms cancel in two pairs and rR(i, j) = 0.

Sub-case B: some column among 1, …, j−1 has its lowest one in row i. Then Rij has one more non-zero column than Ri+1j, and Rij−1 has one more than Ri+1j−1 — the same extra column, since its index is below j. The first difference contributes +1 and the second contributes −1, so rR(i, j) = 0 again.

Why rR = r∂. Left-to-right column operations preserve the rank of every lower-left submatrix: adding column j′ to column j with j′ < j does not change the span of the first j columns, and the row deletion is the same on both sides. So rank Rij = rank ∂ij for all i, j, and the four ranks in r are the same computed from either matrix. Since r∂ is defined without reference to any reduction, the lowest ones are too — and this is where the one-sidedness of the algorithm is spent. A reduction allowed to add later columns to earlier ones would not preserve these submatrix ranks, and the pairing would be an artifact.

Now name what has been built. Oudot regards (T, ≤) as a category, one object per element and a single morphism per pair i ≤ j, and defines a representation to be a functor to vector spaces: spaces Vi and maps vij subject to vii = 1 and vik = vjk ∘ vij — Oudot §1.1, p. 19, constraints (1.10).

Definition 1.7 — Oudot, p. 20

Given T ⊆ ℝ, a persistence module over T is a representation of the poset (T, ≤).

Edelsbrunner's construction is one: take T = {0, 1, …, n}, Vi = Hp(Ki) and vij = fpi,j. Both constraints hold — the first because the inclusion Ki ⊆ Ki is the identity, the second because composing inclusions gives an inclusion and homology is a functor, which is at1-10's content. Under the identification, βpi,j = rank vij, and Edelsbrunner's whole apparatus of births and deaths is a statement about ranks of composites in a module. What the module discards is the cycles and boundaries themselves; what Edelsbrunner uses without naming is precisely the composition constraint, every time he compares Hpi,j with Hpi−1,j. Definition 1.8 covers the case where some arrows run backwards — zigzag modules, representations of an An-type quiver — which no filtration produces, since inclusions all point one way.

Theorem 1.9 (Interval Decomposition) — Oudot, p. 20

A representation 𝕍 of the poset (T, ⪯) decomposes as a direct sum of interval modules when (i) T is finite and the Hasse diagram of ⪯ is of type An, or (ii) T is arbitrary, ⪯ is the natural order, and 𝕍 is pointwise finite-dimensional. The decomposition, when it exists, is unique up to isomorphism and permutation, and each term is indecomposable.

Self-check 3. Case (i) of Theorem 1.9 applies to Edelsbrunner's filtration. Why does he never invoke it?

The algorithm gives a pairing, and the Pairing Lemma shows it does not depend on the reduction — so Edelsbrunner earns his own uniqueness, by a completely different route. What Theorem 1.9 adds is that the pairing corresponds to a decomposition of the module into interval summands, unique up to isomorphism and permutation. That is tda1-04's subject. ℤ₂ is a field, and {0, …, n} with the natural order has Hasse diagram Ln, which case (i) explicitly names.

Self-check 4. Oudot's Rule 1.10 picks, among intervals of ℝ meeting T in a given interval S, the smallest by inclusion. Why does no such ambiguity arise for Edelsbrunner?

His intervals arrive as a pair (i, j) from the algorithm, so there is nothing to choose. Oudot's problem is the reverse one: given a subset S of T that happens to be an interval, write it as an interval of ℝ cut down to T — and with T = {1,2,3,4} and S = {2,3}, the candidates include [2,3], (1,3], [2,4) and (1,4). Finiteness is not the issue: the ambiguity is at its worst for finite T, since a sparse T leaves more room between its points.

Reveal the skeleton

1. The Elder Rule is forced, and monotonicity is what makes it algebraic. Components merge and never split, so the merge tree is a tree; β(a, b) counts spanning paths only for the elder decomposition. A monotonic f on K has subcomplex sublevel sets, giving a finite filtration from ∅ to K; homology turns it into a sequence of groups, and Hpi,j = im fpi,j is both a subgroup of Hp(Kj) and the subquotient Zp(Ki)/(Bp(Kj) ∩ Zp(Ki)). Death is merging with an older class, not becoming a boundary.

2. The diagram is a second difference and loses no persistent Betti number. μ is defined from the β by a double difference; the Fundamental Lemma of Persistent Homology inverts it by a double sum. The quadrant that reads βpk,l is closed on the right and open below, because "born by k" and "not dead by l" are not symmetric conditions. Completeness is about the β and not about the filtration, which the diagram does not determine.

3. One left-to-right column reduction computes all of it, and the object it computes has a name. A compatible ordering exists because f is monotonic; ∂, V and R are upper triangular; βp = #Zerop − #Lowp; and the Pairing Lemma makes the lowest ones independent of the reduction by characterising them through submatrix ranks that left-to-right operations preserve. The sequence of groups is a persistence module in the sense of Definition 1.7, with βpi,j = rank vij; Theorem 1.9 says it decomposes into intervals, which is what tda1-04 turns into a barcode.
