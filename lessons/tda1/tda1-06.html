Metrics on diagrams: bottleneck and interleaving (tda1-06)

How far apart are two diagrams? Match points optimally (bottleneck) — or compare the modules directly (interleaving).

Predict before we start. Edelsbrunner writes that the bottleneck distance "satisfies all axioms of a metric". Oudot writes that it "is not a true distance though, even on the multisets not touching the diagonal". Both sentences are about the same formula. What is going on?

Edelsbrunner's diagrams "consist of finitely many points above the diagonal" — a restriction stated a paragraph earlier and not repeated. Oudot's counterexample is ℚ² minus the diagonal, which is not locally finite. Segment 1 settles it and names the class on which both are right.

Why this matters for the mission: The interleaving distance is the algebraic metric; bottleneck is its geometric shadow — stability relates them.

Three words in that strip to hold loosely. "Metric": neither distance is one, and each source supplies its own counterexample to the same missing axiom. "Shadow": Theorem 3.1 says the two distances are equal on 𝔮-tame modules, and an isometry loses nothing — shadow is the wrong picture, though there is a real asymmetry it may have been reaching for. "Stability relates them": stability is one of the theorem's two inequalities, and the omitted one is the half that makes diagrams worth computing at all. Segment 3 takes all three.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The bottleneck distance, and a contradiction that is not one

Oudot introduces the bottleneck distance first "because the intuition behind it is easier to grasp", and treats undecorated diagrams as plain multisets of points in the extended plane — his footnote records why he may: the metric "is oblivious to the decorations in the persistence diagrams" — Oudot §3.1.1, pp. 49–50. That is tda1-04's promissory note being cashed.

Partial matching, cost, and the bottleneck distance — Oudot, p. 50

A partial matching M : P ↔ Q is a subset of P × Q in which every point of P is matched with at most one point of Q and conversely. The cost of a matched pair (p, q) is ‖p − q‖∞; the cost of an unmatched point s is |sy − sx|/2, the ℓ∞-distance from s to its nearest point of the diagonal Δ. The bottleneck cost is

c(M) = max{ sup(p,q)∈M ‖p − q‖∞ , sups unmatched |sy − sx|/2 },

and the bottleneck distance is db(P, Q) = infM c(M).

Edelsbrunner writes the same thing differently. He adds the diagonal to every diagram with infinite multiplicity — "these extra points are not essential to the diagram but their presence simplifies upcoming definitions" — and then takes bijections: W∞(X, Y) = infη supx∈X ‖x − η(x)‖∞ — Edelsbrunner §VIII.2, p. 216. The two agree, and converting between them is the exercise: an unmatched point becomes a point matched to its own diagonal projection, at exactly the cost Oudot assigns it, and a bijection restricted to off-diagonal pairs is a partial matching.

The picture is Figure VIII.5's construction. Draw a square of side twice the bottleneck distance around each point of X; the matching is good exactly when every square contains its partner. The pair sitting on a corner is the one achieving the supremum — the distance is the smallest side length for which all the boxes still work. The two unattached points near the diagonal are matched to Δ instead: they are the noise, and their cost is their distance to the diagonal, which is small precisely because they are short-lived.

That is Example 3.2's point too. Oudot compares a function f with a noisy approximation f′, whose diagram has extra points near Δ. The genuine minima p, q, s pair with p′, q′, s′; the rest go to the diagonal. Two details are worth extracting. The two ways of matching {p, s} with {p′, s′} give the same cost, since p and s coincide — so the matching is not unique and the distance still is. And Oudot's footnote contrasts the bottleneck distance with the Hausdorff distance: Hausdorff asks only that every point be near some point of the other set, so one point of dgm(f) could account for both p′ and s′. A matching cannot double up, and that is what makes the bottleneck distance notice that dgm(f′) has two points where dgm(f) has two — pp. 50–51.

Worked, then fading: the contradiction, settled.

Edelsbrunner, p. 217: "Clearly, W∞(X, Y) = 0 iff X = Y. Furthermore, W∞(X, Y) = W∞(Y, X) and W∞(X, Z) ≤ W∞(X, Y) + W∞(Y, Z). We see that W∞ satisfies all axioms of a metric and thus deserves to be called a distance."

Oudot, p. 51: "The bottleneck distance is an extended pseudometric … It is not a true distance though, even on the multisets not touching the diagonal Δ, as for instance the multisets ℚ² ∖ Δ and (ℚ + √2)² ∖ Δ are distinct but at bottleneck distance zero — the infimum in (3.2) is zero but not attained in this case."

Take the counterexample seriously first. Every rational is within any ε > 0 of an element of ℚ + √2, so the two multisets admit matchings of cost ⟨ what? ⟩ for every ε, hence db = 0; and they are distinct because ⟨ why? ⟩. The axiom that fails is ⟨ which? ⟩ — and note that the failure is not that some matching is bad but that the infimum is ⟨ what? ⟩.

Now find the missing quantifier in each. Edelsbrunner's is a paragraph earlier: "Under the assumptions on the input functions considered in this book, the diagram consists of finitely many points above the diagonal." Oudot's is the next sentence after the counterexample: a compactness argument shows the infimum "is always attained when P, Q are locally finite multisets in ℝ² ∖ Δ", which "implies that db is a true distance for such multisets, which by Theorem 1.13 include in particular all undecorated persistence diagrams of 𝔮-tame modules."

So both are right, and the class on which they agree is ⟨ which? ⟩. Note what this makes of the word "Clearly" in Edelsbrunner's sentence: the claim is ⟨ true or false? ⟩ in his setting and ⟨ true or false? ⟩ in general, and the step it skips is exactly the compactness argument Oudot cites.

Self-check 1. tda1-05's guided construction predicted this definition before reading it, and flagged the triangle inequality as the hard axiom. How did that prediction do?

The construction was right: Edelsbrunner's W∞ is exactly what it predicted, bijections and all. And it correctly worried that the infimum need not be attained. But it attached that worry to identity of indiscernibles and then called the triangle inequality the hard one — where in fact Oudot proves the triangle inequality in a subordinate clause, "as matchings P ↔ Q and Q ↔ S can be composed in a natural way", and the compactness argument is spent on attainment. Right phenomenon, wrong axiom.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Interleaving: comparing the modules instead of their pictures

The second metric never mentions a diagram. Start with the case that motivates it. If f, g : X → ℝ satisfy ‖f − g‖∞ ≤ ε then f(x) ≤ i forces g(x) ≤ i + ε, so the sublevel sets satisfy

Fi ⊆ Gi+ε and Gi ⊆ Fi+ε for all i ∈ ℝ,

and these inclusions fit into squares commuting with the filtrations' own inclusions. Apply homology and the squares become diagrams of vector spaces, with cross maps φi : H∗(Fi) → H∗(Gi+ε) and ψi : H∗(Gi) → H∗(Fi+ε) — Oudot §3.1.2, p. 52.

Definition 3.3 — Oudot, p. 52

Let 𝕍, 𝕎 be persistence modules over ℝ and ε ≥ 0. An ε-interleaving between 𝕍 and 𝕎 is given by two families of linear maps (φi : Vi → Wi+ε) and (ψi : Wi → Vi+ε) making the diagrams (3.4) and (3.5) commute for all i ≤ j. The interleaving distance is di(𝕍, 𝕎) = inf{ε ≥ 0 : there is an ε-interleaving}.

Oudot's next sentence is the important one: the definition "is the direct translation, at the algebraic level, of the interleaving between filtrations, although it does not actually need filtrations to start with in order to be stated". Nothing in Definition 3.3 refers to a space, a function or a diagram — only to two modules over a common field. That is the same move tda1-03 met in Edelsbrunner's remark that births and deaths need only a sequence of vector spaces, and it has the same payoff: the concept outlives the construction that suggested it.

The categorical rephrasing makes the structure visible. A family φ = (φi : Vi → Wi+ε) making the first square commute is a morphism of degree ε, and morphisms of degree 0 are morphisms in the ordinary sense; write Homε(𝕍, 𝕎) and Endε(𝕍). Composition lands in Homε+ε′. When the composite runs from a module to itself it cannot be the identity, because the indices have shifted — but there is a canonical replacement, the (ε+ε′)-shift morphism 𝟙𝕌ε+ε′, which is just the family (uii+ε+ε′) taken from the module's own structure — p. 53.

Definition 3.3 (rephrased) — Oudot, p. 53

𝕍 and 𝕎 are ε-interleaved if there exist φ ∈ Homε(𝕍, 𝕎) and ψ ∈ Homε(𝕎, 𝕍) with ψ ∘ φ = 𝟙𝕍2ε and φ ∘ ψ = 𝟙𝕎2ε.

This is cat-04's mission strip, arrived at from the other side. There it read: a pair of natural transformations M ⇒ N(·+ε) and N ⇒ M(·+ε) whose composites are the 2ε-shift maps. Every clause matches — a morphism of degree ε is a natural transformation into the shifted module, and the shift morphism is the 2ε-shift map. The categorical phrasing makes clearer why an interleaving is not a morphism: the composites are required to be shifts and not identities, so φ and ψ are not inverse to anything, and there is no map Vi → Wi anywhere in the definition.

Self-check 2. Why must the composite ψ ∘ φ be required to equal the shift morphism rather than the identity?

ψ ∘ φ sends Vi to Vi+2ε. Those are different spaces, so "identity" does not typecheck; the shift morphism is the canonical thing of the right degree. The third option has a true consequence attached to the wrong reason — asking for degree-0 composites would force an isomorphism, and that is exactly what a 0-interleaving is, which is why di = 0 does not imply isomorphic only because the infimum need not be attained.

Like the bottleneck distance, di is an extended pseudometric. Symmetry is immediate; the triangle inequality holds because an ε-interleaving composes with an ε′-interleaving to give an (ε+ε′)-interleaving; and the missing axiom fails for the same structural reason. Oudot's counterexample: 𝕀[0,1] and 𝕀(0,1) are ε-interleaved for every ε > 0 but not 0-interleaved, so their distance is zero and they are not isomorphic — "if they were 0-interleaved, then by definition they would be isomorphic" — p. 53. Two different distances, two different counterexamples, one shared cause: an infimum that is not attained.

Two further properties, which Oudot attributes to Lesnick and states with the caveat that they were proved "under some restrictions". di is stable: di(H∗(𝓕), H∗(𝓖)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞. And it is universal: any other stable pseudometric d satisfies d ≤ di. Universality makes di the largest stable pseudometric, and largest is the useful extreme — a larger distance separates more pairs of modules, so a universal one is the most discriminating comparison that stability permits.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

What the two distances are to each other

Theorem 3.1 (Isometry) — Oudot, p. 49

Let 𝕍, 𝕎 be 𝔮-tame persistence modules over ℝ. Then db(dgm(𝕍), dgm(𝕎)) = di(𝕍, 𝕎).

Oudot splits the statement in two and names what each half is for. The stability part, db ≤ di, "guarantees that persistence diagrams are stable signatures". The converse stability part, di ≤ db, "guarantees that they are also informative signatures". Those are the right words: stability says nearby modules have nearby diagrams, so a diagram cannot be thrown off by a small perturbation; informativeness says diagrams that are close came from modules that are close, so a small diagram distance cannot hide a large difference. A signature with only the first property could be constant. The proof is tda1-07's — Oudot says the stability part "is reputed to be difficult to prove, whereas the converse stability part is easy once the stability part is given".

Edelsbrunner has the inequality but not the equality, and states it twice. For filtrations: two monotonic f, g on a simplicial complex K give W∞(Dgmp(f), Dgmp(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞. For functions, he first has to say which functions. Call a ∈ ℝ a homological critical value of a continuous f on a triangulable 𝕏 if there is no ε > 0 for which fpa−ε,a+ε is an isomorphism in every dimension; call f tame if it has finitely many homological critical values and all sublevel sets have finite-rank homology in every dimension. That is the third sense of "tame" in this module, and it is the one Oudot's footnote on p. 32 calls "a more restrictive condition than 𝔮-tameness" — Edelsbrunner, pp. 217–219.

Guided audit — the strip, in three words.

The strip says: "The interleaving distance is the algebraic metric; bottleneck is its geometric shadow — stability relates them." Settle each word against what you have read.

(1) "metric". Give both counterexamples and say, for each distance, on which class of objects it is a metric.

(2) "geometric shadow". A shadow is a lossy image. What does Theorem 3.1 say instead, and what is the right word? Then find the asymmetry between the two distances that is genuinely there.

(3) "stability relates them". Which half of Theorem 3.1 is stability, and what does leaving out the other half cost?

Reveal the model answer

(1) Neither is a metric in general. For db, ℚ² ∖ Δ and (ℚ+√2)² ∖ Δ are distinct at distance zero; for di, 𝕀[0,1] and 𝕀(0,1) are non-isomorphic at distance zero. In both cases the infimum is not attained, which is the same failure twice. db is a metric on locally finite multisets in ℝ² ∖ Δ, by the compactness argument Oudot cites, and Theorem 1.13 puts every 𝔮-tame module's diagram in that class. di is a metric on isomorphism classes only if one quotients further, and Oudot does not claim it is one at all — he says it is an extended pseudometric between isomorphism classes and leaves it there.

(2) Theorem 3.1 says the two distances are equal, so the map from a 𝔮-tame module to its undecorated diagram is an isometry onto its image. An isometry loses no distance information whatever, which is the opposite of a shadow. The right word is isometric image, and the theorem's name says so.

The real asymmetry is elsewhere, and it is worth keeping. di is defined for arbitrary persistence modules over a common field — Oudot says so explicitly, "there are no conditions on the persistence modules". db needs a diagram to exist, which needs 𝔮-tameness and Theorem 1.13. So the interleaving distance is defined more widely, and the isometry holds exactly where both are defined. That is a genuine sense in which one is primary and the other derived — but it is about domains of definition, not about losing information.

(3) Stability is db ≤ di: perturbing a module cannot move its diagram much. Leaving out di ≤ db leaves out informativeness — and without it, nothing rules out a signature that is stable because it is insensitive. The constant map to a single diagram satisfies stability perfectly. So the omitted half is the one that makes the diagram worth computing, and Oudot's own framing puts the two questions side by side at the head of the chapter: "are these signatures stable? are they informative?"

One last construction, because it explains what stability looks like when you watch it happen. Let f, g be monotonic on K and take the straight-line homotopy ft = (1−t)f + tg. Each t gives a diagram; stacking them along a third axis, each off-diagonal point traces out a path. The pairing of simplices changes at only finitely many t, and between changes each pair gives a straight segment, so the paths are polygonal — Edelsbrunner calls each one a vine and the whole multiset a vineyard. A vine begins at an off-diagonal point of X or at a diagonal point, and ends at an off-diagonal point of Y or on the diagonal. That the paths are connected is the content: points do not jump. Differentiating and projecting bounds the slope of every segment by ‖f − g‖∞, and that bound is the Stability Theorem for Filtrations — pp. 217–218.

Self-check 3. Edelsbrunner introduces the Wasserstein distance because the bottleneck distance has a "drawback". What is it?

His words: "insensitivity to details of the bijection beyond the furthest pair of corresponding points". The degree-q Wasserstein distance sums the costs instead of taking their supremum, which notices everything — at a price he states immediately, that it "requires additional properties to be stable". A more sensitive distance is harder to prove stability for, which is the same trade universality describes from the other end.

Reveal the skeleton

1. The bottleneck distance is an infimum over partial matchings, with unmatched points charged their distance to the diagonal. Oudot's partial matchings and Edelsbrunner's bijections onto a diagram containing Δ at infinite multiplicity are the same definition. Matching, rather than Hausdorff proximity, is what makes it notice multiplicity. It is an extended pseudometric in general — ℚ² ∖ Δ against (ℚ+√2)² ∖ Δ — and a true distance on locally finite multisets off the diagonal, which by Theorem 1.13 is every 𝔮-tame module's diagram. Edelsbrunner's "all axioms of a metric" is right for his finite diagrams and silent about the compactness argument that general ones need.

2. The interleaving distance compares modules with no diagram in sight. ‖f − g‖∞ ≤ ε makes sublevel sets interleave; homology turns that into Definition 3.3. Rephrased, an ε-interleaving is a pair of degree-ε morphisms whose composites are the 2ε-shift morphisms — cat-04's strip exactly. It is defined for arbitrary modules over a common field, it is an extended pseudometric with 𝕀[0,1] against 𝕀(0,1) as the counterexample, and it is universal: the largest stable pseudometric there is.

3. Theorem 3.1 says the two are equal, not that one shadows the other. Stability, db ≤ di, makes diagrams stable signatures; converse stability, di ≤ db, makes them informative, and without it a constant signature would qualify. Edelsbrunner has the inequality only, twice — once for monotonic functions on a complex and once for tame functions on a triangulable space — and proves the first by bounding the slope of every vine by ‖f − g‖∞. The real asymmetry between the two distances is that di needs no hypotheses and db needs a diagram to exist.

Not established here, and the strip is the reason. That either distance is a "metric": both sources give a counterexample to identity of indiscernibles for their own distance, and both failures have the same cause, an infimum that is not attained. That the bottleneck distance is the interleaving distance's "geometric shadow": Theorem 3.1 asserts equality on 𝔮-tame modules, so the passage from module to diagram is an isometry onto its image and loses no distance information at all. The asymmetry that is real — recorded in Segment 3 as a correction rather than a defence — is that di is defined for arbitrary modules over a common field while db needs a diagram, hence 𝔮-tameness and Theorem 1.13. And that "stability relates them": stability is one of the two inequalities, and the omitted converse is what rules out a signature that is stable by being insensitive.
