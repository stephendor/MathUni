Adjunctions (cat-06)

An adjunction is a pair of functors going opposite ways that are not inverse and not nearly inverse, but related by a bijection between two hom-sets — one on each side. Spivak's route in is the cleanest: for preorders, an adjunction is just f(p) ≤ q if and only if p ≤ g(q). Rewrite each side as "this hom-set is non-empty" and the biconditional becomes an isomorphism of hom-sets, which is the general definition. And the payoff, from Aluffi: once you know a functor is a right adjoint, you know it commutes with every limit, without knowing anything else about it.

Predict before we start. Both authors insist the hom-set isomorphism of an adjunction be natural. Predict whether the preorder case can show you what naturality is for.

Invisible. Any two functions between one-element sets agree, so the naturality squares commute for free and the condition costs nothing. That makes the preorder case a good on-ramp and a bad test — exactly the pattern cat-01 saw when every morphism in the ≤ category turned out to be a monomorphism vacuously. Naturality does real work only when hom-sets are large, and Segment 3's proof is where you see it doing that work.

Why this matters for the mission: Adjunctions organise the constructions (free complex, geometric realisation) that turn data into topology.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Neither source mentions simplicial complexes, geometric realisation, or data, so the parenthetical is entirely a forward pointer. The general claim, though, can be made precise here — and the interesting question is what "organise" cashes out to.

What is verified, and what is listed. Aluffi verifies exactly one instance: Example 1.16 establishes the free-group bijection HomSet(A, S(G)) ≅ HomGrp(F(A), G). He then adds that "the free functor is, as a rule, left-adjoint to the forgetful functor" — a remark, and the words "as a rule" are his. Spivak's Example 3.74 lists five families of adjunctions and introduces them with "If you know some abstract algebra or topology, here are some other examples"; he verifies none of them. So the pattern the strip relies on is, in these pages, one worked case and two informal generalisations. Problem 2(e) and Problem 5(e) ask the reader to audit that rather than absorb it.

What "organise" can be cashed out to, from these pages. Lemma 1.17: right adjoints commute with limits, left adjoints with colimits. That is a real and usable consequence — knowing a construction is a left adjoint tells you it preserves colimits, without knowing anything else about it, and contrapositively a construction that destroys some colimit cannot be a left adjoint. Segment 3 completes Aluffi's sketch of it. If the strip's "organise" means anything defensible from this unit, it means that.

And neither source proves Lemma 1.17. Aluffi says so: he "will not prove it in gory detail", offering instead to convince the reader that such statements "just boil down to suitable applications of the universal properties defining the various concepts". The guided proof completes his sketch along the strategy he names. This lesson does not say "Aluffi proves".

Segment 1 · ~25 min

From Galois connections to the general definition

Spivak builds the definition rather than announcing it. An adjunction between preorders P and Q is a pair of monotone maps f : P → Q and g : Q → P that are, in his phrase, "almost inverses": f(p) ≤ q if and only if p ≤ g(q). Not f(g(q)) = q, not g(f(p)) = p — a two-way condition relating one comparison to another.

Now recall from cat-01 that in a preorder, the hom-set P(a, b) has one element when a ≤ b and none otherwise. So "f(p) ≤ q" says Q(f(p), q) is non-empty, and "p ≤ g(q)" says P(p, g(q)) is non-empty. The biconditional says these are non-empty together — and since each has at most one element, that is the same as saying they are isomorphic as sets. Spivak: "either both are one-element sets or both are 0-element sets. This suggests how to define adjunctions in the general case."

Definition 3.70 (Spivak). For categories C, D and functors L : C → D and R : D → C, we say L is left adjoint to R, and R right adjoint to L, if for any objects c of C and d of D there is an isomorphism of hom-sets αc,d : C(c, R(d)) ≅ D(L(c), d) that is natural in c and d. Given f : c → R(d), its image g := αc,d(f) is called its mate, and conversely. We write L ⊣ R. — Spivak §3.4.2, p. 102

Aluffi's version. ℱ : C → D and 𝒢 : D → C are adjoint — 𝒢 right-adjoint to ℱ, ℱ left-adjoint to 𝒢 — if there are natural isomorphisms HomC(X, 𝒢(Y)) ≅ HomD(ℱ(X), Y) for all X, Y. More precisely, he adds, there should be a natural isomorphism of bifunctors Cop × D → Set. — Aluffi Chapter 0 §VIII.1.5, p. 492

The same definition, with the left adjoint written on opposite sides. Both authors put weight on natural, and both mean cat-04's Definition 1.15: the isomorphism must commute with the maps induced by morphisms in each variable separately. Spivak's footnote spells the square out — for f : c′ → c in C and g : d → d′ in D, the square relating αc,d and αc′,d′ commutes. Without naturality the condition would say only that two hom-sets have the same cardinality, which is almost no information at all.

Self-check 1. Why is naturality automatic in the preorder case?

Hom-sets are tiny. A naturality square asserts two composites of functions are equal; when the sets involved have at most one element, any two functions between them are equal and the assertion is free. This is the same collapse cat-01 recorded in Example 4.10 and cat-05 saw again when limits in a preorder reduced to meets. It is why the Galois-connection case is a good introduction and a poor test of understanding.

Worked, then fading: currying. — Spivak Example 3.72 and Exercise 3.73, §3.4.2, pp. 103–104

Fix a set B. For sets B and C, write CB for the set of functions B → C — written that way because if |C| = 10 and |B| = 3 then |CB| = 103, and in general |CB| = |C||B|. Then − × B is left adjoint to (−)B:

Set(A × B, C) ≅ Set(A, CB).

The idea, in Spivak's words: "if I have a function f of two variables a, b, I can 'put off' entering the second variable: if you give me just a, I'll return a function B → C that's waiting for the B input."

Spivak then points out that he has only said what these functors do to objects, and sets Exercise 3.73. Complete it. Given f : X → Y, the functor − × B should send it to ⟨ which morphism X × B → Y × B? ⟩. The functor (−)B should send it to ⟨ which morphism XB → YB, and is it pre- or post-composition? ⟩. Finally, curry addition + : ℕ × ℕ → ℕ to get p : ℕ → ℕℕ; then p(3) is ⟨ … — and note it is a function, not a number ⟩.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Mates, units, and the examples

The isomorphism of Definition 3.70 does more than compare sizes; it lets you move morphisms across. Given f : c → R(d), its mate is αc,d(f) : L(c) → d, and the two operations are mutually inverse because α is an isomorphism.

One choice of arguments is special, and it is the same trick cat-04's Yoneda proof used. Put d = L(c). Then the isomorphism reads C(c, R(L(c))) ≅ D(L(c), L(c)), and the right-hand side contains a canonical element: the identity on L(c). Pulling it back gives a morphism ηc : c → R(L(c)), the unit of the adjunction. Dually one gets εd : L(R(d)) → d, the counit. In the free-group adjunction ηA : A → S(F(A)) is the inclusion of the generating set — each element of A as a one-letter word.

Now the examples, and it is worth being careful about their status.

Example 1.16 (Aluffi), verified in the text. The free group functor F : Set → Grp is left adjoint to the forgetful functor S : Grp → Set: HomSet(A, S(G)) ≅ HomGrp(F(A), G), naturally. This is what the construction of the free group was "concocted" for — a set-function A → G is "the same as" a group homomorphism F(A) → G. — Aluffi Chapter 0 §VIII.1.5, pp. 492–493

Aluffi then adds: "This of course applies to every other construction of 'free' objects we have encountered: the free functor is, as a rule, left-adjoint to the forgetful functor." The italics on "as a rule" are worth keeping — it is a remark about a pattern, not a proved theorem.

Spivak's Example 3.74 is a list, not a set of results. He gives five families: (1) free constructions — free group, monoid, ring, vector space — each a left adjoint to a forgetful functor; (2) free preorders and free categories on a graph, likewise; (3) discrete things — discrete preorder, graph, metric space, category, topological space — each a left adjoint to the underlying-set functor; (4) codiscrete things, each a right adjoint; (5) abelianisation, quotienting a group by its commutator subgroup, a left adjoint to the inclusion of abelian groups. And he introduces the whole list with "If you know some abstract algebra or topology, here are some other examples of adjunctions." None is verified. They are offered for recognition, and that is a legitimate way for a book to proceed — but it means a reader who takes the list as established has been more generous than the author asked.

Self-check 2. In Example 3.74, which of "discrete" and "codiscrete" is the left adjoint?

Discrete on the left, codiscrete on the right — Spivak's items 3 and 4. The underlying-set functor therefore has adjoints on both sides, which is worth noticing: it is a right adjoint (to discrete) and a left adjoint (to codiscrete), so by Lemma 1.17 and its dual it preserves both limits and colimits. The fourth option is wrong for a structural reason: adjoints are not inverses, and Spivak's word for the preorder case was "almost inverses".

Self-check 3. What is the unit ηA of the free-group adjunction?

The inclusion of generators. It is obtained by taking the identity of F(A) in HomGrp(F(A), F(A)) and pulling it across the adjunction isomorphism to HomSet(A, S(F(A))). Notice this is the same manoeuvre as cat-04's Yoneda proof — evaluate at the one morphism that is always there — and it is why the universal property of the free group is exactly the statement that ηA is initial among maps from A into the underlying set of a group.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

What an adjunction buys: Lemma 1.17

Lemma 1.17. Right-adjoint functors commute with limits. That is, if 𝒢 : D → C has a left adjoint ℱ and 𝒜 : I → D is a functor, there is a canonical isomorphism 𝒢(lim 𝒜) ≅ lim(𝒢 ∘ 𝒜), if the limits exist. Dually, left adjoints commute with colimits. — Aluffi Chapter 0 §VIII.1.5, p. 493 — stated, with the detailed proof declined

Aluffi's gloss: "As every good calculus student should readily understand, this says that right-adjoint functors are continuous. (Needless to say, left-adjoint functors turn out to commute with colimits and would rightly be termed cocontinuous.)"

Aluffi then declines the proof, in terms worth quoting because they set the exercise: "We will not prove Lemma 1.17 in gory detail, but I will endeavor to convince the reader that such statements are easier than they look and just boil down to suitable applications of the universal properties defining the various concepts. By contrast, proving that a specific given functor preserves products (for instance), without appealing to abstract nonsense, may sometimes appear to involve some 'real' work."

Guided proof. Complete Aluffi's sketch along the strategy he names: apply the universal properties. Show that if 𝒢 is a right adjoint then 𝒢(lim 𝒜) is a limit of 𝒢 ∘ 𝒜. Track, at each step, which universal property you are using — the limit's or the adjunction's — and find the one place naturality is needed. Write your argument before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

Setup. Let L = lim 𝒜 with projections λI : L → 𝒜(I), satisfying λJ = 𝒜(α) ∘ λI for every α : I → J in I. That is cat-05's Definition 3.92.

Step 1: 𝒢(L) is a cone. Apply 𝒢 to get 𝒢(λI) : 𝒢(L) → 𝒢(𝒜(I)). The cone condition survives because functors preserve composites — cat-03's observation that commutative diagrams go to commutative diagrams. So 𝒢(L) with these projections is a cone over 𝒢 ∘ 𝒜. Functoriality only; no adjunction yet.

Step 2: transport an arbitrary cone across. Let (M, μ∗) be any cone over 𝒢 ∘ 𝒜 in C, so μI : M → 𝒢(𝒜(I)). The adjunction gives, for each I, a mate μ′I : ℱ(M) → 𝒜(I). Adjunction's universal property.

Step 3: the transported family is a cone. We need μ′J = 𝒜(α) ∘ μ′I. This is exactly where naturality is used. The naturality of αc,d in the second variable, applied at the morphism 𝒜(α) : 𝒜(I) → 𝒜(J), says that taking the mate commutes with post-composing by 𝒜(α) upstairs and by 𝒢(𝒜(α)) downstairs. Since (M, μ∗) is a cone, μJ = 𝒢(𝒜(α)) ∘ μI; pushing both sides across gives μ′J = 𝒜(α) ∘ μ′I. Without naturality the mates would be unrelated and nothing would force the transported family to be a cone at all.

Step 4: use the limit downstairs. (ℱ(M), μ′∗) is a cone over 𝒜, so by the universal property of L there is a unique σ : ℱ(M) → L with λI ∘ σ = μ′I for all I. Limit's universal property.

Step 5: transport back. Take the mate of σ to get σ̄ : M → 𝒢(L). Naturality again in the second variable, applied at λI : L → 𝒜(I) — a morphism of D, so it moves the target of the hom-set isomorphism — shows 𝒢(λI) ∘ σ̄ = μI, so σ̄ is a morphism of cones. And it is unique, because σ was unique and taking mates is a bijection. Adjunction plus limit.

Conclusion. Every cone over 𝒢 ∘ 𝒜 admits exactly one morphism to 𝒢(L), so 𝒢(L) is terminal in Cone(𝒢 ∘ 𝒜) — that is, 𝒢(lim 𝒜) ≅ lim(𝒢 ∘ 𝒜). ∎

What to notice. Aluffi's claim about the proof is accurate: nothing happens except applying two universal properties alternately, and the only substantive step is Step 3, where naturality converts "a compatible family of morphisms" into "a compatible family of mates". Skipping it is the standard way this argument goes wrong, because Steps 2, 4 and 5 all look like they work without it.

The lemma is worth having because of what it lets you conclude about functors you know almost nothing about. The forgetful functor Grp → Set is a right adjoint, so it preserves products: the underlying set of G × H is the product of the underlying sets. Checking that directly means verifying a universal property twice; here it is free. And the contrapositive is the sharper tool — a construction that fails to preserve some limit cannot be a right adjoint, and one that destroys a colimit cannot be a left adjoint.

You try. Spivak's Example 3.74 item 4 says codiscrete constructions are right adjoints to the underlying-set functor, while item 3 says discrete ones are left adjoints to it. Conclude that the underlying-set functor has adjoints on both sides, and say what Lemma 1.17 and its dual then give you about it. Check one of the two conclusions directly for a small case.

Then the harder half: use the contrapositive of Lemma 1.17 to argue that some functor you know is not a right adjoint, by exhibiting a limit it fails to preserve. Say honestly whether you can complete the argument from this unit's sources or whether you need a fact from elsewhere.

Self-check 4. In the guided proof, where is naturality of the adjunction isomorphism used?

Step 3. Steps 1 and 4 use functoriality and the limit's universal property respectively, and neither touches naturality. Step 2 needs only that α is a bijection. But the transported morphisms have to satisfy the cone condition, and nothing about a bare family of bijections would give that — it is naturality in the second variable, applied at 𝒜(α), that makes taking mates commute with post-composition. The last option is the standard error: the bijection moves individual morphisms, naturality moves whole diagrams.

Reveal the skeleton

1. An adjunction is a natural isomorphism of hom-sets, and the preorder case builds it. For preorders: f(p) ≤ q iff p ≤ g(q). Since a preorder hom-set has one element or none, that biconditional says Q(f(p), q) ≅ P(p, g(q)), which generalises to Definition 3.70 — αc,d : C(c, R(d)) ≅ D(L(c), d), natural in both variables, written L ⊣ R. Aluffi's version is the same with the sides swapped, plus the note that the precise form is a natural isomorphism of bifunctors Cop × D → Set. Naturality is invisible in the preorder case because its hom-sets are too small for any square to fail.

2. Mates, the unit, and examples of two different statuses. The isomorphism transports morphisms; feeding the identity on L(c) backwards gives the unit ηc : c → R(L(c)), which for free groups is the inclusion of generators — the same evaluate-at-the-identity trick as cat-04's Yoneda proof. Aluffi verifies one example, the free group adjunction, and remarks that free functors are left adjoints "as a rule". Spivak lists five families — free constructions, free preorders and categories, discrete, codiscrete, abelianisation — prefaced by "If you know some abstract algebra or topology", and verifies none.

3. Lemma 1.17 is what an adjunction buys. Right adjoints commute with limits; left adjoints with colimits; Aluffi calls this continuity and cocontinuity, and declines the detailed proof, saying such statements "boil down to suitable applications of the universal properties". They do: apply 𝒢 to the limit cone (functoriality), transport an arbitrary cone across by mates (the adjunction), check the transported family is still a cone (naturality — the one substantive step), use the limit downstairs, transport the comparison morphism back. The dividend is that the forgetful functor preserves products for free, and the contrapositive rules constructions out of being adjoints at all.
