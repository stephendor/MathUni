Homology and persistence as functors (cat-07)

Six units of category theory arrive here. Oudot's Definition 1.7 says a persistence module over T ⊆ ℝ is a representation of the poset (T, ≤) — and a few lines earlier he says what a representation of a poset is: regard (T, ≤) as a category "in the natural way", with one object per element and one morphism per comparison, and take a functor to vector spaces. That is cat-01's Example 3.3 and cat-03's Definition 1.1, verbatim, arriving from a completely different literature. The object this degree has been circling is a functor out of a preorder, and the last thing this unit does is explain why the relation between two of them is not a natural transformation.

Predict before we start. Two persistence modules M and N are functors, so they are objects of a functor category and there is a notion of morphism between them: a natural transformation. Predict whether "M and N are ε-interleaved" means there is a natural transformation M ⇒ N.

No. A natural transformation M ⇒ N supplies a map Mt → Nt for every t; an interleaving supplies Mt → Nt+ε, whose target has moved. The two are different kinds of object and the second is strictly weaker — which is exactly what the mission strip's final clause says, and why it says it. The third option is nearly right and is the subject of Segment 3.

Why this matters for the mission: The categorical statement of the mission — persistence is a functor, and stability is expressed through interleavings of those functors (cat-04), not as a natural transformation in its own right.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. This is the most accurate mission strip in the module — it makes a claim, attributes the machinery, and then corrects an error most readers would make. Very little needs bounding, and what does is a matter of chapter boundaries.

"Persistence is a functor" is established here, by the source, in almost these words. Oudot regards (T, ≤) as a category — footnote 4: "with one object per element i ∈ T and a single morphism per couple i ≤ j" — and defines a representation of it to be a functor to the category of vector spaces; Definition 1.7 then names such a representation a persistence module. He adds that representations of any poset may be defined the same way, and later that the identity and composition rules "follow from functoriality". Nothing here is a rescope; the strip and the source agree.

Where Chapter 1 stops. Oudot's account of filtrations, and of the homology functor turning them into persistence modules, opens Chapter 2 on printed p. 29 — outside this unit's resources. Stability is Chapter 3, printed p. 49, also outside. So the composite producing a persistence module from a filtration is assembled in Segment 2 from cat-03's composition rule and at1-10's homology functor, with Oudot supplying only the target end of it; and the strip's stability clause is a forward pointer to tda1-06 and tda1-07.

The final clause is a correction, and it is correct. Given cat-04, the tempting reading of "stability relates two persistence modules" is that it supplies a natural transformation between them. It does not, and the strip says so. An interleaving is a pair of natural transformations, each into a shifted module, plus a condition on the two composites — three pieces of data where the tempting reading has one. Segment 3 does the comparison and Problem 5(d) makes the reader do it again.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The definition, and how little of it is new

Representations of a poset (Oudot). "Regarding (T, ≤) as a category in the natural way, we let a representation of (T, ≤) be a functor to the category of vector spaces." Footnote 4 gives the category: one object per element i ∈ T, a single morphism per couple i ≤ j. He adds that "more generally, one can define representations for any given poset as functors from that poset to the category of vector spaces". — Oudot ch. 1 §1, p. 19

Definition 1.7. Given T ⊆ ℝ, a persistence module over T is a representation of the poset (T, ≤). — Oudot ch. 1 §2, p. 20

Unwound: a persistence module assigns a vector space Vi to each i ∈ T and a linear map vij : Vi → Vj to each i ≤ j, subject to vii = id and vjk vij = vik. Oudot says later, of exactly these rules, that they follow "from functoriality" — and they do: they are cat-03's Definition 1.1 with nothing added.

Two observations before moving on. First, the category (T, ≤) is cat-01's Example 3.3, and Oudot's footnote is Aluffi's construction word for word. Aluffi required only reflexivity and transitivity, so his version is more general; Oudot's T ⊆ ℝ carries a genuine partial order, and antisymmetry is used nowhere in Definition 1.7. Second, Definition 1.7 introduces no new mathematics whatever. It names an object this degree already built. What earns Chapter 1 its length is Theorem 1.9, not Definition 1.7.

Definition 1.8 marks the boundary. A zigzag module of length n is a representation of an An-type quiver — a line of n nodes whose arrows may point either way. Oudot rephrases it as a poset representation for a partial order whose Hasse diagram is of type An, which "emphasizes its connection to Definition 1.7". Persistence modules are the case where every arrow points the same way.

Self-check 1. Oudot says the identity and composition rules for a persistence module "follow from functoriality". Which is which?

One each. cat-03's Definition 1.1 has exactly two conditions and they map onto Oudot's two rules one for one — which is why he can say the rules follow from functoriality rather than stating them as axioms. Antisymmetry plays no part: it is not used in Definition 1.7 at all, and cat-01's Example 3.3 needs only reflexivity and transitivity.

Worked, then fading: interval modules. — Oudot ch. 1 §2, pp. 20–21

An interval of T is a subset S ⊆ T such that for i ≤ j ≤ k in T, if i and k lie in S then so does j. The associated interval module has k at every index in S, the zero space elsewhere, identities between copies of k, and all other maps zero. Oudot notes the definition "is oblivious to the actual map orientations", which is what lets it serve zigzag modules too.

Check it is a functor. The identity condition is immediate. For composition, take i ≤ j ≤ k and consider the cases. If all three lie in S, both sides of vjkvij = vik are the identity on k. If i ∉ S or k ∉ S, then vik = 0 and at least one factor on the left is ⟨ … ⟩, so both sides vanish. The remaining case is i, k ∈ S with j ∉ S — and this cannot occur, because ⟨ which property of S rules it out? ⟩.

That last case is the whole reason the convexity condition is in the definition. Without it the interval module would ⟨ … ⟩ and would not be a functor at all.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Decomposition, and the composite the module was built for

Theorem 1.9 (Interval Decomposition). Given T ⊆ ℝ and a partial order ⪯ on T, a representation 𝕍 of (T, ⪯) decomposes as a direct sum of interval modules in each of these situations: (i) T is finite and the Hasse diagram of ⪯ is of type An; (ii) T is arbitrary, ⪯ is the natural order ≤, and 𝕍 is pointwise finite-dimensional. Moreover the decomposition, when it exists, is unique up to isomorphism and permutation of the terms, and each term is indecomposable. — Oudot ch. 1 §2, p. 20

This is what Chapter 1 is for. Oudot describes it as summarising the structural results of his §1 and as "our main heritage from quiver theory", and adds a phrase worth noticing: the conditions "are sufficient for our purposes". Sufficient, not necessary — a module failing both (i) and (ii) has not thereby been shown to lack a decomposition, and the theorem is not a characterisation.

The uniqueness clause is what makes a barcode exist as an invariant. A barcode is the multiset of intervals appearing in a decomposition; if a module admitted two essentially different decompositions, "the" barcode would be a property of the chosen decomposition rather than of the module. Uniqueness up to isomorphism and permutation is exactly the hypothesis that rules that out — and it is the same shape of guarantee as cat-02's Proposition 5.4, where uniqueness up to a unique isomorphism was what licensed the phrase "the product".

Guided proof. Assemble the composite this module was built to justify. Let (T, ≤) ⊆ (ℝ, ≤), let 𝒳 : (T, ≤) → Top be a filtration, and let Hp : Top → Vectk be homology with field coefficients. Show that Hp ∘ 𝒳 satisfies Oudot's Definition 1.7. Then say exactly which parts of the argument came from which unit. Write it before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

The index category. (T, ≤) is a category by cat-01's Example 3.3 — one object per element, one morphism per comparison — which is exactly the category Oudot's footnote 4 describes.

The first functor. 𝒳 : (T, ≤) → Top assigns a space Xi to each i and a continuous map Xi → Xj to each i ≤ j, with identities and composites matching. A nested family of spaces with inclusions is such a functor, since composites of inclusions are inclusions.

The second functor. Hp is a functor Top → Ab, established in at1-10 from Hatcher §2.3, where 𝟙∗ = 𝟙 and (fg)∗ = f∗g∗ are precisely the two functor conditions. Taking coefficients in a field k — at1-09's material — lands it in Vectk.

The composite. By cat-03, composites of functors are functors, so Hp ∘ 𝒳 : (T, ≤) → Vectk is a functor. Concretely it assigns Vi = Hp(Xi) to each i and vij = Hp(Xi → Xj) to each i ≤ j.

It is a persistence module. The functor conditions give vii = id and vjkvij = vik — Oudot's identity and composition rules, the ones he says follow from functoriality. A functor (T, ≤) → Vectk is a representation of the poset, and a representation of the poset is, by Definition 1.7, a persistence module over T. ∎

Where each piece came from. The index category: cat-01. The composition rule: cat-03. The homology functor: at1-10, from Hatcher. Field coefficients: at1-09. The target definition: Oudot, Definition 1.7. Nothing came from Oudot's Chapter 1 except the last step — his treatment of filtrations and of homology as the bridge opens Chapter 2, on p. 29, outside this unit's resources.

And one thing not shown. Whether this module is pointwise finite-dimensional, which Theorem 1.9(ii) requires, depends on the spaces Xi, and nothing in this unit's sources lets you check it for any particular family. That is owed by tda1.

Self-check 2. Why does Theorem 1.9's uniqueness clause matter more than its existence clause?

Uniqueness makes it an invariant. Both clauses are needed — without existence there is nothing to read off — but existence alone would leave the barcode depending on a choice, and an invariant that depends on a choice is not an invariant. This is the same structural point cat-02 made with Proposition 5.4: unique-up-to-isomorphism is what licenses the definite article. The fourth option is simply false; indecomposability of the terms is asserted by the theorem, not automatic.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

Why stability is not a natural transformation

M and N, persistence modules over (ℝ, ≤), are functors into Vectk. By cat-04's Exercise 1.9 they are objects of a functor category, whose morphisms are natural transformations. So there is already a notion of "map from M to N": a natural transformation in the sense of cat-04's Definition 1.15, or equivalently Spivak's Definition 3.49 — a family of linear maps φt : Mt → Nt, one per index, commuting with the internal maps of both modules.

That notion is too rigid for what stability needs to say, and seeing why is the point of this segment.

The shift. For ε ≥ 0, shifting by ε is a functor (ℝ, ≤) → (ℝ, ≤): if s ≤ t then s + ε ≤ t + ε, so it is monotone, hence a functor between the poset categories by cat-01. Therefore N(· + ε) — the composite of the shift with N — is again a functor (ℝ, ≤) → Vectk, that is, again a persistence module. Its value at t is Nt+ε. — assembled from cat-01 and cat-03; not stated in this unit's sources

ε-interleaving, as the mission strip states it: a pair of natural transformations M ⇒ N(· + ε) and N ⇒ M(· + ε) whose composites are the 2ε-shift maps. Written out, the first supplies maps Mt → Nt+ε for every t and the second maps Nt → Mt+ε; composing them one way gives Mt → Mt+2ε, which is required to be M's own internal map, and the other way likewise for N. — cat-04's mission strip; established in neither of this unit's sources

Now compare. A natural transformation M ⇒ N gives, at each t, a map landing in Nt — vertically, in the picture. An interleaving gives maps landing in Nt+ε — diagonally. Nothing in an interleaving lands in Nt at all, so the two are not merely different in strength; they are different kinds of data, and an interleaving cannot be compressed into a single arrow between M and N.

The case ε = 0 is worth doing, because it is where the two nearly meet. Then N(· + 0) = N, the two transformations are M ⇒ N and N ⇒ M, and the composite condition says each round trip is the identity. So a 0-interleaving is an isomorphism in the functor category — much stronger than a natural transformation, not weaker. As ε grows the relation weakens; at ε = 0 it is strictest. That is the right way round, and it is why interleaving is useful as a measure of how far apart two modules are.

You try. Take T = {1, 2, 3} and write down a persistence module that is not an interval module — three vector spaces and two maps. Decompose it into interval modules by hand and read off its barcode. Then check your decomposition against Theorem 1.9(i): which of the two conditions applies, and is the Hasse diagram of the right type?

Then the harder half: Oudot says Theorem 1.9's conditions "are sufficient for our purposes". Construct, or describe, a persistence module satisfying neither (i) nor (ii), and say what the theorem does and does not tell you about it. Be careful not to conclude more than the word "sufficient" permits.

Self-check 3. A 0-interleaving between M and N is:

An isomorphism. At ε = 0 the shift is the identity, so the two transformations run M ⇒ N and N ⇒ M, and the 2ε-shift maps they must compose to are the identity maps of each module. That is precisely a natural isomorphism in the sense of cat-04. Notice the direction of the relationship: interleaving gets weaker as ε grows and is strictest at 0, which is what makes "the least ε for which an interleaving exists" a sensible measure of distance.

Self-check 4. What does Oudot's Chapter 1 say about filtrations?

Nothing. Oudot's Chapter 1 is algebraic throughout: quiver representations, persistence modules as poset representations, interval decomposition, barcodes and diagrams. His treatment of filtrations and of the homology functor as the bridge from topology to algebra opens Chapter 2 on printed p. 29, and stability is Chapter 3. So the composite in Segment 2's guided proof was assembled from cat-01, cat-03, at1-09 and at1-10, with Oudot supplying only Definition 1.7 at the far end.

Self-check 5. Oudot says Theorem 1.9's conditions "are sufficient for our purposes". What follows about a module satisfying neither?

Nothing follows. The theorem lists situations in which a decomposition exists and does not claim to have listed them all — Oudot's phrase "sufficient for our purposes" is an explicit disclaimer of characterisation. Reading a sufficient condition as necessary is the most common way to over-read a theorem, and it is worth catching here, where the stakes are whether a barcode can be computed at all.

Reveal the skeleton

1. A persistence module is a functor, and the source says so. Oudot regards (T, ≤) as a category with one object per element and one morphism per comparison — cat-01's Example 3.3 — and defines a representation of it to be a functor to vector spaces; Definition 1.7 names that a persistence module. Concretely: a space Vi per index, a map vij per comparison, with vii = id and vjkvij = vik, rules he says follow from functoriality — and they are cat-03's two conditions exactly. Zigzag modules (Definition 1.8) are the same idea over an An order whose arrows may point either way.

2. Theorem 1.9 is what earns the chapter, and its uniqueness clause is what makes a barcode an invariant. Interval modules — k on a convex S, zero elsewhere — are the building blocks, and the convexity condition is exactly what makes them functors. Theorem 1.9 gives two sufficient situations for decomposing into them, plus uniqueness up to isomorphism and permutation. Existence yields a barcode; uniqueness makes it a property of the module rather than of the chosen decomposition, the same role Proposition 5.4 played in cat-02. "Sufficient for our purposes" is Oudot's own disclaimer against reading the conditions as necessary.

3. Stability relates modules diagonally, not vertically. M and N are objects of a functor category, so a natural transformation M ⇒ N is available — a map Mt → Nt for each t. An ε-interleaving instead gives Mt → Nt+ε and Nt → Mt+ε, with both composites required to be the 2ε-shift maps: three pieces of data, none of which lands in Nt. Shifting is a functor because ε ≥ 0 makes it monotone, so N(· + ε) is again a module. At ε = 0 an interleaving is a natural isomorphism, and the relation weakens as ε grows — which is what makes the least such ε a distance.
