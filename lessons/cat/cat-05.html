Limits and colimits (cat-05)

Spivak opens §3.5 with a question: what do products of sets, the results of Π!-operations on databases, and meets in a preorder have in common? They look nothing alike. The answer is that all three are limits — and once you have the definition, terminal objects, products, intersections and inverse limits stop being separate constructions and become the same construction over different indexing categories. Then reverse every arrow and the whole thing happens again on the other side, where it is called a colimit and where disjoint unions, quotients and gluings live.

Predict before we start. Limits and colimits are dual: a colimit in C is a limit in Cop. Predict: does it follow that a category with all limits also has all colimits?

No, and the slip is easy. Duality is a translation: every statement about colimits in C becomes a statement about limits in Cop. To conclude that C has all colimits you would need Cop to have all limits — which is a fact about a different category. A theorem proved for all categories does transfer, because it applies to Cop too; a property of one particular C does not. Keep the two apart; Problem 5(e) turns on it.

Why this matters for the mission: Quotient and gluing moves from la, aa, and top are all colimits — dual to the limits behind products and intersections; one categorical idea seen from both sides.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Its last clause — "one categorical idea seen from both sides" — is exactly Spivak's Definition 3.102 and is established here. The rest needs care, and one part of the care is about the sources themselves.

Spivak gives limits in full and colimits in one line, and says so. §3.5 builds terminal objects, products, cones, the category Cone(D), the limit as its terminal object, and pullbacks. Then Definition 3.102 says a cocone in C is a cone in Cop — and Spivak immediately adds that this "is like a compressed file: useful for transmitting quickly, but completely useless for working with, unless you can successfully unpack it. We will unpack it later in Chapter 6." Chapter 6 is not in this unit's resources. So the colimit half of a unit titled "Limits and colimits" arrives, from its first-named source, as a dualisation with the working-out deliberately withheld.

Aluffi §I.5 is what fills that gap, and it is the reason the split matters. He works coproducts concretely — disjoint union in Set by Proposition 5.6, max in the ≤ category — and treats the quotient as an initial object in Claim 5.5, which is the colimit side. Segment 3 therefore does the unpacking Spivak declines, for the smallest indexing category, and takes its concrete colimits from Aluffi. The section title "Bonus: An introduction to limits and colimits" is accurate and should be read as the warning it is.

"Quotient and gluing moves from la, aa, and top are all colimits" is not established. Neither source mentions linear algebra, abstract algebra or topology in this connection, and neither proves a general statement about quotients. Aluffi shows one quotient is an initial object; that is one construction, not a class of them. The claim is true and standard, and it is not on these pages. Problem 5(e) asks the reader to audit it construction by construction and to report which cases they can exhibit from the sources and which they are recognising from elsewhere.

Also not established: that intersections are limits, in the generality the strip implies. What is here is Spivak's Example 3.99: the pullback over a cospan, which in Set selects the pairs agreeing in the base — and for two subsets with their inclusions is their intersection. That is one case, worked, and Segment 2 does it.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The smallest limits

Definition 3.79 (Spivak). An object Z of C is terminal if for each object C there exists a unique morphism ! : C → Z. — Spivak §3.5.1, p. 108

This is Aluffi's final object from cat-02 under a different name — and cat-02 recorded Aluffi's advice against using "terminal" without saying which end you mean. Spivak uses it for this end only. In Set, any one-element set is terminal: for any C there is exactly one function C → {•}, the one that does the only thing available.

Proposition 3.84. All terminal objects in a category are isomorphic. — Spivak §3.5.1, p. 108

Proof. Let Z, Z′ be terminal. There are unique a : Z → Z′ and b : Z′ → Z. The composite a ; b : Z → Z must be unique since Z is terminal, and idZ is such a map, so a ; b = idZ. Similarly b ; a = idZ′. So a is an isomorphism with inverse b. ∎

That is cat-02's Proposition 5.4 again, argued identically. Spivak's Remark 3.85 adds the refinement Aluffi put inside the proposition: not only are terminal objects isomorphic, the isomorphism between any two is unique — "we hence say 'terminal objects are unique up to unique isomorphism'. To a category theorist, this is very nearly the same thing as saying 'all terminal objects are equal'." The word nearly is load-bearing, and cat-02's Self-check 5 is where it bites: in Set every singleton is terminal and ∅ is initial and unique on the nose, so "equal" is false while "unique up to unique isomorphism" is exactly right.

Definition 3.86 (Spivak). A product of X and Y is an object X × Y with morphisms pX, pY such that for every object C with f : C → X and g : C → Y there is a unique morphism ⟨f, g⟩ : C → X × Y making the diagram commute. — Spivak §3.5.1, pp. 108–109

The same universal property cat-02 took from Aluffi §5.4, notation aside. And the same collapse in a preorder: the product of two elements is their meet, which for ≤ on ℤ is min, exactly as Aluffi computed.

Self-check 1. Remark 3.85 says unique-up-to-unique-isomorphism is "very nearly" the same as equality. What is the nearly protecting against?

Genuine distinctness. Every singleton is terminal in Set and no two are the same set, so equality is simply false while the unique isomorphism exists and is canonical. The point of the remark is that nothing a categorical argument can see distinguishes them, so speaking of "the" terminal object costs nothing — but the objects have not become equal. cat-02's Proposition 5.4 makes the same claim from the other source.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Cones, and the definition that subsumes the rest

Definition 3.92 (Spivak). Let D : 𝒥 → C be a diagram — that is, a functor from an indexing category, in the sense of Definition 3.51 met in cat-04. A cone (C, c∗) over D consists of an object C and, for each object j of 𝒥, a morphism cj : C → D(j), such that for each f : j → k in 𝒥 we have ck = cj ; D(f). A morphism of cones (C, c∗) → (C′, c′∗) is a morphism a : C → C′ with cj = a ; c′j for all j. Cones over D and their morphisms form a category Cone(D). The limit of D is the terminal object of Cone(D). — Spivak §3.5.2, p. 111

That Cone(D) really is a category — identities, composition, and the clauses of cat-01's Definition 3.1, disjointness included — is the routine verification Problem 3(b) asks for, and it has to be done before the last sentence means anything.

Read that last sentence again, because it is doing a great deal of work in eight words. Terminality says: for every cone there is exactly one morphism of cones into the limit. "At least one" is the comparison morphism whose existence the usual formulation asserts; "at most one" is its uniqueness; and being a morphism of cones is the requirement that every triangle commutes. Three clauses, one word.

Everything from Segment 1 is now a special case, obtained by varying the indexing category alone.

Worked, then fading: the small indexing categories. — Spivak Examples 3.93, 3.94 and 3.99, §3.5, pp. 111–112

𝒥 empty (Example 3.93). A functor ∅ → C carries no data, so a cone over it is an object C with no components and no conditions to satisfy. Then Cone(D) has one object per object of C and one morphism per morphism, so it is C, and its terminal object is a terminal object of C. Terminal objects are limits over the empty diagram.

𝒥 two objects, no non-identity arrows (Example 3.94). A functor picks out two objects X, Y. A cone is an object C with morphisms to each — and the cone condition is vacuous, since ⟨ why? ⟩. So a cone is a pair (f, g), and the terminal one is ⟨ … ⟩. Products are limits over the discrete two-object category.

𝒥 the cospan • → • ← • (Example 3.99). A functor picks out X → A ← Y. A cone is an object C with maps to X, to Y and to A, and the cone condition forces the map to A to be ⟨ what, in terms of the other two? ⟩ — which is why Spivak says "the diagonal arrow ca is in some sense superfluous" and drops it from the picture. The limit is called a pullback, written X ×A Y.

In Set the pullback is {(x, y) ∈ X × Y : f(x) = g(y)}. Now specialise: let X and Y be subsets of A with f and g the inclusions. Then f(x) = g(y) says ⟨ … ⟩, so the pullback is ⟨ which familiar set? ⟩. Intersection is a limit.

Spivak's Remark 3.100 warns that this use of "pullback" is not the one in his Definition 3.68, where pullback meant precomposing a set-valued functor along a functor. Two concepts, one word, and for now best kept apart.

Self-check 2. Why does "the limit is the terminal object of Cone(D)" contain the uniqueness clause of the usual definition?

Exactly one. Definition 3.79 says a terminal object receives a unique morphism from every object, and "unique" decomposes into existence and uniqueness — precisely the two clauses the elementary formulation of a universal property states separately. The third and fourth options are both false: Cone(D) generally has many objects and many morphisms between them.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

The other side, and how far the sources go

Definition 3.102 (Spivak). Given a category C, a cocone in C is a cone in Cop. — Spivak §3.5, p. 113

And here Spivak stops. His next sentence: Definition 3.102 "is like a compressed file: useful for transmitting quickly, but completely useless for working with, unless you can successfully unpack it. We will unpack it later in Chapter 6 when we discuss electric circuits." Chapter 6 is not among this unit's resources. So the colimit half of this unit arrives from its first-named source as a one-line dualisation with the working-out explicitly withheld — which is why §3.5 is titled a Bonus and an introduction, and why Aluffi does the rest of the work below.

Unpacking it is not hard, and doing it is the point. Reversing every arrow of C turns a cone into: an object C together with, for each j, a morphism cj : D(j) → C — running into the apex — such that cj = D(f) ; ck for each f : j → k. And the terminal object of a cone category in Cop is an initial object of the cocone category in C. So: the colimit of D is the initial cocone.

Guided proof. Do the unpacking Spivak defers, for the smallest interesting case, and check it against a source that does work colimits concretely. Take 𝒥 to be the two-object discrete category and show that the colimit of D : 𝒥 → C is exactly the coproduct of cat-02. Then verify the two instances Aluffi gives. Write your argument before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

Unpacking. Let 𝒥 have two objects and only identities, and let D pick out A and B. A cocone under D is an object Z with morphisms iA : A → Z and iB : B → Z; the cocone condition is vacuous, since the only morphisms of 𝒥 are identities and D sends them to identities. A morphism of cocones (Z, i) → (Z′, i′) is σ : Z → Z′ with i′A = iA ; σ and i′B = iB ; σ.

The colimit is the coproduct. The colimit is the initial cocone: an object A ∐ B with iA, iB such that for every other cocone (Z, fA, fB) there is exactly one σ : A ∐ B → Z with fA = iA ; σ and fB = iB ; σ. That is, word for word, cat-02's universal property of the coproduct from Aluffi §5.5.

Instance one: Set. Aluffi's Proposition 5.6 gives A ∐ B = ({0} × A) ∪ ({1} × B) with iA(a) = (0, a) and iB(b) = (1, b); given fA, fB into Z, define σ by cases, which is forced by the commutativity. So disjoint union is the colimit over the discrete two-object category.

Instance two: the ≤ category on ℤ. A cocone under a and b is an integer z with a ≤ z and b ≤ z; the initial such is the least upper bound, max(a, b) — which is Aluffi's answer. Uniqueness of σ is automatic because Hom-sets there have at most one element, the collapse cat-01 recorded.

Duality, stated carefully. Everything above is the limit story with every arrow reversed, and that is what Definition 3.102 asserts. (Passing to Cop reverses the diagram's own arrows too; when one instead speaks of a cocone under a fixed diagram D in C, as the unpacking above does, D's arrows stay put and only the legs turn round. The two agree, and here the indexing category is discrete so there are no diagram arrows either way.) But note what has not been shown: that a category with all limits has all colimits. Duality relates C to Cop; it says nothing about C resembling its own opposite. In the ≤ category on ℤ both exist and are min and max; in other categories one may exist without the other. ∎

What to notice. The unpacking took a paragraph. Spivak's reason for deferring it is not that it is hard but that the compressed form is unusable until you have done it once — which is exactly why doing it here, rather than reading it, is the exercise.

Aluffi's other colimit is the quotient, and it is worth seeing that it is one. cat-02 proved Claim 5.5: the pair (π, A/∼) is an initial object of the category of pairs (φ, Z) with φ : A → Z collapsing equivalent elements. Initial, not final — the colimit side. What the sources do not quite give is the diagram it is a colimit of; the natural candidate is a coequalizer of two parallel arrows, and neither Aluffi's §I.5 nor Spivak's §3.5 constructs one. Problem 4(e) asks for that gap to be stated rather than papered over.

Self-check 3. In a preorder category, what is the limit of a diagram?

The meet. A cone is an element below every object of the diagram — a lower bound — and the terminal cone is the greatest such. The general definition collapses because Hom-sets have at most one element, so the "exactly one morphism" clause is automatic and only existence of the bound is at issue. Aluffi's min for the two-object case is the instance you have already seen; the colimit is dually the join, his max.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Terminal objects and products are the two smallest limits, and both are unique up to unique isomorphism. Definition 3.79: exactly one morphism in, from every object. Proposition 3.84 proves any two are isomorphic by composing the two unique maps — cat-02's Proposition 5.4 again. Remark 3.85 adds that the isomorphism is unique, so one may say "the" terminal object; the word nearly in "nearly the same as equal" is protecting against Set, where every singleton is terminal and no two are equal. Definition 3.86 gives products, which in a preorder are meets, hence min for ≤ on ℤ.

2. A cone over a diagram, and the limit as the terminal cone, subsumes all of it. Definition 3.92: an apex with a morphism to each object of the diagram, commuting with the diagram's own arrows; morphisms of cones; the category Cone(D); and lim(D) as its terminal object — eight words carrying existence, uniqueness and commutativity. Varying only the indexing category gives terminal objects (𝒥 empty), products (two objects, no arrows) and pullbacks (a cospan), and the pullback of two subset inclusions in Set is their intersection.

3. Colimits are the same idea reversed, and the sources divide over them. Definition 3.102: a cocone in C is a cone in Cop, so the colimit is the initial cocone. Spivak calls that "a compressed file … completely useless for working with" and defers unpacking to Chapter 6, outside this unit; the unpacking is done here for the two-object case and recovers cat-02's coproduct, with Aluffi supplying the concrete instances — disjoint union in Set, max in the ≤ category, and the quotient as an initial object. Duality relates C to Cop and does not imply that a category with all limits has all colimits.
