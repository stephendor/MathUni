Natural transformations and the Yoneda lemma (cat-04)

cat-03 made functors the arrows between categories. This unit makes arrows between functors, and then proves — or rather, works the exercise in which Aluffi asks you to prove — something startling about them. Fix an object X. The natural transformations from Hom(−, X) to any set-valued functor ℱ are in bijection with the mere elements of ℱ(X), and the bijection is given by asking what happens to the identity on X. One consequence: an object is completely determined by the maps into it. Aluffi's word for the general phenomenon is that any "there is a natural homomorphism…" is probably hiding a natural transformation.

Predict before we start. A natural transformation ν : hX ⇒ ℱ consists of one function νA for every object A of C — potentially a proper class of choices. Predict how much data is really there.

One element. That is the Yoneda lemma, and the mechanism is the identity morphism: νX(idX) ∈ ℱ(X) determines every other component, because naturality applied to φ : A → X forces νA(φ) = ℱ(φ)(νX(idX)). All the apparent freedom collapses onto the one object where a canonical morphism happens to live.

Why this matters for the mission: An ε-interleaving of persistence modules is a pair of natural transformations M ⇒ N(·+ε) and N ⇒ M(·+ε) whose composites are the 2ε-shift maps — the native language in which stability is stated (tda1-06/07).

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. It is unusually precise for a strip — it names the two transformations, gives their directions, and states the condition on their composites, which is exactly the clause that distinguishes an interleaving from an arbitrary pair of natural transformations. Nothing in it needs correcting. But almost none of it is established here.

Neither source contains persistence modules, interleavings or stability. Aluffi §VIII.1 and Spivak ch. 1 and ch. 3 have no filtrations and no barcodes; the word persistence occurs in neither. What this unit supplies is the grammar: Definition 1.15 and Definition 3.49 say what a natural transformation is, and Aluffi's Exercise 1.9 builds the functor category in which M and N live as objects. That is genuinely what the strip is made of, and it is all this unit gives.

One clause can be checked here, and checking it corrects a tempting misattribution. For N(·+ε) to be a functor at all, shifting by ε must itself be a functor P → P — and it is, for every real ε, since p ≤ q implies p+ε ≤ q+ε regardless of sign. So N(·+ε) is a composite of functors and is legitimate by cat-03 with no hypothesis on ε whatever. The condition ε ≥ 0 is needed somewhere else: it is what supplies the canonical maps Mt → Mt+ε internal to a module, and hence what makes the strip's clause about the composites being the 2ε-shift maps say anything. The strip states neither, and it is worth being precise about which one is doing which job.

Nothing here shows that interleavings exist, or that stability holds. The strip says what kind of object an interleaving is. Whether two given persistence modules admit one, and the theorem relating the smallest such ε to a distance between the inputs, are tda1-06 and tda1-07's. Problem 5(e) asks the reader to keep those two apart explicitly.

And on the Yoneda lemma: neither source proves it. Aluffi sets it, as Exercise 1.10 on p. 497, with a statement and a hint. Spivak gives only the preorder special case, at p. 20, and says of the general lemma that "we have not introduced it". The guided proof below is Aluffi's exercise worked from Aluffi's hint — a legitimate use of the source, since the statement is the text's and the proof is the exercise the text sets. This lesson does not say "Aluffi proves".

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Arrows between functors

Definition 1.15 (Aluffi). For functors ℱ, 𝒢 : C → D, a natural transformation ν : ℱ ⇝ 𝒢 is a morphism νX : ℱ(X) → 𝒢(X) in D for every object X of C, such that for every α : X → Y in C the square commutes: 𝒢(α) ∘ νX = νY ∘ ℱ(α). A natural isomorphism is one whose every component is an isomorphism. — Aluffi Chapter 0 §VIII.1.5, p. 492

Definition 3.49 (Spivak). To specify a natural transformation α : F ⇒ G, one specifies for each object c a morphism αc : F(c) → G(c), the c-component, subject to the naturality condition: for every f : c → d, F(f) ; αd = αc ; G(f). — Spivak §3.3.4, p. 95

The same definition again, in the two composition orders cat-03 warned about. In words: transform, then map, equals map, then transform. Every component is a morphism in the target category, and the whole content is that one square commutes for every arrow of the source.

Aluffi's remark is worth internalising. "Any statement such as 'there is a natural (or canonical) homomorphism…' is likely hiding a natural transformation between two functors. This technical meaning of the word 'natural' matches, as a rule, its psychological use." His example is the Hurewicz homomorphism π₁ → H₁ — two functors from based spaces to groups, and a homomorphism for each space, commuting with everything induced by maps of spaces.

The contrast that makes "natural" contentful is a family that is not. Take the identity functor on finite-dimensional vector spaces and the double-dual functor. There is an isomorphism V → V∗∗ that is natural, and an isomorphism V → V∗ that requires choosing a basis and is not: the square fails for a linear map that does not respect the chosen bases. Naturality is precisely the condition ruling out constructions that need an arbitrary choice.

The functor category (Aluffi's Exercise 1.9). For C small and D any category, there is a category DC whose objects are the covariant functors C → D and whose morphisms are the natural transformations. — Aluffi §VIII.1, Exercise 1.9, p. 497

Identities are the transformations whose every component is an identity; composition is componentwise. The smallness of C is what keeps the collection of natural transformations between two functors a set, so that cat-01's Definition 3.1 is satisfied.

This is the construction cat-03 used and could not build. There, the category TopP of filtrations was needed for the evaluation functor and was flagged as deferred. Here it exists: its objects are functors P → Top, and a morphism between two filtrations is a natural transformation — a map of spaces at each parameter value, commuting with all the inclusions. That is also, exactly, the shape of the M ⇒ N(·+ε) in the mission strip.

Self-check 1. Why does Aluffi require C to be small when forming DC?

Hom-sets must be sets. A natural transformation has one component per object of C, so if C has a proper class of objects the collection of transformations can fail to be a set — and Definition 3.1 was explicit that HomC(A, B) is a set even though Obj(C) is only a class. Composition and invertibility are componentwise and cause no trouble; it is the size condition alone that bites, which is the same class-versus-set difficulty cat-01 recorded and set aside.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The preorder rehearsal

Before the general lemma, Spivak's special case, which is small enough to see all at once. Let (P, ≤) be a preorder — cat-01's Example 3.3 again — and for p ∈ P write ↑p = {p′ ∈ P : p ≤ p′}, the upper set of p.

Worked, then fading: the Yoneda lemma for preorders. — Spivak Exercise 1.66 and the remark following it, §1.2, p. 20

1. ↑p is an upper set. Suppose p′ ∈ ↑p and p′ ≤ p″. Then p ≤ p′ ≤ p″, so p ≤ p″ by transitivity and p″ ∈ ↑p. Note that only transitivity was used.

2. ↑ is a monotone map Pop → U(P). U(P) is the set of upper sets ordered by inclusion. We must show that if p ≤ p′ in P then ⟨ which inclusion holds, and in which direction? ⟩. Suppose p ≤ p′ and take p″ ∈ ↑p′, so p′ ≤ p″; then ⟨ … ⟩ by transitivity, so p″ ∈ ↑p. Hence ↑(p′) ⊆ ↑(p) — the order is reversed, which is why the source is Pop.

3. p ≤ p′ if and only if ↑(p′) ⊆ ↑(p). One direction is part 2. For the converse, suppose ↑(p′) ⊆ ↑(p). We need an element of ↑(p′) to test with, and there is exactly one canonical candidate: ⟨ which element of P is always in ↑(p′), and by which property of the relation? ⟩. Feeding it through the inclusion gives ⟨ … ⟩, which is what was wanted.

Spivak's gloss: "to know an element is the same as knowing its upper set — that is, knowing its web of relationships with the other elements of the preorder."

Two things to carry forward. First, look at what made part 3 work: an element that is always present in ↑(p′), supplied by reflexivity. In the general lemma the same role is played by the identity morphism, supplied by the category axioms. Second, look at what is invisible here. In a preorder, Hom(p, p′) has at most one element, so ↑p records only whether there is a morphism, never which. The general lemma is about a set of morphisms, and it recovers all of them.

Self-check 2. Which property of the preorder plays, in part 3, the role that the identity morphism plays in the general Yoneda lemma?

Reflexivity. To use ↑(p′) ⊆ ↑(p) you need something in ↑(p′), and reflexivity supplies p′ itself for free. In cat-01's Example 3.3 reflexivity is precisely what supplies the identity morphism, so the correspondence is exact: the general proof evaluates a natural transformation at idX, and this one evaluates an inclusion at p′. Antisymmetry is not used anywhere — Spivak's statement is for preorders, as cat-01 would predict.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

The Yoneda lemma

Yoneda lemma — stated by Aluffi as Exercise 1.10, with a hint, and not proved in the text. Let C be a category, X an object, and hX := HomC(−, X) the contravariant Hom functor of cat-03. For every contravariant functor ℱ : C → Set there is a bijection between the set of natural transformations hX ⇝ ℱ and the set ℱ(X), given by sending ν to νX(idX). — Aluffi §VIII.1, Exercise 1.10, p. 497

Guided proof. Work Aluffi's Exercise 1.10 from Aluffi's own hint: "Produce an inverse of the specified map. For every f ∈ ℱ(X) and every φ ∈ HomC(A, X), how do you construct an element of ℱ(A)?" Write your argument before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

The forward map. Given ν : hX ⇝ ℱ, its component at X is a function νX : hX(X) = HomC(X, X) → ℱ(X). Since idX ∈ HomC(X, X), we may set Φ(ν) := νX(idX) ∈ ℱ(X).

Answering the hint. Given f ∈ ℱ(X) and φ ∈ HomC(A, X), the functor ℱ is contravariant, so ℱ(φ) runs from ℱ(X) to ℱ(A). Apply it: ℱ(φ)(f) ∈ ℱ(A). That is the only thing available, and it is what we want.

The candidate inverse. For f ∈ ℱ(X) define νf by νfA(φ) := ℱ(φ)(f) for every object A and every φ ∈ HomC(A, X). This is natural: for ψ : A′ → A, the functor hX sends ψ to pre-composition, and νfA′(φψ) = ℱ(φψ)(f) = ℱ(ψ)(ℱ(φ)(f)) = ℱ(ψ)(νfA(φ)), which is the naturality square — the middle step being contravariant functoriality, with the order reversed.

Round trip one, from ℱ(X) and back. Φ(νf) = νfX(idX) = ℱ(idX)(f) = f, using only that ℱ preserves identities.

Round trip two, from ν and back. Put f = νX(idX) and take any φ : A → X. Then νfA(φ) = ℱ(φ)(νX(idX)). Now apply the naturality square of ν at the morphism φ: it says ℱ(φ) ∘ νX = νA ∘ hX(φ). Evaluating both sides at idX gives ℱ(φ)(νX(idX)) = νA(hX(φ)(idX)) = νA(idX ∘ φ) = νA(φ). So νf = ν. ∎

What to notice. Naturality is used exactly once, in the second round trip, and it is the whole content: it is what forces every component of ν to be determined by the single value νX(idX). Everything else is functoriality of ℱ. And notice the mechanism — the proof works because HomC(X, X) contains a canonical element for free. That is the same reflexivity that supplied p′ ∈ ↑(p′) in Segment 2.

Aluffi's Exercise 1.11 draws the consequence. Call a contravariant functor C → Set representable when it is naturally isomorphic to some hX; then C is equivalent — in cat-03's sense, fully faithful and essentially surjective — to the subcategory of representable functors in SetCop. That is the precise form of "an object is known by its arrows": not up to equality, but up to isomorphism, exactly as cat-03's Example 1.8 warned is the most one can ask.

You try. Aluffi introduces adjunctions informally in the same section: ℱ and 𝒢 are adjoint when there are natural isomorphisms HomC(X, 𝒢(Y)) ≅ HomD(ℱ(X), Y). Check Example 1.16 — that the free group functor is left-adjoint to the forgetful functor — and verify naturality in the set variable. Then say what the word "natural" is ruling out, by exhibiting two functors whose Hom-sets are merely equinumerous without being naturally so.

Then the harder half: Aluffi says the formal version needs a natural isomorphism of bifunctors Cop × D → Set. Say why two variables are needed and what would be lost by asking for naturality in one only. cat-06 will take this up properly.

Self-check 3. In the second round trip, where exactly is naturality used?

In the rewriting. The naturality square of ν at φ says ℱ(φ) ∘ νX = νA ∘ hX(φ); evaluating at idX turns the left side into ℱ(φ)(νX(idX)) and the right into νA(idX ∘ φ) = νA(φ). Without it there would be no reason for the components at other objects to be related to the component at X at all. The third option is a different check — that one uses functoriality of ℱ, not naturality of ν, since νf is being built rather than assumed.

The grammar only. Definition 1.15 says what a natural transformation is, Exercise 1.9 builds the category in which M and N are objects, and cat-03's composition rule makes N(·+ε) well formed — for every real ε, since shifting is monotone whatever its sign. That is a real contribution and it is the whole of it. Blank page. Look away and reconstruct the skeleton: three load-bearing claims and why each forces the next.

Reveal the skeleton

1. A natural transformation is one square, repeated. Definition 1.15 and Definition 3.49: a component νX : ℱ(X) → 𝒢(X) for each object, such that 𝒢(α)νX = νYℱ(α) for every α. Natural isomorphism means every component is one. Aluffi's diagnostic: "there is a natural homomorphism…" almost always hides one, the Hurewicz map being his example. Naturality is what rules out constructions needing an arbitrary choice — V ≅ V∗∗ is natural, V ≅ V∗ is not. By Exercise 1.9, for C small the functors C → D form a category DC with natural transformations as morphisms; smallness is needed only to keep those collections sets.

2. The preorder case shows the mechanism. For a preorder, ↑p = {p′ : p ≤ p′}, and Spivak's Exercise 1.66 gives: ↑p is an upper set by transitivity; ↑ is monotone Pop → U(P), reversing the order; and p ≤ p′ if and only if ↑(p′) ⊆ ↑(p). The converse direction needs an element of ↑(p′) to test with, and reflexivity supplies p′ itself — the exact analogue of the identity morphism in the general proof. What the preorder case cannot show is anything about which morphism, since its Hom-sets have at most one element.

3. Yoneda: a natural transformation out of hX is one element. Nat(hX, ℱ) ≅ ℱ(X), by ν ↦ νX(idX). The inverse sends f to νfA(φ) = ℱ(φ)(f), which is the only construction the types allow. One round trip is ℱ(id) = id; the other is naturality applied at φ and evaluated at idX, and that single use of naturality is the whole content. Exercise 1.11 draws the consequence: C is equivalent to the representable functors in SetCop — an object is known by its arrows, up to isomorphism and never up to equality.
