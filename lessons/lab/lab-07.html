TDA in machine-learning pipelines (lab-07)

The obvious task: one circle against two circles, sixty point clouds, a persistence pipeline. It scores 0.9333. Six numbers containing no topology at all score 1.0000. Nothing in this lesson is about making the pipeline win that race — it is about the fact that without running the race you would have reported the 0.9333 as evidence for persistent homology, and it is not. Two negative controls do all the work here: a baseline, which tests whether topology earned the score, and a label permutation, which tests whether the score is real at all. The second one catches a protocol that reports 0.7167 accuracy from labels drawn at random.

Predict before we start. You cross-validate a classifier on features you chose, before the loop, by picking the 3 of 1000 most correlated with the labels. What does the cross-validated score measure?

Optimistic, and invisibly so. Selecting on all the labels means the features were chosen partly because they fit the test folds; by the time the loop begins there is no data held out with respect to that choice, so cross-validation cannot see it. Measured here: on real labels the leaky and honest procedures differ by 0.0167, one sample in sixty, indistinguishable from noise. On random labels the honest one sits at 0.5117 and the leaky one at 0.6242, reaching 0.7167. The second option overstates — the score is not meaningless, it is a real measurement of the wrong quantity. The fourth is the actual error people make: SelectKBest takes y, so it is supervised, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous.

Why this matters for the mission: End-to-end pipelines are how TDA earns its keep in an actual data-analysis project.

The strip is right, and "earns its keep" is a claim that has to be tested rather than assumed.

The mechanism is exactly as stated. A pipeline is the object that makes a topological feature usable: it lets cross_val_score clone and refit the entire procedure per fold, which is the only way a reported number refers to what you would actually run on new data.

But a pipeline earns nothing by scoring well. On the first dataset in this lesson the topological pipeline scores 0.9333 and a six-number moment baseline scores 1.0000 — so on that data TDA earned nothing, and the fault was in the dataset, which differed in scale as well as in topology. Only after the confound is removed, provably (the baseline features have spread exactly zero), does 0.9500 mean anything.

And a pipeline can report a number that is not a measurement. Segment 2's leaky protocol reports 1.0000 on real labels and 0.6242 on random ones. Its real-label score is higher than the honest procedure's. Nothing about the number tells you which you are looking at.

No syllabus action; the strip is accurate and states a goal rather than a result. This lesson supplies the two controls that decide whether the goal was met on any particular occasion.

Not claimed here. That persistent homology is or is not useful in general. One constructed dataset settles nothing either way, and the lesson's own numbers are the argument for saying so.

The baseline, and what a score is a claim about

Sixty point clouds: thirty from one circle of radius 1, thirty from two circles of radius 0.5 at centres ±0.55. Class 0 has one loop, class 1 has two. If persistence is good for anything it is good for this.

Two coordinate means and four covariance entries. No filtration, no complex, no homology. Perfect separation.

The dataset was broken and the pipeline would not have told me. Two circles of radius 0.5 at ±0.55 span ±1.05 horizontally and ±0.5 vertically; one circle of radius 1 is isotropic. The classes differ in shape of spread as much as in topology, and the last entry of the spread vector — 0.184615, an order of magnitude above the mean terms — is the tell. The topological pipeline scores 0.9333 on this data. Without the baseline that reads as "persistence distinguishes one hole from two"; with it, it reads as "something distinguishes these classes and the pipeline extracts it worse than six moments do".

Self-check 1. A cross-validated accuracy of 0.9333 from a persistence pipeline is a claim about what?

The procedure, and only the procedure. Attributing it to topology is an additional claim needing an additional experiment, namely a non-topological baseline — which here beats it. The fourth option is the subtle one and is still wrong: the pipeline's features are a landscape amplitude and a point count, so the score speaks about those two numbers, not about diagram space, and a different vectorisation could score differently on identical diagrams.
Faded — the repair, and why it is provable rather than empirical.

Whiten each cloud: centre it and set its covariance to the identity. The map is affine, so one loop stays one loop and two stay two — the number of long H₁ bars is untouched, though every birth and death moves.

Spread exactly zero, in every one of the six features. The baseline now scores chance, and it could not have scored anything else.

Self-check 2. Whitening is computed from each cloud, using that cloud's own covariance. Is that leakage?

No, and the criterion is worth memorising: a step must go inside the cross-validation loop if its output depends on any sample other than the one it is applied to, or on any label. Whitening depends only on its own cloud, so a single new sample can be whitened knowing nothing about the training set. BettiCurve.fit fails that test — its grid is computed from a whole collection — and SelectKBest fails it worse, because it depends on the labels. The third option confuses this with the standard global scaler, which is fitted across samples and does need to be inside the loop.

Why the transformer is a step and not a preprocessing line

lab-06 found that BettiCurve vectorises the same diagram to two different arrays depending on the batch it was fitted in. Put that inside a cross-validation loop and it stops being a curiosity.

Worked — the grid is refitted, and it moves. 
topological pipeline on the RAW clouds,      5-fold CV: 0.9333
topological pipeline on the WHITENED clouds, 5-fold CV: 0.9500

what a transformer inside the pipeline is refitted on, fold by fold:
  fold   n train   H1 grid upper end
  0      48        2.334698
  1      48        2.371028
  2      48        2.371028
  3      48        2.371028
  4      48        2.371028
Fold 0's training half happens to exclude whichever diagram carries the largest H₁ death, so its grid stops short.

That difference is the point, not a nuisance. In fold 0 the twelve test clouds are vectorised on a grid that knows nothing about them. Fix the grid once on all sixty and every training vector in every fold is computed on a grid whose upper end was set by a diagram in some test set — a quantity the model is not supposed to have seen. The score goes up, no error is raised, and the only visible symptom is a number that is slightly too good.

Self-check 3. The pipeline's feature is Amplitude(metric='landscape'), and lab-06 established Theorem 13.1: the landscape map is 1-Lipschitz in the bottleneck distance. What do the pipeline's predictions inherit?

Nothing. Three clauses, and only the first two carry the theorem: diagram to landscape is 1-Lipschitz (up to lab-06's discretisation caveat); landscape to number is a norm, hence 1-Lipschitz for its own metric, provided that metric is the one Theorem 13.1 bounds. Then logistic regression: Lipschitz with a constant set by the fitted coefficients, which nothing bounds in advance, and a decision boundary that turns an arbitrarily small feature change into a flipped label. The fourth option raises a real question — Amplitude does default to a norm the theorem does not directly bound, which is why the problem set asks for the three clauses separately — but it is not the reason the predictions inherit nothing.

The whole argument in four bars. Blue is the 5-fold cross-validated accuracy on the real labels; amber is the mean over 20 random permutations of those labels, with the open tick showing the largest of the 20. On real labels the two procedures are 0.9833 and 1.0000 — indistinguishable, and the leaky one is the higher. On permuted labels the honest procedure sits on the chance line at 0.5117 and the leaky one reaches a mean of 0.6242 and a maximum of 0.7167, from labels containing no information at all. The blue bars cannot tell you which procedure you are looking at; the amber ones can.

Guided — where does the extra 0.12 come from?

Before reading on. There are 1000 Betti-curve features and 60 samples, and the leaky procedure picks the best 3 against all 60 labels before the loop starts. Write down (i) what those 3 features are, when the labels are random; (ii) why 5-fold cross-validation afterwards does not detect the problem; and (iii) what would have to change about the data for the effect to disappear.
The mechanism, in three parts (i) They are the features that happen to correlate with this particular random label vector. With 1000 candidates and 60 samples some always will — that is what a thousand draws buys. They are not noise features that got lucky in some vague sense; they were selected because they fit these labels, test folds included.

(ii) Because the leak has already happened. Cross-validation protects the steps inside it and nothing else. By the time the first fold is cut, the feature choice has consumed information from every sample, so there is no data held out with respect to that choice. The loop faithfully measures the accuracy of a classifier on three features that were tailored to the answer.

(iii) More samples relative to features. The effect scales with the number of candidate features and inversely with the sample count; at 60 samples and 1000 features it is worth 0.12 of accuracy on pure noise. Reduce to 10 features and it nearly vanishes; increase the samples to 6000 and it vanishes too. Which is why it is most dangerous exactly where TDA lives — a persistence image or a Betti curve is hundreds of features from a study with dozens of subjects.

What a mean is, and why the pipeline avoids the question

lab-06 exhibited two diagrams with three midpoints. Dey and Wang §13.3 gives the general theory, and then says what it costs.

Definition 13.10 (Fréchet function, variance, and expectation), printed 406. Given a probability distribution ρ on 𝒟, its Fréchet function is ℱρ(X) := ∫ d²(X, Y) dρ(Y). The Fréchet variance is infX ℱρ(X), and the set at which it is attained is the Fréchet expectation. Defined, printed 406, on the space 𝔻pq of Definition 13.9, itself built on the refined Definition 13.8 of (p, q)-Wasserstein distance — the two indices matter, and Dey and Wang note the q-Wasserstein distance of their earlier Definition 3.10 is the case d∞W,q.
Theorem 13.10, printed 407. Let ρ be a probability measure on 𝒟 with a finite second moment. If ρ has compact support, then 𝔼(ρ) ≠ ∅. Stated, not proved; attributed to references [231] and [291], neither on disk. The same page records two things the theorem does not give: "in general, the Fréchet mean is not unique", and "the computational question for Fréchet mean … remains open".

So: a mean of persistence diagrams exists, is not unique, and cannot be computed. Every pipeline in this unit sidesteps all three by averaging in a vector space instead — four lines, no hypotheses, one formula.

Worked — the average that is available.

mean landscape of class 1, H1 layer 1, peak 0.148169
Class 0's mean second layer peaks at exactly zero.

Layer 1 is the second upper envelope, non-zero only where two bars are alive at once. One circle gives one long H₁ bar, so its second layer vanishes identically and the average of thirty such is exactly 0. Two circles give two overlapping bars, and the average is 0.148169. That single number is the cleanest topological reading in the whole module — "there are two loops and they coexist" — and it was obtained by averaging, which diagram space does not permit.

Self-check 4. Theorem 13.10 needs compact support and a finite second moment; the mean landscape needed no hypotheses at all. Why?

Different spaces, different objects. The Fréchet mean is an element of diagram space minimising a sum of squared diagram distances — an optimisation over an infinite-dimensional non-linear metric space whose geodesics branch, which is why existence needs a theorem and uniqueness fails anyway. The mean landscape is pointwise arithmetic in a vector space: no optimisation, no existence question. The first option is the tempting error — the landscape average is not an approximation to anything in diagram space, because by lab-06 it is generally the landscape of no diagram at all.

Self-check 5. The honest procedure's permuted accuracies had mean 0.5117 and maximum 0.6333. What should you conclude about a single reported accuracy of 0.63 on this dataset?

Not evidence. The permutation distribution is the answer to "what does chance look like here", and here its maximum over twenty draws is 0.6333. So 0.63 is inside the range that random labels produce, and intuition about "above 0.5" is worthless at this sample size — with 60 samples in 5 folds each fold is a count out of twelve, so the resolution is 1/12 ≈ 0.083 and a fold cannot even land on 0.5. The last option gets the logic backwards: an honest procedure's permuted scores scatter around chance, and that scatter is the useful output.
