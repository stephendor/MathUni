Bottleneck and Wasserstein distance; stability (lab-05)

Four units have deferred the same question. lab-01 found a long bar and a ratio of 98.1 and could not say whether 98.1 was enough. lab-03 confirmed the reduction returns every feature and ranks none of them. lab-04 called the diagram central and admitted the claim was underwritten. This is the unit where the threshold arrives — and it arrives with a condition attached that is not a theorem, and that the theorem cannot supply. Knowing which part is proved and which part you are asserting is the whole of using stability honestly.

Predict before we start. The stability theorem bounds how far a persistence diagram moves when the data moves. What does it let you conclude about lab-01's long bar?

It converts a bound on the input error into a bound on the output error, and the input bound is yours to supply. Given δ, no dataset within δ of this one has a diagram more than 2δ away, so a bar longer than 4δ cannot be created or destroyed by noise of that size — and at δ = 0.05 the bar of 1.2370 clears that comfortably while the runner-up of 0.0126 does not. The first option overreaches in the way this module keeps warning about: the theorem compares two datasets, never a dataset with a space it was sampled from. The second is tempting and wrong — one diagram plus a noise model is exactly two diagrams' worth of information. The fourth inverts the logic: short bars might be noise, which is not the same as being absent.

Why this matters for the mission: The stability theorem (an epsilon statement, cf. pw-04) is why TDA is trustworthy on noisy real data.

The strip is true, and "trustworthy" is doing something it should not.

The theorem is real and it is strong. Oudot's Theorem 3.1 is an equality, not merely a bound: the bottleneck distance between two diagrams equals the interleaving distance between the modules. He calls it "the cornerstone of the theory".

Read where the equality lives. It is between a persistence module and its diagram. The step from a point cloud to its Rips module is no part of it, and that step is not invertible — two very different clouds can carry the same diagram. So the chain from data to diagram runs one way only, with a factor of 2, and no bound on a diagram converts back into a bound on the data. Reading the equality as though it applied end to end is the easiest available mistake here, because the theorem genuinely is an equality.

pw-04 is an unwritten S1 unit and is cited nowhere in this lesson. Every ε-statement here comes from Oudot Chapter 3, by theorem number and printed page.

But the theorem is unconditional and the number you apply it with is not. Every conclusion in Segment 3 has the shape "if the data is accurate to δ, then…". δ is a claim about measurement — instrument precision, a sampling model, a corruption bound — and it comes from outside the data. At δ = 0.2 lab-01's long bar clears the threshold; at δ = 0.4 it does not. No syllabus action; the strip is accurate and locates the trust in the wrong place — the theorem is where the trust is safe, and δ is where the risk lives.

Not claimed here. That the points were sampled from a circle, or that the long bar is that circle's hole. Every theorem in this unit relates two datasets and their diagrams, and none of them mentions a generating space.
Segment 1 · 25 min

A distance that is allowed to delete things

Two diagrams need not have the same number of points, so a distance between them cannot be a bijection cost. The bottleneck distance uses a partial matching: points may be paired with points, or discarded onto the diagonal, and the cost of a matching is the largest cost it incurs.

Why discarding is legitimate. The diagram is defined to contain the diagonal with infinite multiplicity (Edelsbrunner printed 181, carried from lab-03), so a point matched to the diagonal is matched to a point genuinely there. In the ℓ∞ norm the cost of that match is half the lifetime: the nearest diagonal point to (b, d) is the midpoint, at sup-norm distance (d − b)/2.

One matching, two kinds of move. P has the two blue points (0, 3) and (1, 1.4); Q has the single orange point (0.2, 3.1). The matching pairs (0, 3) with (0.2, 3.1) at sup-norm cost 0.2, and sends (1, 1.4) to the diagonal at cost (1.4 − 1)/2 = 0.2. The bottleneck cost is the maximum of the two, not the sum, so this matching costs 0.2 — and persim.bottleneck returns exactly 0.2000. The dashed squares are the ℓ∞ balls of radius 0.2: matching is possible precisely because each blue point's square reaches its target.

Self-check 1. The cost is a maximum rather than a sum. What does that make the distance blind to?

Multiplicity. A hundred points at lifetime 0.02 are all within 0.01 of the diagonal, so discarding all hundred costs 0.01 — exactly what discarding one costs. The distance sees the single worst discrepancy and nothing about how many there are, which is precisely why the Wasserstein distances exist: they sum the costs (to a power) instead. The fourth option overstates a true thing — near-diagonal points are cheap, not free, and a point at lifetime 0.02 costs 0.01 rather than nothing.
Faded — the qualification an2-01 recorded, found in the source.

an2-01 established, through tda1-06, that the bottleneck distance on diagrams in general is an extended pseudometric: distinct objects at distance zero, values in [0, ∞]. Oudot says the same at printed 51 and then says when it stops being true.

His witness is sharp: ℚ² \ Δ and (ℚ + √2)² \ Δ are distinct multisets at bottleneck distance zero, because "the infimum in (3.2) is zero but not attained".

And the repair: a compactness argument shows the infimum is attained for locally finite multisets in the extended plane off the diagonal, so db is a true distance there — and by Theorem 1.13 those include every undecorated diagram of a q-tame module.

Notice that the witness has to be off the diagonal, and why. Adjoining a point that lies exactly on the diagonal changes a persim array and changes no diagram at all, since the diagonal is already there with infinite multiplicity — so db = 0 in that case is identity of indiscernibles working. The problem set runs that experiment precisely because it is the natural first attempt and it fails: what it establishes is that the array is a representation, and the map from arrays to diagrams is not injective.

Segment 2 · 30 min

The Isometry Theorem, and both of its halves

Theorem 3.1 (Isometry), printed 49. Let V, W be q-tame persistence modules over ℝ. Then db(dgm(V), dgm(W)) = di(V, W). Stated; proved across the chapter, via the ε-interleaving of Definition 3.3 (printed 52), the Box Lemma and an interpolation argument, with Lemma 3.4 (printed 56) supplying the crude 2ε bound that is then sharpened. Oudot: the stability half "is reputed to be difficult to prove", the converse half "easy once the stability part is given".

It is an equality, and Oudot splits it deliberately:

halfstatementwhat it guarantees
stabilitydb ≤ didiagrams are stable — similar modules have similar diagrams
converse stabilitydi ≤ dbdiagrams are informative — dissimilar modules have dissimilar diagrams

The second half is the one people forget, and it is what stops the diagram from being a summary that throws everything away. A constant map is perfectly stable and useless.

Worked — the bound, tested, and the slack read correctly.

Perturbing every point of lab-01's circle by at most δ changes each Vietoris–Rips filtration value by at most 2δ, so the guarantee is db ≤ 2δ. Measured: 
delta    max shift      d_b(H1)        d_b / 2*delta
0.01     0.0100         0.005729       0.286
0.05     0.0500         0.020832       0.208
0.20     0.2000         0.073852       0.185
Ratios of 0.286, 0.208 and 0.185 against a permitted 1.

δ has to bound the Euclidean displacement of each point, because that is the quantity a Rips filtration value is built from. Scaling a random direction so its largest coordinate is 1 — the obvious thing to write — leaves rows of norm up to √2, and in this sample the largest row had norm 1.169474. The table would then have been dividing by a δ the perturbation does not satisfy, and reporting ratios about 17% too high. Nothing would have looked wrong: the numbers stay well under 1 either way. The check that catches it is naming the norm the theorem uses before measuring anything in it.

The slack means the worst case did not occur, and nothing else. The theorem is a guarantee, not a prediction: it forbids a ratio above 1 and is silent about where below 1 any particular perturbation lands. This one is not lucky — a single random direction is typical, and worst cases are engineered rather than stumbled into. What a ratio above 1 would have meant is that something is wrong: a bug in the perturbation, the diagram, or the matching, because the mathematics forbids it.

lab-01's H₁ has a longest bar of 1.2370 and a runner-up of 0.0126. Before reading on: assuming the data is accurate to δ, derive the shortest lifetime a bar can have and still be guaranteed not to be an artefact of that noise. Then evaluate it at δ = 0.05 and say what happens to each of the two bars. Finally, state the largest δ at which the long bar's survival is still guaranteed.
The threshold, and where it breaks Two datasets within δ have diagrams within 2δ. For a feature to be destroyed it must be matched to the diagonal, which costs half its lifetime. So a bar of lifetime L can be destroyed only if L/2 ≤ 2δ, that is L ≤ 4δ. A bar longer than 4δ is guaranteed to survive.

At δ = 0.05 the threshold is 0.20. The long bar at 1.2370 clears it by a factor of six; the runner-up at 0.0126 does not come close. The same verdict holds at δ = 0.01 and δ = 0.20, which is what the problem set's table reports.

The long bar's guarantee breaks at δ = 1.2370 / 4 ≈ 0.309. Above that, stability permits a dataset within δ whose diagram lacks the feature entirely, and the conclusion fails. Notice what that number is: it is not a property of the circle, it is the point at which your claim about measurement stops supporting your claim about shape.

Segment 3 · 20 min

The one place a modelling assumption enters

Oudot's Corollary 3.6 (printed 61) is the version that mentions functions rather than modules: for q-tame f, g on X, db(dgm f, dgm g) ≤ ‖f − g‖∞. He notes it is "a direct consequence of the Isometry Theorem", and adds a piece of history worth keeping: the original statement by Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Harer carried extra conditions — X finitely triangulable, f and g continuous, and a stricter notion of tameness — "because the authors did not have the concept of interleaving between persistence modules" available.

Self-check 3. What does that history imply about citing "the stability theorem"?

That a name is not a citation. "Cohen-Steiner–Edelsbrunner– Harer stability" can mean the 2007 statement, with X finitely triangulable and f, g continuous, or Oudot's Corollary 3.6 under q-tameness — and they are not equivalent, since the modern hypotheses are strictly weaker. A report relying on the weaker ones must cite the source that proves them. This is the same discipline lab-01 derived for library defaults and an2 for page numbers, arriving here as a date: the hypotheses attached to a named theorem are a property of the statement you read, not of the name.

Self-check 4. Where in the chain from point cloud to conclusion does a modelling assumption enter?

Once, at δ. Walk it: a point cloud is a finite metric space (no hypothesis); Rips or Čech at a scale, with Čech carrying the Nerve Theorem and Rips the √2 sandwich (both proved); the reduction, needing a monotonic filtration the construction guarantees; the barcode, needing pointwise finite-dimensionality which is free for a finite complex; stability, needing q-tameness which finite diagrams satisfy. Every hypothesis is discharged by the construction — except δ, which is a claim about measurement and cannot be computed from the data it describes. The first and fourth options name real choices that affect what you compute, but both are choices between theorems, not assumptions about the world.

Self-check 5. lab-01 asked what you could conclude if the ratio were 1.4 rather than 98.1. Answer it with this unit's machinery.

A separating δ always exists. The test is a single threshold 4δ applied to every bar, so the longest survives and the runner-up fails exactly when δ ∈ [L₂/4, L₁/4) — a non-empty interval whenever L₁ > L₂. The second option is the intuitive answer and it is algebraically false; it was the answer this unit gave in draft, and a reviewer caught it.

What the ratio controls is how wide the window is in ratio — the interval is [L₂/4, L₁/4), so its endpoints differ by exactly the factor L₁/L₂ — and so how much error in δ the verdict tolerates. At 98.1 the window is [0.0032, 0.3093) and δ may be wrong by a factor near 98; at 1.4 the endpoints differ by a factor of 1.4 and δ must be right to within about 40%. It is the ratio of the endpoints that carries this, not their difference: the additive width at 1.4 is 0.1 L₂, which shrinks with the bars and so measures nothing about how tolerant the verdict is. Since δ arrives from outside the analysis with no accuracy attached, at 1.4 the honest report is that the conclusion is not robust to the one number nothing here can check — a statement about the evidence, not about the data. The fourth option is a real temptation and wrong: Wasserstein aggregates differently, it does not manufacture a separation the lifetimes do not contain.
