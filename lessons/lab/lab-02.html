Simplicial complexes: Vietoris–Rips and Čech (lab-02)

lab-01 left the complex as a black box, and the black box has a seam in it. There are two standard constructions, they are not the same, and the asymmetry between them is the opposite of what convenience would suggest: the one with a theorem attaching it to the data is the one nobody computes, and the one everybody computes has no such theorem. An equilateral triangle of side 1 is enough to separate them. Three balls of radius 0.5 around its corners meet in pairs and share no common point, so the union has a hole in the middle — and Vietoris–Rips fills it in.

Predict before we start. Three points form an equilateral triangle of side 1. Put a closed ball of radius 0.5 at each corner. Which statement about the union of the three balls is right?

A hole. Each pair of corners is at distance exactly 1 = 2 × 0.5, so the balls touch — they meet, in a single point per pair, which is why the fourth option is nearly right and wrong about the count. For a common point you would need one point within 0.5 of all three corners, and the closest candidate is the circumcentre at distance 0.57735. That exceeds 0.5, so there is no common point and the middle of the triangle is uncovered. The third option is the one worth dwelling on: pairwise-contractible overlaps do not give a contractible union, and the whole content of the Nerve Theorem is which combinatorial data does control the homotopy type.

Why this matters for the mission: Vietoris-Rips and Cech complexes are how raw points become something with computable homology.

The strip is true, and it names the two in the wrong order.

Both constructions do turn points into something with computable homology. That is not in dispute, and lab-01's pipeline already ran one of them.

But only one of the two is connected to the data by a theorem. The Čech complex at radius r is the nerve of the balls of radius r, and the Nerve Theorem — Edelsbrunner and Harer, printed 71 — says the nerve of a finite collection of closed convex sets has the homotopy type of their union. So Čech(r) is homotopy equivalent to the r-thickening of the data. Vietoris–Rips is the nerve of nothing, and the Nerve Theorem says nothing about it at all.

The strip names Vietoris–Rips first, and a reader who stops at the strip will take them for interchangeable routes to one answer. They are not: Segment 2 exhibits a case where the union of balls is homotopy equivalent to a circle and the Vietoris–Rips complex is contractible. No syllabus action — the strip is accurate and its ordering misleads, which is the mildest form this finding takes and still worth recording.

Not claimed here. That either complex recovers the shape the points were sampled from. Both are statements about the thickened data at one radius; getting from there to the sampled object needs lab-05.

One triangle, three filtration values, none of them wrong

Take the equilateral triangle of side 1 and ask both libraries when the 2-simplex appears.

simplex    Rips filtration        Cech filtration
(0, 1)     1.00000                0.50000
(0, 2)     1.00000                0.50000
(1, 2)     1.00000                0.50000
(0, 1, 2)  1.00000                0.57735

Two separate things are visible. On the edges the two columns differ by a clean factor of two, every time. On the triangle they differ by 1.00000 against 0.57735, which is not a factor of two, and that is the mathematically interesting half.

What each library is measuring. gudhi's RipsComplex filters by the diameter — the longest edge. gudhi's Čech filters by the radius of the smallest enclosing ball. On two points those are the same number up to a factor of two, because the smallest ball around two points has the segment joining them as a diameter. On three points they part company. Edelsbrunner writes Rips(r) = {σ : diam σ ≤ 2r}, so his r is gudhi's max_edge_length halved. Three conventions, one triangle.

And then the default. Reading the Čech values without setting the flag gives a third number:

Cech, default             0.333333
Cech, output_squared_values=False  0.577350
square root of the default        0.577350
1/sqrt(3), the circumradius       0.577350

So the same 2-simplex carries 1.00000, 0.57735 and 0.33333, and every one is correct. 0.57735 is 1/√3, the circumradius of a unit equilateral triangle; 0.33333 is its square, because DelaunayCechComplex.create_simplex_tree() returns squared values unless told otherwise. This is lab-01's rule arriving with teeth: an API's default is part of the citation, and here the default squares every number on the page.

Self-check 1. A report says "the triangle enters the filtration at 0.33". What is missing?

All three. Rips or Čech changes 1.00000 to 0.57735; output_squared_values changes 0.57735 to 0.33333; and the parameter convention — Edelsbrunner's radius r against gudhi's max_edge_length = 2r — moves it again. A reader given "0.33" cannot recover which object is being described. The fourth option is a genuine misconception worth killing: filtration values are distances in the data's own units and do not scale with the number of points at all.

Segment 2 · 30 min

The Nerve Theorem, and the complex that does not get it

Nerve Theorem (printed 71). Let ℱ be a finite collection of closed, convex sets in Euclidean space. Then the nerve of ℱ and the union of the sets in ℱ have the same homotopy type. Stated without proof — Edelsbrunner and Harer say so in the sentence introducing it, "a fundamental result which we state formally but without proof". They then note the hypothesis can be relaxed: if the union is triangulable, the sets are closed, and every non-empty common intersection is contractible, the conclusion survives.

The Čech complex of S at radius r is the nerve of the closed balls of radius r about the points of S (printed 72). Closed balls are closed and convex, which is the hypothesis word for word. So:

What Čech buys. Čech(r) is homotopy equivalent to the union of the balls of radius r about the data — the r-thickening of the point cloud. Its homology is the homology of a geometric object you can point at.

Now Vietoris–Rips. Edelsbrunner motivates it as a shortcut at printed 74: "Instead of checking all subcollections, we may just check pairs and add 2- and higher-dimensional simplices whenever we can." That gives Rips(r) = {σ ⊆ S : diam σ ≤ 2r}. It is not the nerve of any collection of convex sets, and the Nerve Theorem gives exactly nothing about it.

The whole unit in one picture. Left, radius 0.5: each pair of discs touches, and the circumcentre — marked — sits at distance 0.57735 from every corner, so it is outside all three discs and the middle is uncovered. The union is homotopy equivalent to a circle. Čech(0.5) is the hollow triangle, three edges and no face, and gets that right. Rips(0.5) fills the face in, because all three edges are present and Rips asks nothing further, and so reports a contractible complex for a union with a hole. Right, radius 0.57735: the discs now reach the circumcentre, the three-way intersection is that single point, Čech acquires the 2-simplex, and the two complexes agree. The gap between the panels is the entire difference between the constructions.

Self-check 2. At r = 0.5 in the figure, Rips(0.5) is contractible and the union of balls is homotopy equivalent to a circle. What has gone wrong?

Nothing is wrong, and nothing was promised. Rips(r) is defined to contain every simplex its edges warrant — that is the whole shortcut — so containing this face is correct behaviour, not a bug. The second option is the tempting error: the Vietoris–Rips Lemma gives containments between complexes at radii differing by √2, and containment is not homotopy equivalence. There is no radius at which Rips(0.5) is claimed to be homotopy equivalent to any union of balls. The fourth is false too — the equilateral triangle is as general as any other, and small perturbations of it behave the same way.
Vietoris–Rips Lemma (printed 74). For S a finite set of points in some Euclidean space and r ≥ 0, Vietoris-Rips(r) ⊆ Čech(√2 · r). Proved, printed 74–75, by computing the distance from the origin to the barycentre of a standard d-simplex. Note the contrast with the Nerve Theorem on the facing pages, which is stated and not proved — the modality differs between two results this lesson uses side by side.

With the immediate containment Čech(r) ⊆ Rips(r), that sandwiches one complex between two copies of the other. Checked on twelve random points:

r      |Cech(r)|  |Rips(r)|  Cech(r) not in Rips(r) Rips(r) not in Cech(sqrt2 r)
0.15   9          9          0                      0
0.25   45         45         0                      0
0.35   135        139        0                      0

The counts agree at 0.15 and 0.25 and differ by four at 0.35. That is not luck: on 1-simplices the two definitions coincide, since the smallest ball around two points has radius exactly half their distance. The complexes can only diverge once 2-simplices appear, and the four extra simplices at r = 0.35 are triples of diameter at most 0.7 whose smallest enclosing ball exceeds 0.35 — Segment 1's triangle, four times over.

Worked — what a green column is worth.

Both violation columns read zero at all three radii. It is tempting to call that confirmation of the Vietoris–Rips Lemma. It is not, and the reason is lab-01 Problem 1(c) again.

Čech(r) ⊆ Rips(r) is immediate from the definitions, and Rips(r) ⊆ Čech(√2 r) is proved at printed 74–75. A run cannot confirm a theorem and could not have refuted one. What a non-zero entry would have meant is that the enclosing-ball routine or the diameter computation is wrong. So the honest label for that table is "the code agrees with two things already proved" — which is exactly what you want from a test, and is not evidence about the mathematics.
Guided — find the smallest constant that could work.

The lemma's constant is √2 ≈ 1.41421. Before reading on, use the figure to bound it from below. The equilateral triangle of side 1 is in Rips(r) for r = 0.5, and in Čech(ρ) only once ρ reaches the circumradius. Write down the inequality any working constant c must satisfy for this one simplex, and solve it.
The bound, and why √2 and not that The triangle is in Rips(0.5) and enters Čech at ρ = 1/√3 = 0.57735. For Rips(r) ⊆ Čech(c·r) to hold at r = 0.5 we need c · 0.5 ≥ 1/√3, so c ≥ 2/√3 ≈ 1.15470.

So no constant below 1.1547 can work, and in particular c = 1 is false — the triangle at r = 0.5 is the counterexample, which is the figure's left panel read as a refutation.

But 1.1547 is the bound from one triangle in the plane. Edelsbrunner's proof works in every dimension, and it is the regular d-simplex as d grows that forces the constant up: the distance from the barycentre of a standard d-simplex to its vertices approaches √2 times half the edge length. So √2 is the supremum over all dimensions, and in the plane alone the lemma is not tight. If you derived 1.1547 and wondered why the book says √2, that gap is the answer, and it is worth noticing that a lemma can be sharp in general and slack in the case you are actually computing.

Why anyone computes the one without the theorem

Faded — Rips is its own 1-skeleton. gudhi 3.13.0, RipsComplex with points= against distance_matrix=.

Building Rips from the twelve points' coordinates and from their distance matrix gives literally the same list of simplices: True, 151 simplices either way.

That is not a coincidence about this data. A simplex is in Rips(r) exactly when all of its edges are, so the 1-skeleton determines the whole complex — and the 1-skeleton is nothing but the pairwise distances.

Self-check 3. A report computes Rips and wants to say something about the shape of the data. Which sentence is defensible?

The third. The first is the Nerve Theorem misapplied to the complex that does not have it — Segment 2's figure refutes it outright. The second is false as stated: the two filtrations are genuinely different, as the 139-against-135 count shows, and their diagrams differ. The fourth inverts the logic — Rips containing more simplices means it can destroy features by filling them in, which is exactly what happens at r = 0.5 in the figure, so "any feature it finds is genuinely present" is precisely backwards. The third sentence concedes what must be conceded: features narrower than the √2 band are not distinguished by this argument at all.

Self-check 4. gudhi 3.13.0 ships RipsComplex, AlphaComplex and DelaunayCechComplex, and no CechComplex. Why?

Tractability. Plain Čech tests one enclosing-ball problem per candidate simplex, so it invites users to launch something O(nk+1). DelaunayCechComplex computes the Čech filtration restricted to the Delaunay triangulation, which is far smaller and, for points in general position, carries the same persistent homology. The third option has the containment backwards, and it is worth being clear that neither is a special case of the other. But the substitution has a price: the two complexes are genuinely different, so a claim about the Čech complex — as opposed to its persistent homology — cannot be read off the Delaunay version, and Segment 2's simplex-counting table could not have been produced with it. That is why the problem set implements the enclosing-ball test by hand.
