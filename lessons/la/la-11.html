Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, invariant subspaces (la-11)

Directions a transformation cannot bend, only stretch.

An operator maps a space to itself, and the way to understand one is to break the space into pieces the operator preserves — subspaces it maps into themselves. Restricted to such a subspace the operator is still an operator, so every tool that needs a domain and codomain to coincide is still available, on a smaller space.

The smallest interesting pieces are the one-dimensional ones, and a one-dimensional invariant subspace is exactly a direction that the operator only scales. The scale factor is an eigenvalue, the direction an eigenvector, and the whole of the rest of the module is about finding enough of them. They also come with a warning that this unit delivers early: whether they exist at all depends on the field, and the same operator can have none over R and a full complement over C.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Define T on F² by T(w, z) = (−z, w). Over R this is a 90° counterclockwise rotation of the plane. Does T have an eigenvalue when F = R? Does it when F = C? Commit to an answer for each — and note that nothing about the formula changes between the two cases. Only the scalars you are allowed to use do. The verdict arrives in Segment 2.

Why this matters for the mission: Spectral methods appear in Mapper and graph-based TDA.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

Invariant subspaces

Definition 5.2 (invariant subspace). Suppose T ∈ ℒ(V). A subspace U of V is invariant under T if u ∈ U implies Tu ∈ U — equivalently, if T|U is an operator on U. — Axler §5.A, Definition 5.2, p. 132

The motivation is worth stating before the examples. If V = U₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Um then understanding T ought to reduce to understanding it on each piece — but only if each restriction is still an operator, so that powers T², T³ and the machinery of la-08 remain available. That is precisely what invariance buys, and it is why the definition is about mapping U into itself rather than merely somewhere. — Axler §5.A, p. 132

Worked example — four subspaces that are always invariant (fully worked). For any T ∈ ℒ(V):

{0}: if u ∈ {0} then u = 0 and Tu = 0 ∈ {0}. V: if u ∈ V then Tu ∈ V, since T maps into V. null T: if Tu = 0 then Tu = 0 ∈ null T — read that twice, the point being that 0 lies in every null space. range T: if u ∈ range T then Tu is an output of T, hence in range T, and the hypothesis on u is not even used. — Axler §5.A, Example 5.3, pp. 132–133

The two trivial ones, {0} and V, are invariant for every operator and tell you nothing. Whether other invariant subspaces must exist is a real question, and null T and range T do not settle it: when T is invertible they are {0} and V again. — Axler §5.A, p. 133

Contrast — an invariant subspace found by degree. Let T ∈ ℒ(𝒫(R)) be Tp = p′. Then 𝒫₄(R) is invariant under T, because differentiating a polynomial of degree at most 4 gives one of degree at most 4 — in fact of degree at most 3, which is more than invariance requires. — Axler §5.A, Example 5.4, p. 133

Note the shape of the check: identify a property that membership of the subspace is defined by, and show T preserves it. Every invariance proof in this unit's problem set has that form.

Why one dimension is the interesting case. Take v ≠ 0 and let U = span(v), a one-dimensional subspace — and every one-dimensional subspace arises this way. If U is invariant then Tv ∈ U, so Tv = λv for some scalar λ. Conversely if Tv = λv then span(v) is invariant. So T has a one-dimensional invariant subspace if and only if the equation Tv = λv has a solution with v ≠ 0, and that equation is what the next segment names. — Axler §5.A, p. 133

Self-check 1. Is range T invariant under T for every operator T?

If u ∈ range T then Tu is an output of T and so lies in range T — and the argument never used that u was in the range, only that it was in V. Invariance of the range is that cheap. The two conditions offered are irrelevant to it, though they do bear on whether the range is a useful invariant subspace: when T is invertible, range T = V and the information is vacuous.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Definition 5.5 and Definition 5.7 (eigenvalue, eigenvector). For T ∈ ℒ(V), a scalar λ ∈ F is an eigenvalue of T if there is v ∈ V with v ≠ 0 and Tv = λv; such a v is an eigenvector of T corresponding to λ. — Axler §5.A, Definition 5.5 and Definition 5.7, p. 134

The clause v ≠ 0 is not decoration. T0 = λ0 holds for every scalar λ, so without it every scalar would be an eigenvalue of every operator and the definition would say nothing. Since Tv = λv is the same equation as (T − λI)v = 0, a nonzero v is an eigenvector for λ exactly when v ∈ null(T − λI). — Axler §5.A, pp. 134–135

Theorem 5.6 (equivalent conditions). Suppose V is finite-dimensional, T ∈ ℒ(V) and λ ∈ F. Then these are equivalent: λ is an eigenvalue of T; T − λI is not injective; T − λI is not surjective; T − λI is not invertible. — Axler §5.A, Theorem 5.6, p. 134
Why it is true — and where the hypothesis is spent. The first two are equivalent by rewriting: Tv = λv with v ≠ 0 says exactly that null(T − λI) contains a nonzero vector, which by la-06's Theorem 3.16 is failure of injectivity. No finiteness is needed for that.

The remaining equivalences are la-08's Theorem 3.69 verbatim — for an operator on a finite-dimensional space, injective, surjective and invertible coincide. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.69, p. 87 That is the only place dim V < ∞ is used, and it is why the theorem states the hypothesis. The practical value is the third condition: "λ is an eigenvalue" becomes "T − λI is singular", which is what every computational method actually tests. — Axler §5.A, p. 134

Resolving the prediction gate

Verdict: no eigenvalues over R, two of them over C. With T(w, z) = (−z, w):

Over R. T rotates the plane by 90° counterclockwise. A nonzero vector and its 90° rotation are never parallel, so Tv is never a scalar multiple of v, and T has no eigenvalues and no eigenvectors at all.

Over C. Solve honestly. T(w, z) = λ(w, z) means −z = λw and w = λz. Substituting the second into the first gives −z = λ²z. Here z ≠ 0 — if z were 0 the second equation would force w = 0 and we are looking for a nonzero vector — so λ² = −1, giving λ = i and λ = −i. The eigenvectors for i are the (w, −wi) with w ≠ 0, and for −i the (w, wi) with w ≠ 0. — Axler §5.A, Example 5.8, p. 135

Nothing about the operator changed. The rotation still rotates; over C there are simply more scalars available, and multiplication by i is a rotation by 90° in the complex plane, so the "impossible" scaling becomes possible. This is the third time the field has decided an answer — la-04's dimRC = 2 against dimCC = 1, la-05's conjugation, and now this — and it is the reason Axler's later chapters split into a complex case and a real case.

Theorem 5.10 and Theorem 5.13 (distinct eigenvalues, independent eigenvectors). Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. Consequently, if V is finite-dimensional then each operator on V has at most dim V distinct eigenvalues. — Axler §5.A, Theorem 5.10 and Theorem 5.13, p. 136
Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof). Suppose v₁, …, vm were dependent. By la-03's Linear Dependence Lemma there is a smallest k with vk ∈ span(v₁, …, vk−1), say vk = a₁v₁ + ⋯ + ak−1vk−1.

Apply T: λkvk = a₁λ₁v₁ + ⋯ + ak−1λk−1vk−1. Multiply the previous line by λk instead and subtract: 0 = a₁(λk − λ₁)v₁ + ⋯ + ak−1(λk − λk−1)vk−1. Minimality of k makes v₁, …, vk−1 independent, so every coefficient vanishes; the λ differences are nonzero because the eigenvalues are distinct, so every aj = 0 — which makes vk = 0, contradicting that it is an eigenvector. — Axler §5.A, p. 136

Two devices worth keeping. Subtracting two expressions for the same vector to cancel the awkward term is the same move la-03 used to derive independence from uniqueness. And Theorem 5.13 is then immediate: independent lists cannot exceed dim V by la-03's Theorem 2.23, so there cannot be more than dim V distinct eigenvalues. — Axler §2.A, Theorem 2.23, p. 35

Self-check 2. An operator on a 4-dimensional space has eigenvalues 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. What can you conclude?

Theorem 5.13 caps the number of distinct eigenvalues at dim V = 4, so the situation described cannot occur. The other options answer questions about a situation that does not exist — always check the hypotheses are satisfiable before reasoning from them.

Self-check 3. Why does Definition 5.5 insist that v ≠ 0?

The zero vector satisfies the eigenvalue equation for every scalar at once, so admitting it would make the definition vacuous. Note that 0 may perfectly well be an eigenvalue — it is one exactly when T is not injective — which is why the first option is wrong. The third is false twice over: 0 belongs to every subspace, invariant or not.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Restriction and quotient operators

Definition 5.14 (T|U and T/U). Suppose T ∈ ℒ(V) and U is invariant under T. The restriction operator T|U ∈ ℒ(U) is T|U(u) = Tu, and the quotient operator T/U ∈ ℒ(V/U) is (T/U)(v + U) = Tv + U. — Axler §5.A, Definition 5.14, p. 137
Where invariance is spent — twice, differently. For the restriction, invariance is what makes T|U land in U rather than merely in V, so that it is an operator on U and not just a linear map out of it. Nothing else is needed.

For the quotient, invariance is what makes the formula well defined. Suppose v + U = w + U; then v − w ∈ U by la-09's Theorem 3.85, so T(v − w) ∈ U because U is invariant, hence Tv − Tw ∈ U and Tv + U = Tw + U. — Axler §3.E, Theorem 3.85, p. 95 Without invariance the recipe would give different answers for different representatives — the same representative-independence obligation la-09 met, now with an extra hypothesis to discharge it. — Axler §5.A, p. 137

Worked example — when the two pieces tell you nothing (fully worked). Define T ∈ ℒ(F²) by T(x, y) = (y, 0), and let U = {(x, 0) : x ∈ F}.

(a) T(x, 0) = (0, 0) ∈ U, so U is invariant and T|U is the zero operator on U.

(b) There is no subspace W invariant under T with F² = U ⊕ W. Such a W would have dim W = 1, so every nonzero vector in it would be an eigenvector of T. But the only eigenvalue of T is 0 and every eigenvector lies in U, so no one-dimensional invariant W can avoid U.

(c) (T/U)((x, y) + U) = (y, 0) + U = 0 + U, since (y, 0) ∈ U. So T/U is the zero operator too. — Axler §5.A, Example 5.15, p. 138

Both pieces are zero and T is not. The moral is a caution against the programme the segment opened with: decomposing along an invariant subspace does not always decompose the operator, because the invariant subspace need not have an invariant complement. Repairing that is what Axler's later chapters spend their effort on.

Self-check 4. U is invariant under T. What makes (T/U)(v + U) = Tv + U a legitimate definition?

A coset has many representatives, so the formula must be shown to give the same coset for all of them — and the step T(v − w) ∈ U needs exactly that U is invariant. Linearity alone gives Tv − Tw = T(v − w) but says nothing about where that lands. This is la-09's well-definedness obligation with one more hypothesis in play.

Self-check 5. In Example 5.15 both T|U and T/U are the zero operator, yet T ≠ 0. What does that show?

Both operators are correctly defined and correctly computed; the pair simply loses information, because T also moves vectors from outside U into U, and neither piece records that. Part (b) of the example is the underlying obstruction: U has no invariant complement, so the decomposition the segment hoped for is unavailable.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose S, T ∈ ℒ(V) satisfy ST = TS. Prove that null S is invariant under T. (Take u ∈ null S and ask what must be shown; then find the one place the hypothesis can be applied.) — cf. Axler §5.A, Exercise 2, p. 138

Reveal model answer

Proof. Let u ∈ null S, so Su = 0. To show null S is invariant under T we must show Tu ∈ null S, that is, S(Tu) = 0. Now

S(Tu) = (ST)u = (TS)u = T(Su) = T0 = 0,

using the hypothesis at the second equality and la-05's Theorem 3.11 at the last. So Tu ∈ null S, and null S is invariant under T. ∎

Two things to notice. The proof is symmetric in S and T only in appearance: it shows null S is invariant under T, and swapping the names gives that null T is invariant under S — two different statements from one hypothesis. And the same three-step computation, with range in place of null, is a problem in this unit's set; the difference is that a range membership must be produced rather than assumed, exactly as in la-06.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. A subspace is invariant under T when T maps it into itself (Definition 5.2), which is what makes the restriction an operator and keeps la-08's machinery available. {0}, V, null T and range T always qualify (Example 5.3), and the first two say nothing while the other two can collapse to them.
  2. A one-dimensional invariant subspace is exactly a solution of Tv = λv with v ≠ 0 — an eigenvalue and eigenvector (Definitions 5.5, 5.7). Equivalently T − λI fails to be injective, surjective, or invertible, these being the same failure in finite dimensions (Theorem 5.6). Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent (Theorem 5.10), capping the number of distinct eigenvalues at dim V (Theorem 5.13). Existence depends on the field: the 90° rotation has none over R and two over C.
  3. An invariant U yields two smaller operators, T|U and T/U (Definition 5.14) — invariance making the first land in U and the second well defined on cosets. But the pair need not determine T: Example 5.15 has both zero with T ≠ 0, because U has no invariant complement.
