Upper-triangular matrices and diagonalisability (la-12)

Every complex operator can be put in triangular form. Which ones go further, to diagonal?

Operators can be raised to powers, and general linear maps cannot — that single asymmetry is why operators have a theory. Once T², T³ and so on exist, any polynomial p can be evaluated at T, producing another operator p(T). Polynomials in one operator commute with each other, so a whole commutative algebra sits inside ℒ(V), and factorising a polynomial factorises the operator built from it.

That is the engine behind the two results this unit is aimed at. Over C every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional space has an eigenvalue — the proof simply builds a polynomial that kills some vector and factors it. And an operator is diagonalisable exactly when its eigenspaces fill the whole space, which is the cleanest possible structure an operator can have: the space breaks into pieces on which T is nothing but multiplication by a number.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let T be an operator on an n-dimensional space. Answer both halves separately. (i) If T has n distinct eigenvalues, do you predict it must be diagonalisable? (ii) If T has fewer than n distinct eigenvalues, do you predict it must fail to be diagonalisable? Commit to yes or no for each before Segment 3 — and notice that the two halves are not the same question asked twice.

Why this matters for the mission: Structure theorems here foreshadow persistence module decomposition.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

Polynomials applied to operators

Definition 5.16 and Definition 5.17 (Tm and p(T)). For T ∈ ℒ(V) and m a positive integer, Tm is T composed with itself m times, T⁰ = I, and T−m = (T−1)m when T is invertible. If p(z) = a₀ + a₁z + ⋯ + amzm, then p(T) = a₀I + a₁T + ⋯ + amTm. — Axler §5.B, Definition 5.16 and Definition 5.17, p. 143

Two details are doing real work. T⁰ = I rather than anything else is what makes the constant term of p come out as a₀I — a scalar cannot be added to an operator, so the identity has to appear. And the powers exist at all only because the domain and codomain coincide, which is exactly what la-08's definition of operator secured. — Axler §5.B, p. 143

Worked example — a polynomial in the differentiation operator (fully worked). Let D ∈ ℒ(𝒫(R)) be Dq = q′ and let p(x) = 7 − 3x + 5x². Then p(D) = 7I − 3D + 5D², so

(p(D))q = 7q − 3q′ + 5q″

for every q. So a polynomial in the differentiation operator is a constant-coefficient linear differential operator — the objects an entire branch of analysis is about. Reading the correspondence in reverse, solving 5q″ − 3q′ + 7q = 0 is asking for null p(D). — Axler §5.B, Example 5.18, p. 144

Definition 5.19 and Theorem 5.20 (products, and commutativity). With (pq)(z) = p(z)q(z), we have (pq)(T) = p(T)q(T) and, consequently, p(T)q(T) = q(T)p(T). — Axler §5.B, Definition 5.19 and Theorem 5.20, p. 144

The proof of the first part is a double sum rearranged, and the point is that expanding a product of polynomials never needs the symbol to be a number — the distributive law is all it uses, and operators have that. The second part then follows because pq = qp for polynomials. This is worth pausing on: la-05 made a great deal of the fact that operators do not commute in general, and here is a large family that does. — Axler §5.B, p. 144

Where this unit stops, and what comes next. Theorem 5.21's proof below carries a germ worth naming. For v ≠ 0 in an n-dimensional space the list v, Tv, …, Tnv has n + 1 members and so must be dependent, which hands you a nonzero polynomial p with p(T)v = 0. Run the same argument on ℒ(V) rather than on a single vector and you get a polynomial that annihilates T itself; the monic one of least degree is the minimal polynomial, and every polynomial annihilating T turns out to be a multiple of it. That is Axler §8.C, beyond Semester 1. Keep the construction — it is the tool this unit uses twice — and take the theorem on account.

Self-check 1. Why must T⁰ be defined as I rather than left undefined?

a₀ + a₁T is not a legal expression: the two terms live in different sets. Writing a₀I puts the constant into ℒ(V) where it can be added. The third option treats a convention as a derivation — there is nothing to compute, since composing T with itself zero times has no independent meaning.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Eigenvalues exist over C, and upper-triangular matrices

Theorem 5.21 (existence of an eigenvalue over C). Every operator on a finite-dimensional, nonzero, complex vector space has an eigenvalue. — Axler §5.B, Theorem 5.21, p. 145
Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof) — and it is worth following closely. Let dim V = n > 0 and pick any v ≠ 0. The list v, Tv, T²v, …, Tnv has n + 1 members in an n-dimensional space, so it is dependent by la-03's squeeze. That gives complex a₀, …, an, not all 0, with a₀v + a₁Tv + ⋯ + anTnv = 0. Note that a₁, …, an cannot all vanish, since that would leave a₀v = 0 and force a₀ = 0 too.

Now make the a's the coefficients of a polynomial and factor it into linear factors over C. — Axler, Chapter 4, Theorem 4.14, p. 125 That factorisation is where the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra enters. — Axler, Chapter 4, Theorem 4.13, p. 124 The relation becomes 0 = c(T − λ₁I) ⋯ (T − λmI)v with c ≠ 0, so at least one factor T − λjI fails to be injective — and by la-11's Theorem 5.6 that λj is an eigenvalue. — Axler §5.B, p. 145

The step that converts an equation about polynomials into an equation about operators is Theorem 5.20(a): factoring the polynomial factors the operator. Nothing in the argument constructs an eigenvector explicitly, and nothing could — the proof is an existence argument through and through, which is why it works at all.

The hypothesis F = C is not removable. la-11's rotation of R² has no eigenvalue, and the reason shows up exactly here: over R a polynomial need not factor into linear factors.

Definitions 5.24 and 5.25 (diagonal, upper triangular). The diagonal of a square matrix consists of the entries from the upper-left to the bottom-right corner. A matrix is upper triangular if every entry below the diagonal is 0. — Axler §5.B, Definition 5.24 and Definition 5.25, p. 147
Theorem 5.26 (what upper-triangular means). For T ∈ ℒ(V) and a basis v₁, …, vn of V, these are equivalent: the matrix of T with respect to that basis is upper triangular; Tvj ∈ span(v₁, …, vj) for each j; span(v₁, …, vj) is invariant under T for each j. — Axler §5.B, Theorem 5.26, p. 148

This is the bridge between the matrix picture and la-11's invariant subspaces. "Upper triangular" looks like a statement about where zeros sit; the theorem says it is a statement about a chain of nested invariant subspaces, each one dimension larger than the last. Every proof about upper-triangular matrices in this unit works through the third condition rather than the first. — Axler §5.B, p. 148

Theorem 5.27 (over C, every operator is upper-triangularisable). If V is a finite-dimensional complex vector space and T ∈ ℒ(V), then T has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some basis of V. — Axler §5.B, Theorem 5.27, p. 149

Axler gives two proofs, both by induction on dim V, and the second is the one la-09 makes available: take an eigenvector v₁ (Theorem 5.21 supplies one), let U = span(v₁), apply the induction hypothesis to the quotient operator T/U on V/U, whose dimension is dim V − 1, and lift the resulting basis back. That is la-11's quotient operator and la-09's dimension formula doing exactly the work they were built for. — Axler §5.B, p. 150

Theorems 5.30 and 5.32 (reading an upper-triangular matrix). If T has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some basis, then T is invertible if and only if every diagonal entry is nonzero; and the eigenvalues of T are precisely the entries on that diagonal. — Axler §5.B, Theorem 5.30, p. 150, and Theorem 5.32, p. 152
Worked example — eigenvalues read off in one glance (fully worked). Define T ∈ ℒ(F³) by T(x, y, z) = (2x + y, 5y + 3z, 8z).

Column k of the matrix is T applied to the k-th standard basis vector (la-07): T(1,0,0) = (2,0,0), T(0,1,0) = (1,5,0), T(0,0,1) = (0,3,8). So the matrix has rows (2,1,0), (0,5,3), (0,0,8) — upper triangular. By Theorem 5.32 the eigenvalues are 2, 5 and 8, with no further computation. — Axler §5.B, Example 5.33, p. 152

Theorem 5.32 is the practical payoff of the whole section, and it is worth being clear about what it does not give: there is no general method for computing eigenvalues exactly from an arbitrary matrix. The theorem is only available once you have found a basis making the matrix triangular, which is itself the hard part. — Axler §5.B, p. 151

Self-check 2. Why does Theorem 5.27 fail over R?

If ℳ(T) is upper triangular with respect to v₁, …, vn, then Tv₁ ∈ span(v₁), so v₁ is an eigenvector. la-11's rotation of R² has no eigenvectors at all, so it has no upper-triangular matrix with respect to any basis. The induction starts perfectly well at dimension 1; what fails is the appeal to Theorem 5.21, which is where C is needed.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Eigenspaces and diagonalisability

Definition 5.34 and Definition 5.36 (diagonal matrix, eigenspace). A diagonal matrix is a square matrix that is 0 everywhere except possibly on the diagonal. For T ∈ ℒ(V) and λ ∈ F, the eigenspace is E(λ, T) = null(T − λI) — the eigenvectors for λ together with 0. — Axler §5.C, Definition 5.34 and Definition 5.36, p. 155

E(λ, T) is a subspace, being a null space (la-06), and λ is an eigenvalue of T exactly when E(λ, T) ≠ {0}. Restricted to E(λ, T) the operator T is nothing but multiplication by λ, which is why eigenspaces are the pieces one hopes to decompose into. — Axler §5.C, p. 155

Canvas: how much structure a basis can buy

The same operator, seen through three bases: an arbitrary one, one making the matrix upper triangular, and one making it diagonal. Zero entries are dark; the amber cells are the diagonal. Nothing about the operator changes — only which entries are forced to vanish.

Theorem 5.38 (eigenspaces are independent). If V is finite-dimensional and λ₁, …, λm are distinct eigenvalues of T, then E(λ₁, T) + ⋯ + E(λm, T) is a direct sum, and dim E(λ₁, T) + ⋯ + dim E(λm, T) ≤ dim V. — Axler §5.C, Theorem 5.38, p. 156
Why it is true. Suppose u₁ + ⋯ + um = 0 with each uj ∈ E(λj, T). Any nonzero uj among them would be an eigenvector, and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent by la-11's Theorem 5.10 — so no nontrivial relation among them is possible and every uj = 0. la-02's zero-decomposition test then makes the sum direct. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.44, pp. 21–23

The dimension inequality follows because a direct sum's dimension is the sum of the dimensions — la-09's Theorem 3.78 — and it is a subspace of V, so la-04's Theorem 2.38 bounds it. — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.38, p. 45 Everything here is assembled from earlier units; the only genuinely new ingredient is Theorem 5.10. — Axler §5.C, p. 156

Definition 5.39 and Theorem 5.41 (diagonalisable, five ways). T is diagonalisable if it has a diagonal matrix with respect to some basis. For finite-dimensional V with distinct eigenvalues λ₁, …, λm, the following are equivalent: T is diagonalisable; V has a basis of eigenvectors of T; V is a direct sum of one-dimensional T-invariant subspaces; V = E(λ₁, T) ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ E(λm, T); dim V = dim E(λ₁, T) + ⋯ + dim E(λm, T). — Axler §5.C, Definition 5.39, p. 156, and Theorem 5.41, p. 157
Which of the five to reach for. The first is the definition and is almost never the one to check. The second is the working criterion — find enough eigenvectors. The third is the structural statement, and it is the one that generalises: a persistence module decomposing into interval modules is the same shape of claim, which is what this unit's mission strip is pointing at. The fourth says the eigenspaces exhaust the space. The fifth turns all of it into a single arithmetic check, and is the one to use when the eigenspaces are already computed.

Resolving the prediction gate

(i) Yes. (ii) No — and the second half is where the mistake usually lives.

(i) If T has n = dim V distinct eigenvalues, pick one eigenvector for each. They are independent by la-11's Theorem 5.10, and an independent list of length dim V is a basis by la-04's Theorem 2.39. That basis consists of eigenvectors, so criterion (b) of Theorem 5.41 holds and T is diagonalisable. — Axler §5.C, Theorem 5.44, p. 159

(ii) The converse is false. Define T on F³ by T(z₁, z₂, z₃) = (4z₁, 4z₂, 5z₃). Its matrix in the standard basis is already diagonal, so T is diagonalisable — and it has only two distinct eigenvalues, 4 and 5, in a three-dimensional space. Having fewer than dim V eigenvalues is no obstruction at all; what matters is whether the eigenspaces are large enough to fill V, and here dim E(4, T) = 2 and dim E(5, T) = 1 sum to 3. — Axler §5.C, p. 160

Theorem 5.44 is a sufficient condition and nothing more. Reading it as a criterion — "diagonalisable if and only if enough distinct eigenvalues" — is the standard error, and criterion (e) of Theorem 5.41 is the actual test.

Contrast — an operator that genuinely fails, over C. Define T ∈ ℒ(C²) by T(w, z) = (z, 0). Then T² = 0, so any eigenvalue λ satisfies λ²v = T²v = 0 for an eigenvector v ≠ 0, giving λ = 0; and E(0, T) = null T = {(w, 0)} has dimension 1. The eigenspace dimensions sum to 1, not 2, so criterion (e) fails and T is not diagonalisable. — Axler §5.C, Example 5.43, p. 158

Being over C guarantees an eigenvalue (Theorem 5.21) and an upper-triangular matrix (Theorem 5.27), and guarantees nothing about diagonalisability. That gap is what Axler's Chapter 8 exists to close, and it is where the minimal polynomial finally does its work.

Self-check 3. T is an operator on a 5-dimensional space with distinct eigenvalues 1, 2 and 3, and dim E(1, T) = 2, dim E(2, T) = 2, dim E(3, T) = 1. Is T diagonalisable?

Criterion (e) of Theorem 5.41: 2 + 2 + 1 = 5 = dim V, so T is diagonalisable. The second option applies the converse of Theorem 5.44, which the prediction gate just disproved. The third is unnecessary — the eigenspace dimensions are exactly the data the criterion needs, and no matrix is required.

Self-check 4. T is an operator on C⁴. Which is guaranteed?

Theorem 5.21 gives an eigenvalue and Theorem 5.27 an upper-triangular matrix, both because the field is C. Neither diagonalisability nor a full complement of eigenvalues follows: T(w, z) = (z, 0) extended by zeros is a counterexample to both.

Self-check 5. In the proof of Theorem 5.38, where is it used that the eigenvalues are distinct?

Theorem 5.10's hypothesis is that the eigenvalues are distinct; without it eigenvectors for one eigenvalue can certainly be dependent, and the sum of eigenspaces would not be direct — indeed E(λ, T) + E(λ, T) = E(λ, T) is never direct for a nonzero eigenspace. The dimension inequality is a consequence of directness, not a place where the hypothesis is spent.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose T ∈ ℒ(V) and U is a subspace of V invariant under T. Prove that U is invariant under p(T) for every polynomial p ∈ 𝒫(F). (Two stages: first handle the powers Tk, then assemble. Say which subspace property each stage uses.) — cf. Axler §5.B, Exercise 6, p. 153

Reveal model answer

Proof. Stage 1: U is invariant under Tk for every integer k ≥ 0. Induct on k. For k = 0, T⁰ = I and Iu = u ∈ U. Suppose U is invariant under Tk−1 and let u ∈ U. Then Tk−1u ∈ U by hypothesis, and applying T to a vector of U keeps it in U by invariance, so Tku = T(Tk−1u) ∈ U.

Stage 2: assemble. Let p(z) = a₀ + a₁z + ⋯ + amzm and u ∈ U. By Definition 5.17, p(T)u = a₀u + a₁Tu + ⋯ + amTmu. Each Tku lies in U by Stage 1; each akTku lies in U because U is closed under scalar multiplication; and the sum lies in U because U is closed under addition. So p(T)u ∈ U, and U is invariant under p(T). ∎

Both closure conditions of la-02's subspace test are used, and in the second stage only — the first stage needs nothing but invariance. That division is the reason the proof splits into two stages rather than being one induction: powers need invariance, combinations need subspace-hood, and running them together hides which is doing what.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. Operators, unlike general linear maps, can be raised to powers, so polynomials can be applied to them (Definition 5.17). Polynomials in one operator multiply as polynomials do and therefore commute (Theorem 5.20) — factoring the polynomial factors the operator.
  2. Over C that gives an eigenvalue for every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional space (Theorem 5.21): the list v, Tv, …, Tnv is too long to be independent, so some nonzero polynomial kills v, and factoring it exhibits a non-injective T − λI. Iterating gives an upper-triangular matrix (Theorem 5.27), which is really a chain of nested invariant subspaces (Theorem 5.26) and off which the eigenvalues can simply be read (Theorem 5.32). None of this survives over R.
  3. Eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues always form a direct sum (Theorem 5.38). Diagonalisable means that sum is all of V — equivalently a basis of eigenvectors exists, equivalently the eigenspace dimensions add to dim V (Theorem 5.41). Enough distinct eigenvalues is sufficient (Theorem 5.44) and not necessary, and even over C some operators fail entirely (Example 5.43).
