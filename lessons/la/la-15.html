Spectral theorem (real case) (la-15)

The crown jewel: symmetric operators diagonalise perfectly. Why?

Two results earlier in this module were bad news. la-11 produced a real operator with no eigenvalues at all — the rotation of the plane. la-12 produced an operator over C, where eigenvalues are guaranteed, that still fails to be diagonalisable. Between them they say that neither existence nor decomposability can be taken for granted.

The Real Spectral Theorem says that one hypothesis repairs both at once. If an operator on a real inner product space is self-adjoint — equivalently, if its matrix in an orthonormal basis is symmetric — then it has an eigenvalue, it is diagonalisable, and the diagonalising basis can be taken orthonormal. Nothing weaker delivers this, and nothing more is needed. It is the last unit of Semester 1's linear algebra because everything before it is used in the proof, and because the symmetric matrices it governs are exactly the graph Laplacians the mission's Mapper pipelines compute with.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let T be an operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space, and suppose T is self-adjoint — that is, ⟨Tv, w⟩ = ⟨v, Tw⟩ for all v, w. Answer both halves. (i) Do you predict T must have an eigenvalue, given that la-11's rotation of R² has none? (ii) Do you predict T must be diagonalisable, given that la-12 found non-diagonalisable operators even over C? Commit to both before Segment 3.

Why this matters for the mission: Graph Laplacians in Mapper are symmetric — their spectra behave.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

The adjoint

Definition 7.2 (adjoint). For T ∈ ℒ(V, W), the adjoint T* : W → V is the function satisfying ⟨Tv, w⟩ = ⟨v, T*w⟩ for every v ∈ V and w ∈ W. — Axler §7.A, Definition 7.2, p. 204
Why the definition defines anything. It asserts the existence of a vector T*w without constructing one. Fix w; the map v ↦ ⟨Tv, w⟩ is a linear functional on V, so la-14's Riesz Representation Theorem supplies a unique vector representing it — and that vector is T*w. — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.42, p. 188 Uniqueness is what makes T* well defined as a function; the whole of la-14 is standing behind this one definition. — Axler §7.A, p. 204
Worked example — computing an adjoint from the definition (fully worked). Define T : R³ → R² by T(x₁, x₂, x₃) = (x₂ + 3x₃, 2x₁). Find T*.

T* goes from R² to R³ — check the direction first, since it is reversed. Fix (y₁, y₂) and compute ⟨T(x₁, x₂, x₃), (y₁, y₂)⟩ = (x₂ + 3x₃)y₁ + 2x₁y₂. Regroup by the x's: this is x₁(2y₂) + x₂(y₁) + x₃(3y₁), which is ⟨(x₁, x₂, x₃), (2y₂, y₁, 3y₁)⟩.

Since that holds for every x, Definition 7.2 gives T*(y₁, y₂) = (2y₂, y₁, 3y₁). The technique is always the same: expand, then regroup by the first argument until the second argument is visible. — Axler §7.A, Example 7.3, p. 204

Theorems 7.5 and 7.6 (the adjoint is linear, and its algebra). If T ∈ ℒ(V, W) then T* ∈ ℒ(W, V). Moreover (S + T)* = S* + T*, (λT)* = conj(λ)T*, (T*)* = T, I* = I, and (ST)* = T*S*. — Axler §7.A, Theorem 7.5, p. 205, and Theorem 7.6, p. 206

Every one of these proofs is the same manoeuvre: flip T* from one side of the inner product to become T on the other, compute, and flip back. Two features are worth marking. The conjugate in (λT)* = conj(λ)T* is la-13's second-slot conjugation surfacing again. And (ST)* = T*S* reverses order — the third time this module has met that reversal, after la-08's inverses and la-10's dual maps, and for the same reason. — Axler §7.A, p. 206

Definition 7.8 and Theorem 7.10 (the matrix of the adjoint). The conjugate transpose of a matrix is obtained by interchanging rows and columns and conjugating every entry. With respect to orthonormal bases, ℳ(T*) is the conjugate transpose of ℳ(T) — over R, simply the transpose. — Axler §7.A, Definition 7.8, p. 207, and Theorem 7.10, p. 208
Adjoint and dual are not the same map, and this is where they meet. la-10's dual map T′ sends W′ to V′ and needs no inner product; ℳ(T′) is the transpose. The adjoint T* sends W to V and needs one; with orthonormal bases its matrix is the conjugate transpose. The two agree once Riesz is used to identify each space with its dual — which is exactly what an inner product buys, and is why the transpose appears twice in this module wearing different clothes. The hypothesis orthonormal in Theorem 7.10 is not decoration: in a non-orthonormal basis the matrix of T* is not the conjugate transpose. — Axler §3.F, Theorem 3.114, p. 110

Self-check 1. T ∈ ℒ(R⁵, R³). What are the domain and codomain of T*, and what size is ℳ(T*)?

The adjoint reverses direction, so it maps R³ to R⁵. la-07's rule then makes its matrix 5-by-3 — rows indexed by the codomain, columns by the domain — which is also what transposing the 3-by-5 matrix of T produces. The third option gets the direction right and the matrix convention backwards.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Self-adjoint and normal operators

Definition 7.11 (self-adjoint). An operator T ∈ ℒ(V) is self-adjoint if T = T*, equivalently if ⟨Tv, w⟩ = ⟨v, Tw⟩ for all v, w ∈ V. — Axler §7.A, Definition 7.11, p. 209
The analogy to keep. The adjoint plays the role on ℒ(V) that complex conjugation plays on C. A complex number is real exactly when z = conj(z); an operator is self-adjoint exactly when T = T*. Every result in this unit is a version of something true about real numbers — self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues, sums of them are self-adjoint, real multiples of them are self-adjoint, and Segment 3's quadratic argument is completing the square. Carry the analogy and the theorems stop being surprising. — Axler §7.A, p. 209
Worked example — reading self-adjointness off a matrix (fully worked). Suppose T on F² has matrix with rows (2, b) and (3, 7) in the standard basis, which is orthonormal. For which b is T self-adjoint?

ℳ(T*) is the conjugate transpose, with rows (2, 3) and (conj(b), 7). Self-adjointness means ℳ(T) = ℳ(T*), so b = 3 and conj(b) = 3 — both satisfied exactly when b = 3. — Axler §7.A, Example 7.12, p. 209

Over R this is the familiar statement that a self-adjoint operator is one whose matrix is symmetric — provided the basis is orthonormal, which is where the hypothesis of Theorem 7.10 earns its place.

Theorem 7.13 (real eigenvalues). Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real. — Axler §7.A, Theorem 7.13, p. 210

Over R that is automatic and the theorem says nothing; over C it is the analogy's first payoff, and it is proved in one chain of equalities — the unit's guided proof asks you to write it. — Axler §7.A, p. 210

Definition 7.18 and Theorem 7.20 (normal). An operator is normal if it commutes with its adjoint, TT* = T*T. Equivalently, T is normal if and only if ‖Tv‖ = ‖T*v‖ for every v ∈ V. — Axler §7.A, Definition 7.18, p. 212, and Theorem 7.20, p. 213
Normal is strictly weaker than self-adjoint. Every self-adjoint operator is normal, since T* = T certainly commutes with T. The converse fails: the operator on F² with matrix rows (2, −3) and (3, 2) is not self-adjoint — the entry 3 is not the conjugate of −3 — yet both TT* and T*T have matrix rows (13, 0) and (0, 13), so it is normal. — Axler §7.A, Example 7.19, p. 212

Schur's upper-triangular matrix already gives the eigenvalues, since for a triangular matrix they are exactly the diagonal entries. — Axler §5.B, Theorem 5.32, p. 152 What the complex case adds is that for a normal operator the off-diagonal entries must vanish as well.

Theorem 7.20 is the characterisation actually used in proofs, because it converts an operator identity into a statement about norms, one vector at a time. It is the engine of the Complex Spectral Theorem, which shows Schur's upper-triangular matrix must in fact be diagonal by comparing ‖Tej‖ with ‖T*ej‖ row by row. — Axler §7.B, Theorem 7.24, p. 218

Self-check 2. Which implication holds between "self-adjoint" and "normal"?

T = T* makes TT* = T*T immediately. The reverse fails, and Example 7.19's rotation-and-scaling matrix is the standard witness. This is why the Complex Spectral Theorem is stated for normal operators — a strictly larger class — while the Real Spectral Theorem needs self-adjointness.

Self-check 3. Over R, what does self-adjointness say about the matrix of T?

Theorem 7.10 identifies ℳ(T*) with the transpose only when the basis is orthonormal, so "symmetric matrix" and "self-adjoint operator" correspond only under that hypothesis. Diagonality is the conclusion of the Real Spectral Theorem, not the hypothesis — and it holds for a particular orthonormal basis, not for the one you started with.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

The Real Spectral Theorem

One ingredient comes from a section this module has not otherwise used. For a subspace U of an inner product space, the orthogonal complement U⊥ is the set of vectors orthogonal to every vector in U. — Axler §6.C, Definition 6.45, p. 193 All that is needed below is that it is a subspace and that V = U ⊕ U⊥. — Axler §6.C, Theorem 6.47, p. 194

Theorem 7.26 (invertible quadratics). If T ∈ ℒ(V) is self-adjoint and b, c ∈ R satisfy b² < 4c, then T² + bT + cI is invertible. — Axler §7.B, Theorem 7.26, p. 219
Why it is true — completing the square, with operators. The condition b² < 4c is exactly the condition under which the real quadratic x² + bx + c = (x + b/2)² + (c − b²/4) is strictly positive, hence never zero. The operator version runs the same computation inside an inner product.

For v ≠ 0, ⟨(T² + bT + cI)v, v⟩ = ‖Tv‖² + b⟨Tv, v⟩ + c‖v‖², where ⟨T²v, v⟩ = ⟨Tv, Tv⟩ because T is self-adjoint — that is the only place the hypothesis is used, and it is indispensable. Bounding the middle term below by −|b|‖Tv‖‖v‖ with la-13's Cauchy–Schwarz and completing the square gives (‖Tv‖ − |b|‖v‖/2)² + (c − b²/4)‖v‖² > 0. — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.15, p. 172

A positive inner product forces (T² + bT + cI)v ≠ 0, so the operator is injective, hence invertible by la-08's Theorem 3.69. — Axler §7.B, p. 219

Theorem 7.27 (self-adjoint operators have eigenvalues). If V ≠ {0} and T ∈ ℒ(V) is self-adjoint, then T has an eigenvalue. — Axler §7.B, Theorem 7.27, p. 220
Why it is true — la-12's argument, repaired for R. la-12 proved existence over C by taking v, Tv, …, Tnv, which is too long to be independent, and factoring the resulting polynomial into linear factors. Over R a polynomial need not factor that far — that is precisely why la-11's rotation has no eigenvalue — but it does factor into linear factors and irreducible quadratics.

So the relation becomes 0 = c(T² + b₁T + c₁I)⋯(T − λ₁I)⋯(T − λmI)v with every bj² < 4cj. Theorem 7.26 makes every quadratic factor invertible, so they can all be cancelled — and since c ≠ 0 and v ≠ 0, at least one linear factor must survive, giving m > 0 and some non-injective T − λjI. — Axler §7.B, p. 220

That is the whole role of self-adjointness in the existence half: it turns the quadratic factors, which over R are exactly the obstruction, into invertible operators that cannot absorb the vector.

Theorem 7.28 (complements stay invariant). If T is self-adjoint and U is invariant under T, then U⊥ is invariant under T, and both T|U and T|U⊥ are self-adjoint. — Axler §7.B, Theorem 7.28, p. 221

The proof of the first part is one line — for v ∈ U⊥ and u ∈ U, ⟨Tv, u⟩ = ⟨v, Tu⟩ = 0, since Tu ∈ U — and it is exactly what la-11's Example 5.15 showed can fail in general: there an invariant subspace had no invariant complement, and the decomposition programme stalled. Self-adjointness is what supplies one, and supplies it canonically. — Axler §7.B, p. 221

Resolving the prediction gate

Theorem 7.29 (Real Spectral Theorem). Suppose F = R and T ∈ ℒ(V). Then the following are equivalent: T is self-adjoint; V has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of T; T has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis of V. — Axler §7.B, Theorem 7.29, p. 221
Verdict: yes to both — and the two halves are the two halves of the proof.

(i) An eigenvalue exists. Theorem 7.27, whose proof is la-12's polynomial argument with the irreducible quadratics neutralised by Theorem 7.26. So la-11's rotation is not a counterexample to anything here: it is not self-adjoint, its matrix being antisymmetric.

(ii) Diagonalisable, and orthonormally. Induct on dim V. Take a unit eigenvector u — Theorem 7.27 supplies one, and dividing by its norm makes it a unit vector — and set U = span(u), invariant and one-dimensional. Theorem 7.28 makes T|U⊥ self-adjoint on a space of dimension dim V − 1, so the induction hypothesis gives an orthonormal basis of U⊥ of eigenvectors. Adjoining u gives one for V. — Axler §7.B, p. 222

The reverse implication is the easy one and worth doing first: a diagonal real matrix equals its own transpose, so an operator diagonal in an orthonormal basis satisfies T = T*.

What the theorem costs is worth stating. Every earlier unit is in the proof — la-03's squeeze bounds the list v, …, Tnv; la-04 extends bases; la-08 turns injectivity into invertibility; la-12 supplies the polynomial argument; la-13 supplies Cauchy–Schwarz; la-14 supplies Riesz, which is what makes the adjoint exist at all. The Real Spectral Theorem is where the module's machinery is spent all at once.

Canvas: a symmetric operator has perpendicular axes

The grey circle is the unit vectors of R² and the green curve is its image under a varying operator; the amber lines are the operator's eigenvector directions, computed from the matrix rather than read off the picture. In the symmetric state they are perpendicular, whatever the off-diagonal entry does — that is the theorem. The non-symmetric state is upper-triangular with distinct real eigenvalues, so it is still diagonalisable and still has two eigenvector directions; they simply stop being at right angles. Watch also where the eigenvector lines sit relative to the ellipse: in the symmetric state they are its axes, and in the non-symmetric state they are not.

Worked example — the theorem in three dimensions (fully worked). Let T on R³ have matrix with rows (14, −13, 8), (−13, 14, 8), (8, 8, −7) in the standard basis.

The matrix is symmetric and the standard basis is orthonormal, so T is self-adjoint by Theorem 7.10 and Definition 7.11, and Theorem 7.29 promises an orthonormal eigenbasis. It is (1, −1, 0)/√2, (1, 1, 1)/√3, (1, 1, −2)/√6, with respect to which ℳ(T) is diagonal with entries 27, 9, −15. — Axler §7.B, Example 7.30, p. 222

Once such a basis is known the operator is completely pinned down by it, and so is any operator built by prescribing values on it. — Axler §3.A, Theorem 3.5, p. 54 That is what makes "define S on the eigenbasis" a construction rather than a wish, and this unit's problem set uses it to build a cube root.

Two things to notice. The eigenvalues are real, as Theorem 7.13 requires. And the three eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal without being made so — they came out that way, which is the content of the theorem and not a feature of this example.

Self-check 4. Why is la-11's rotation T(x, y) = (−y, x) not a counterexample to Theorem 7.29?

Its matrix in the standard orthonormal basis has rows (0, −1) and (1, 0), whose transpose has rows (0, 1) and (−1, 0) — so T* = −T, not T. Theorem 7.29 is stated for F = R, so the second option has it backwards, and the theorem holds in every finite dimension including 2.

Self-check 5. Where exactly does the proof of Theorem 7.29 use self-adjointness?

Theorem 7.27 supplies the eigenvector, and Theorem 7.28 supplies the self-adjointness of T|U⊥ without which the induction hypothesis would not apply. Dropping the second use is the standard gap in this proof: la-11's Example 5.15 shows an invariant subspace need not have an invariant complement at all, so nothing about the restriction can be taken for granted.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Prove that every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real. (Let Tv = λv with v ≠ 0, and compute ⟨Tv, v⟩ in two ways — once moving λ out of the first slot, once out of the second. Say where v ≠ 0 is used.) — cf. Axler §7.A, Theorem 7.13, p. 210

Reveal model answer

Proof. Let T be self-adjoint, λ an eigenvalue, and v ≠ 0 with Tv = λv. Then

λ‖v‖² = ⟨λv, v⟩ = ⟨Tv, v⟩ = ⟨v, Tv⟩ = ⟨v, λv⟩ = conj(λ)‖v‖².

The third equality is self-adjointness; the last is la-13's second-slot conjugation. Since v ≠ 0, definiteness gives ‖v‖² ≠ 0, so it may be cancelled, leaving λ = conj(λ) — that is, λ is real. ∎

Two remarks. The hypothesis v ≠ 0 is used exactly once, for the cancellation, and without it the chain would read 0 = 0 and prove nothing — which is also why Definition 5.5 insists an eigenvector be nonzero. And note that the statement is empty over R, where every scalar is real by definition; its content is entirely about C, and it is the sharpest form of the analogy between self-adjoint operators and real numbers.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. The adjoint T* is defined by ⟨Tv, w⟩ = ⟨v, T*w⟩ (Definition 7.2) and exists because Riesz says the functional v ↦ ⟨Tv, w⟩ has a unique representing vector. It is linear (7.5), reverses products (7.6), and with respect to an orthonormal basis its matrix is the conjugate transpose (7.10).
  2. Self-adjoint means T = T* (Definition 7.11) — over R, a symmetric matrix in an orthonormal basis. The adjoint behaves like complex conjugation, so self-adjoint operators behave like real numbers: their eigenvalues are real (7.13). Normal, TT* = T*T (7.18), is strictly weaker and governs the complex case.
  3. The Real Spectral Theorem (7.29): over R, self-adjoint ⟺ an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors exists ⟺ a diagonal matrix in an orthonormal basis exists. The proof needs two things self-adjointness alone provides — an eigenvalue (7.27, via the invertibility of irreducible quadratics in T, 7.26) and an invariant orthogonal complement to induct into (7.28), which la-11 showed is not available in general.
