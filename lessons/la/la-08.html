Invertibility and isomorphism (la-08)

When are two vector spaces 'the same'? The first taste of isomorphism.

𝒫₃(R) is a space of polynomials. R⁴ is a space of quadruples of numbers. R2,2 is a space of 2-by-2 matrices. Nothing about their elements resembles anything about the others' — and every theorem true of one is true of all three, because there is a dictionary between them that respects addition and scaling in both directions.

That dictionary is an isomorphism, and this unit shows that for finite-dimensional spaces one number decides whether one exists: the dimension. Two spaces over the same field are interchangeable exactly when they have the same dimension, full stop. The unit ends with something stranger. For an operator on a finite-dimensional space, injectivity alone implies surjectivity and surjectivity alone implies injectivity — so half the usual work of checking invertibility simply vanishes. On infinite-dimensional spaces it does not, and the two operators that fail are ones you already know.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let T be a linear map from a vector space V to itself, and suppose T is injective but not surjective. Do you predict such a T exists? Give a separate answer for two cases: V finite-dimensional, and V infinite-dimensional. If your two answers differ, say what breaks. Commit before Segment 3.

Why this matters for the mission: Functoriality sends isomorphisms to isomorphisms — the habit starts here.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

Invertible maps

Definition 3.53 (invertible, inverse). A linear map T ∈ ℒ(V, W) is invertible if there is a linear map S ∈ ℒ(W, V) with ST = I on V and TS = I on W. Such an S is an inverse of T. — Axler §3.D, Definition 3.53, p. 80

Read the two equations carefully: the two I's are different maps, one the identity on V and one the identity on W. Both are required. An S satisfying only ST = I is a left inverse and need not be an inverse at all — Segment 3 shows when the distinction collapses and when it does not.

Theorem 3.54 and Notation 3.55 (the inverse is unique). An invertible linear map has exactly one inverse, written T−1. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.54 and Notation 3.55, p. 80
Why it is true — one line, and worth memorising as a pattern. If S₁ and S₂ are both inverses of T, then
S₁ = S₁I = S₁(TS₂) = (S₁T)S₂ = IS₂ = S₂.

The move is to insert the identity in the form of the other inverse's equation, then reassociate. Associativity of composition — la-05's Theorem 3.9 — is what licenses the middle step, and each of the two inverses is used exactly once: S₂ from the right, S₁ from the left. This argument is not about linear maps at all; it works in any setting where composition is associative and has an identity. — Axler §3.D, p. 80

Theorem 3.56 (invertible = injective and surjective). A linear map is invertible if and only if it is injective and surjective. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.56, p. 80
Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof). (⇒) If Tu = Tv then applying T−1 gives u = v, so T is injective; and any w ∈ W equals T(T−1w), so T is surjective.

(⇐) Given injectivity and surjectivity, define Sw to be the vector with T(Sw) = w — surjectivity says one exists, injectivity says only one does, and it takes both to make S a function. Then TS = I immediately, and ST = I because T((ST)v) = Tv and T is injective.

There is a third obligation that is easy to overlook and is a third of the proof: S must be shown linear, because Definition 3.53 demands an inverse in ℒ(W, V), not merely a function. Axler gets it from T(Sw₁ + Sw₂) = w₁ + w₂ together with uniqueness: Sw₁ + Sw₂ is the preimage of w₁ + w₂, which is what S(w₁ + w₂) is defined to be. — Axler §3.D, p. 81

Worked example — two maps that fail, in different ways (fully worked).

Multiplication by x² on 𝒫(R), (Tp)(x) = x²p(x), is not invertible because it is not surjective: the constant polynomial 1 is not x² times anything, since x²p has degree at least 2 whenever p ≠ 0. It is injective — x²p(x) is identically zero only when p is.

The backward shift on F∞, T(x₁, x₂, x₃, …) = (x₂, x₃, …), is not invertible because it is not injective: (1, 0, 0, …) is in its null space, as la-06 computed. It is surjective — any sequence is the shift of the sequence with a 0 prepended.

Between them the two examples show that the conditions in Theorem 3.56 are independent: each fails one and satisfies the other. Keep both in mind; they are the counterexamples that make Segment 3's theorem surprising. — Axler §3.D, Example 3.57, p. 81

Self-check 1. T ∈ ℒ(V, W) and S ∈ ℒ(W, V) satisfy ST = I on V. Does that make T invertible?

Definition 3.53 requires both equations, on two different spaces. ST = I alone does force T injective — if Tv = 0 then v = S(Tv) = 0 — but injectivity alone is not invertibility, as multiplication by x² shows. Theorem 3.54 says nothing here: uniqueness presupposes that an inverse exists.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Isomorphism: sameness up to relabelling

Definition 3.58 (isomorphism). An isomorphism is an invertible linear map. Two vector spaces are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism from one onto the other. — Axler §3.D, Definition 3.58, p. 82

"Isomorphism" and "invertible linear map" name the same objects; the two words carry different emphasis. Think of an isomorphism T : V → W as relabelling each v as Tv. Because the relabelling respects addition and scaling in both directions, no statement expressible in the language of vector spaces can tell V and W apart. — Axler §3.D, p. 82

Theorem 3.59 (dimension decides). Two finite-dimensional vector spaces over F are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.59, p. 82
Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof). (⇒) An isomorphism T has null T = {0} and range T = W, so la-06's Fundamental Theorem reads dim V = 0 + dim W.

(⇐) Given dim V = dim W = n, take bases v₁, …, vn and w₁, …, wn and let T send c₁v₁ + ⋯ + cnvn to c₁w₁ + ⋯ + cnwn. This is a well-defined linear map by la-05's Theorem 3.5; it is surjective because the w's span, and injective because the w's are independent. Theorem 3.56 makes it an isomorphism.

Notice how cleanly the two halves of "basis" divide the labour here, one giving surjectivity and the other injectivity — the same split as in Theorem 3.5 itself. — Axler §3.D, p. 82

The consequence to internalise. Every finite-dimensional space V over F is isomorphic to Fⁿ with n = dim V. That does not make general vector spaces pointless. Study Fⁿ alone and you immediately meet null spaces and ranges of maps on it, and although each of those is isomorphic to some Fᵏ, insisting on that description adds bookkeeping and no insight. The right lesson is that dimension is a complete invariant here, which is rare and will not survive into later structures. — Axler §3.D, p. 83

Canvas: same dimension, therefore interchangeable

Two spaces drawn as columns of basis vectors. When the counts agree, a pairing exists and every dot is matched — that pairing extends to an isomorphism by Theorem 3.5. When they differ, some dot is always left over, and Theorem 3.59 says no relabelling can fix it.

Theorems 3.60 and 3.61 (maps are matrices, and how many there are). With bases fixed, ℳ is an isomorphism from ℒ(V, W) onto Fm,n. Consequently ℒ(V, W) is finite-dimensional with dim ℒ(V, W) = (dim V)(dim W). — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.60 and Theorem 3.61, p. 83

The ℳ in question is la-07's matrix of a linear map. — Axler §3.C, Definition 3.32, p. 70 la-07 showed it is linear; Theorem 3.60 adds that it is bijective, and the two together are what licence the habit of saying "the matrix" as though a map and its matrix were the same object. They are the same only relative to a fixed pair of bases, which is precisely what the isomorphism depends on. The dimension count then follows from Theorem 3.59 and la-07's dim Fm,n = mn. — Axler §3.D, p. 83

Definition 3.62 (matrix of a vector) and Theorem 3.65. For v ∈ V with basis v₁, …, vn, the matrix ℳ(v) is the n-by-1 column of coefficients in v = c₁v₁ + ⋯ + cnvn. Then for every T ∈ ℒ(V, W) and every v ∈ V, ℳ(Tv) = ℳ(T)ℳ(v). — Axler §3.D, Definition 3.62, p. 84, and Theorem 3.65, p. 85
The column is well defined only because a vector has exactly one list of coefficients in a given basis, which is la-04's criterion. — Axler §2.B, Theorem 2.29, p. 39
Worked example — a vector as a column, and the identity that ties everything together (fully worked). With the standard basis of 𝒫₃(R), the polynomial 2 − 7x + 5x³ has ℳ(2 − 7x + 5x³) = the column (2, −7, 0, 5) — and the 0 in the third slot must be written, since the basis has four entries and x² is one of them. — Axler §3.D, Example 3.63, p. 84

Theorem 3.65 then says that applying T to a vector and multiplying columns by matrices are the same operation, read through ℳ. Its proof is a three-line chain: expand Tv along the basis, use linearity of ℳ, then recognise the result via la-07's column readings — column k of ℳ(T) is ℳ(Tvk), and a combination of columns is a matrix times a column. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.64, p. 85

That is the precise sense in which "every linear map is a matrix multiplication": relabel V as Fn,1 and W as Fm,1 via ℳ, and T becomes multiplication by ℳ(T). The relabelling is basis-dependent, and choosing a basis that makes the matrix simple is the recurring project of the rest of the subject. — Axler §3.D, p. 86

Self-check 2. dim V = 3 and dim W = 5. What is dim ℒ(V, W)?

Theorem 3.61 gives (dim V)(dim W) = 15, matching the 5-by-3 matrices, which number mn = 15 basis elements. The third option confuses "infinitely many elements" with "infinite-dimensional" — R¹ has infinitely many elements and dimension 1.

Self-check 3. Are 𝒫₃(R) and R2,2 isomorphic?

dim 𝒫₃(R) = 4 (basis 1, x, x², x³) and dim R2,2 = 2·2 = 4 (la-07, Theorem 3.40), so Theorem 3.59 settles it. The second option is exactly the intuition the theorem overturns: what the elements are is irrelevant. The third mistakes an existence theorem for a construction — Theorem 3.59 proves an isomorphism exists without your having to name one, though of course you can.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Operators, and where half the work disappears

Definition 3.67 (operator). A linear map from a vector space to itself is an operator, and ℒ(V) = ℒ(V, V) denotes the set of operators on V. — Axler §3.D, Definition 3.67, p. 86

ℒ(V) is a vector space, but many natural subsets of it are not subspaces. The non-invertible operators are one: as soon as dim V > 1, two of them can sum to the identity, so the set fails closure under addition. Settling such a question means running la-02's three-condition test. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.34, pp. 18–19 It is a problem in this unit's set, and the interest is in which of the three conditions fails — not the one most people check first.

Resolving the prediction gate

Verdict: yes if V is infinite-dimensional, no if it is finite-dimensional. Both cases are already in front of us.

Infinite-dimensional. Multiplication by x² is an operator on 𝒫(R) that is injective and not surjective. The backward shift is an operator on F∞ that is surjective and not injective. So on infinite-dimensional spaces neither condition implies the other, and neither alone implies invertibility. — Axler §3.D, Example 3.68, p. 86

Theorem 3.69 (injective = surjective = invertible, in finite dimensions). Suppose V is finite-dimensional and T ∈ ℒ(V). Then (a) T is invertible, (b) T is injective, and (c) T is surjective are equivalent. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.69, p. 87
Why it is true — and why it needs domain and codomain to coincide. (a) ⇒ (b) is Theorem 3.56. For (b) ⇒ (c): injectivity gives dim null T = 0, so la-06's Fundamental Theorem gives dim range T = dim V; range T is a subspace of V of full dimension, hence equals V. For (c) ⇒ (a): surjectivity gives dim range T = dim V, so dim null T = 0 and T is injective, and with surjectivity already in hand Theorem 3.56 gives invertibility.

Every step is the Fundamental Theorem plus the observation that range T ⊆ V. That containment is the hypothesis doing the hidden work: for a map between different spaces of the same dimension the equivalence still holds, but for a map V → W with dim W ≠ dim V it fails at once — la-06's Theorems 3.23 and 3.24 say so. — Axler §3.D, p. 87

Worked example — a hard-looking claim, made easy (fully worked). Show that for every polynomial q ∈ 𝒫(R) there is a polynomial p with ((x² + 5x + 7)p)″ = q.

𝒫(R) is infinite-dimensional, so Theorem 3.69 does not apply to it — but any particular nonzero q has some degree m, and we may work inside 𝒫m(R), which is finite-dimensional. Define T on 𝒫m(R) by Tp = ((x² + 5x + 7)p)″. Multiplying by a quadratic raises degree by 2 and differentiating twice lowers it by 2, so T really does map 𝒫m(R) into itself and is an operator.

It is injective: if ((x² + 5x + 7)p)″ = 0 then (x² + 5x + 7)p has the form ax + b, degree at most 1; but if p ≠ 0 that product has degree at least 2. So p = 0 and null T = {0}. Theorem 3.69 now hands over surjectivity with no further work, so some p ∈ 𝒫m(R) has Tp = q.

The technique is worth naming: restrict an infinite-dimensional problem to a finite-dimensional subspace large enough to hold the answer, and the free half of Theorem 3.69 does the rest. Checking surjectivity directly here would mean solving a differential equation. — Axler §3.D, Example 3.70, p. 87

Self-check 4. T is an operator on a 6-dimensional space and dim range T = 6. Is T invertible?

range T is a subspace of V with dim range T = dim V, hence equals V (la-04's guided-proof result), so T is surjective, and Theorem 3.69 supplies the rest. The second option is the habit the theorem exists to break. The third raises a fair point — dimension does need converting into set equality — but that conversion is a one-line argument, not an obstruction.

Self-check 5. Why does Theorem 3.69 fail on 𝒫(R)?

Every step of the proof runs through dim V = dim null T + dim range T, which presupposes a finite dimension — la-06's Theorem 3.22 states that hypothesis explicitly. The third option is false and multiplication by x² is the counterexample: it is injective on 𝒫(R) and still not invertible.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose T ∈ ℒ(U, V) and S ∈ ℒ(V, W) are both invertible. Prove that ST ∈ ℒ(U, W) is invertible and that (ST)−1 = T−1S−1. (Do not argue via injectivity and surjectivity — exhibit the inverse and verify Definition 3.53 directly. Note that the claimed inverse reverses the order, and be able to say why.) — cf. Axler §3.D, Exercise 1, p. 88

Reveal model answer

Proof. First check the composite makes sense: T−1 ∈ ℒ(V, U) and S−1 ∈ ℒ(W, V), so T−1S−1 ∈ ℒ(W, U) — which is the right home for an inverse of ST ∈ ℒ(U, W). Written in the other order, S−1T−1 is not even defined.

Now verify both equations, using associativity (la-05, Theorem 3.9) throughout:
(T−1S−1)(ST) = T−1(S−1S)T = T−1IT = T−1T = I on U;
(ST)(T−1S−1) = S(TT−1)S−1 = SIS−1 = SS−1 = I on W.
So ST is invertible with inverse T−1S−1, and Theorem 3.54 makes that inverse the only one, justifying the notation (ST)−1. ∎

The reversal is not a quirk of notation. ST does T then S; undoing it means undoing the last thing done first. The type check at the top is the mechanical version of that observation, and it is the fastest way to remember which order is correct.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. Invertible means a two-sided linear inverse exists (Definition 3.53); it is unique (Theorem 3.54), and it exists exactly when the map is injective and surjective (Theorem 3.56). Both conditions are needed and are independent: multiplication by x² has one, the backward shift the other.
  2. An isomorphism is an invertible linear map, and for finite-dimensional spaces over one field dimension is a complete invariant: isomorphic exactly when the dimensions agree (Theorem 3.59). Applying this to ℳ shows maps and matrices are the same thing relative to fixed bases (Theorem 3.60), giving dim ℒ(V, W) = (dim V)(dim W) (Theorem 3.61) and, with the matrix of a vector, ℳ(Tv) = ℳ(T)ℳ(v) (Theorem 3.65).
  3. For an operator on a finite-dimensional space, injective, surjective and invertible are the same condition (Theorem 3.69) — because the Fundamental Theorem's count applies and the range sits inside the domain. On infinite-dimensional spaces the equivalence fails, and the standard technique is to restrict to a finite-dimensional subspace big enough to contain the answer.
