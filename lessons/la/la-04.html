Bases and dimension (la-04)

Why every basis of the same space has the same size — a genuinely deep fact.

R² has infinitely many bases. (1, 0), (0, 1) is one; (7, 5), (−4, 9) is another; (1, 2), (3, 5) is a third. They look nothing alike, they were chosen for different reasons, and no two of them share a vector. Every single one has length 2.

Why should that be? Nothing in the definition of a basis mentions a number. A basis is a list that is independent and spans — two conditions, both about behaviour, neither about counting. And yet the counts agree, always, in every vector space, and they agree so reliably that we are entitled to give the common value a name and call it the dimension. That entitlement is not obvious and it is not free: it is a theorem, and la-03's squeeze is the whole of its proof. Once it is in hand, the rest of this lesson is a sequence of results that convert counting back into structure.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Inside R⁹, take two subspaces U and W, each of dimension 5. Do you predict that U and W must share a nonzero vector — that is, must U ∩ W ≠ {0} — or could two such subspaces meet only at the origin? Commit to yes or no, and if you say yes, try to say what would go wrong if they met only at 0. The verdict arrives in Segment 3.

Why this matters for the mission: dim(ker ∂)/im ∂ computations are how Betti numbers are actually found.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

Bases: independent and spanning at once

Definition 2.27 (basis). A basis of V is a list of vectors in V that is linearly independent and spans V. — Axler §2.B, Definition 2.27, p. 39

Both halves are needed, and dropping either gives something recognisable but different. (1, 2, −4), (7, −5, 6) is independent in F³ but does not span it. (1, 2), (3, 5), (4, 13) spans F² but is not independent. Neither is a basis. — Axler §2.B, Example 2.28, p. 39

Theorem 2.29 (criterion for basis). A list v₁, …, vn of vectors in V is a basis of V if and only if every v ∈ V can be written uniquely in the form v = a₁v₁ + ⋯ + anvn with a₁, …, an ∈ F. — Axler §2.B, Theorem 2.29, p. 39
Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof). The two halves of "basis" map exactly onto the two halves of "uniquely written": spanning gives at least one representation, independence gives at most one.

(⇒) Spanning supplies scalars aj with v = Σajvj. If also v = Σcjvj, subtract: 0 = Σ(aj − cj)vj. Independence forces every aj − cj = 0.

(⇐) Existence of a representation for every v is spanning, immediately. For independence, apply uniqueness at the single point v = 0: the trivial representation is one of them, so by uniqueness it is the only one. That last move — test the general hypothesis at 0 — is the whole of the converse, and it is the same instinct la-03 used when it stated independence as a condition on 0 rather than on a general vector. — Axler §2.B, p. 40

Two construction results follow, and they are exact mirrors of each other. One shrinks a spanning list until it becomes a basis; the other grows an independent list until it becomes one.

Theorem 2.31 (spanning list contains a basis). Every spanning list in a vector space can be reduced to a basis of the vector space. — Axler §2.B, Theorem 2.31, p. 40
Theorem 2.33 (independent list extends to a basis). Every linearly independent list of vectors in a finite-dimensional vector space can be extended to a basis of the vector space. — Axler §2.B, Theorem 2.33, p. 41
Why they are true, and why one proof does both. For Theorem 2.31, walk the list left to right and delete vj whenever vj ∈ span(v₁, …, vj−1). Deleting such a vector cannot shrink the span, so what survives still spans; and no survivor lies in the span of its predecessors, which by the Linear Dependence Lemma (la-03, Lemma 2.21) is exactly independence. Note the direction the lemma is being used in — la-03 read it as "dependence lets you delete", and here it is read contrapositively, as "nothing deletable left means independent".

Theorem 2.33 is then a corollary rather than a fresh argument. Given an independent u₁, …, um, pick any basis w₁, …, wn and run the reduction on the concatenated list u₁, …, um, w₁, …, wn, which certainly spans. No u is ever deleted, because independence of the u's means no u lies in the span of its predecessors. So the result is a basis containing all the u's and some of the w's. Putting the new vectors first is what protects them — with the order reversed, the argument does not run. — Axler §2.B, pp. 40–41

The pattern to keep. "Reduce a spanning list" and "extend an independent list" are the two moves available whenever you need a basis with a prescribed feature. Want a basis of V whose first few vectors lie in a chosen subspace? Take a basis of the subspace and extend. Want a basis drawn from a given generating set? Reduce. Almost every basis-construction argument in the rest of the semester is one of these two, and Theorem 2.34 below is the first of them.
Theorem 2.32 (existence) and Theorem 2.34 (complements). Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis. And if V is finite-dimensional and U is a subspace of V, then there is a subspace W of V with V = U ⊕ W. — Axler §2.B, Theorem 2.32, p. 41, and Theorem 2.34, p. 42
Worked example — how a complement gets built (fully worked). Theorem 2.34 is la-02's direct sums and this lesson's bases meeting for the first time, so it is worth watching the machine run.

U is a subspace of the finite-dimensional V, hence finite-dimensional itself (la-03, Theorem 2.26), hence has a basis u₁, …, um (Theorem 2.32). That list is independent in V, so extend it to a basis u₁, …, um, w₁, …, wn of V (Theorem 2.33). Now set W = span(w₁, …, wn).

By la-02's Theorem 1.45 it suffices to check V = U + W and U ∩ W = {0}. For the first: any v ∈ V expands along the full basis as (a₁u₁ + ⋯ + amum) + (b₁w₁ + ⋯ + bnwn), and the two bracketed pieces lie in U and W respectively. For the second: a vector in U ∩ W has an expression in the u's and another in the w's; equating them and moving everything to one side gives a dependence relation among u₁, …, um, w₁, …, wn, whose coefficients must therefore all vanish — so the vector is 0. — Axler §2.B, p. 42

Self-check 1. (1, 2), (3, 5), (4, 13) spans F². Which statement is correct?

A basis must also be independent, and this list is not (Example 2.28(d)). But Theorem 2.31 guarantees that any spanning list reduces to a basis by deleting vectors — so a sublist is a basis. That is the general point: spanning lists are never too small, only possibly too big.

Self-check 2. In the proof of Theorem 2.33, the independent list u₁, …, um is placed before the basis w₁, …, wn in the concatenated list. Why does the order matter?

Theorem 2.31's procedure is directional: it scans left to right and deletes anything already reachable from what came before. With the u's first, a deleted u would lie in the span of earlier u's, contradicting independence — so none can go. With the w's first, the w's would already span V by the time the u's were reached, and every u would be deleted, producing a basis containing none of them.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Dimension: the number that is allowed to exist

Theorem 2.35 (basis length does not depend on basis). Any two bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same length. — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.35, p. 44
Why it is true — three lines, and la-03 does all the work. Let B₁ and B₂ be bases of V. Then B₁ is independent and B₂ spans, so by la-03's Theorem 2.23, length(B₁) ≤ length(B₂). Now swap the roles: B₂ is independent and B₁ spans, so length(B₂) ≤ length(B₁). Two inequalities, opposite directions, therefore equality.

It is worth pausing on how little is happening and how much it buys. The proof has no construction in it at all; the entire content sits in la-03's squeeze, and the only new idea is that a basis is both things at once, so it can be fed into the squeeze from either side. This is the payoff for having proved Theorem 2.23 in the general form "every independent list ≤ every spanning list" rather than for some particular pair. — Axler §2.C, p. 44

Definition 2.36 (dimension). The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the length of any basis of the space, written dim V. — Axler §2.C, Definition 2.36, p. 44

"Any basis" is doing real work in that sentence, and it is licensed by Theorem 2.35 and by nothing else. Definitions of this shape — pick a representative, take a number off it, declare the number to be a property of the object — are only legitimate once the choice has been proved not to matter. You will meet the same obligation again in la-07 (the matrix of a linear map depends on the basis; its rank does not) and in la-09.

Worked example — two dimensions read off directly (fully worked). dim Fⁿ = n, because the standard basis has length n. And dim 𝒫m(F) = m + 1, because 1, z, …, zm is a basis and that list has length m + 1 — the off-by-one comes from the constant polynomial, which is easy to forget to count. — Axler §2.C, Example 2.37, p. 44. The basis it counts is Axler §2.B, Example 2.28(g), p. 39.

A warning that costs marks if ignored: the field is part of the question. C as a vector space over C has dimension 1, with basis the single vector 1. The same set C, regarded as a vector space over R, has dimension 2, with basis 1, i. Nothing about the vectors changed; only the scalars you are permitted to use did. — Axler §2.C, p. 45

Theorem 2.38 (dimension of a subspace). If V is finite-dimensional and U is a subspace of V, then dim U ≤ dim V. — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.38, p. 45

Again three lines: a basis of U is an independent list in V, a basis of V is a spanning list in V, and la-03's Theorem 2.23 compares them. Nearly everything in this segment is that theorem wearing a different hat. — Axler §2.C, p. 45

Theorems 2.39 and 2.42 (the right length is enough). Suppose V is finite-dimensional. Every linearly independent list in V of length dim V is a basis of V; and every spanning list in V of length dim V is a basis of V. — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.39, p. 45, and Theorem 2.42, p. 46
Why they are true — and why they are the same proof twice. For 2.39: an independent list extends to a basis (Theorem 2.33), every basis has length dim V (Theorem 2.35), and the list already has that length — so the extension adjoined nothing, and the list was already a basis. For 2.42: a spanning list reduces to a basis (Theorem 2.31), every basis has length dim V, and the list already has it — so the reduction deleted nothing.

Each argument runs a construction that is known in advance to have no room to move, and reads off the conclusion from the fact that it did nothing. That is a proof technique in its own right and it recurs; la-08 will use it to show that in finite dimensions injectivity and surjectivity are the same condition. — Axler §2.C, pp. 45–46

Worked example — a basis certified by counting (fully worked). Is (5, 7), (4, 3) a basis of F²?

Independence is quick: neither vector is a scalar multiple of the other (la-03, Example 2.18(b)), since (5, 7) = λ(4, 3) would need λ = 5/4 from the first slot and λ = 7/3 from the second. The list therefore has length 2 = dim F², so Theorem 2.39 declares it a basis — and we never checked spanning at all. Halving the work is the point of the theorem. — Axler §2.C, Example 2.40, p. 45

Self-check 3. Why is Definition 2.36 (dimension) not circular or ill-posed?

A definition that reads a number off an arbitrarily chosen representative is only well posed once the choice is proved irrelevant — that is exactly Theorem 2.35's content, and exactly why it comes first. Most spaces have no "standard" basis at all (what is the standard basis of {(x, y, z) : x + y + z = 0}?), and dimension is defined far beyond Fⁿ.

Self-check 4. A list of 4 vectors in a 4-dimensional space V is known to span V. What else can you conclude?

Theorem 2.42: a spanning list of length dim V is a basis, so it is independent too. The third option inverts the truth — 4 independent vectors is exactly what a 4-dimensional space accommodates; it is 5 that would be too many.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Dimension of a sum, and the answer to the gate

Theorem 2.43 (dimension of a sum). If U₁ and U₂ are subspaces of a finite-dimensional vector space, then dim(U₁ + U₂) = dim U₁ + dim U₂ − dim(U₁ ∩ U₂). — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.43, p. 47

The shape is familiar from counting finite sets: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|, with sum standing in for union. The analogy is a good mnemonic and a bad guide — Segment 3's problem set asks what happens with three subspaces, where the set-counting formula has an obvious extension and the subspace version does not survive it. — Axler §2.C, p. 46

Why it is true (the shape of Axler's proof). The construction is the one this lesson has been rehearsing: start at the intersection and extend outwards, twice. Take a basis u₁, …, um of U₁ ∩ U₂. Extend it inside U₁ to u₁, …, um, v₁, …, vj, and separately inside U₂ to u₁, …, um, w₁, …, wk. So dim U₁ = m + j and dim U₂ = m + k. Claim: the pooled list u's, v's, w's is a basis of U₁ + U₂, giving dim(U₁ + U₂) = m + j + k = (m + j) + (m + k) − m, which is the formula.

Spanning is immediate. Independence is where the argument earns its keep. Given a vanishing combination, move the w-part to one side: c₁w₁ + ⋯ + ckwk is then visibly a combination of u's and v's, hence lies in U₁ — and it lies in U₂ too, since the w's do. So it lies in U₁ ∩ U₂, and is therefore expressible in the u's alone. Comparing that with the w-expression uses independence of u's-with-w's to kill all the c's. What remains is a relation among u's and v's only, and independence inside U₁ kills the rest. — Axler §2.C, p. 47

The single idea to extract: a vector that can be written two ways, once inside each subspace, is a vector in the intersection — and the intersection is exactly the part whose basis you started from.

Resolving the prediction gate

Verdict: yes, they must meet in more than 0 — and the count proves it. Let U, W ⊆ R⁹ with dim U = dim W = 5. Then U + W is a subspace of R⁹, so dim(U + W) ≤ 9 by Theorem 2.38. But Theorem 2.43 says dim(U + W) = 5 + 5 − dim(U ∩ W). Combining, 10 − dim(U ∩ W) ≤ 9, so dim(U ∩ W) ≥ 1, and a subspace of dimension at least 1 contains a nonzero vector. Hence U ∩ W ≠ {0}.

If you predicted "no", the intuition to correct is probably a picture of two planes in three dimensions meeting in a line and imagining that more room always lets things separate. It does not: the ambient dimension is a hard budget, and two subspaces whose dimensions overspend it are forced to overlap by exactly the amount of the overspend. — cf. Axler §2.C, Exercise 12, p. 49

Self-check 5. U and W are subspaces of R⁸ with dim U = 3, dim W = 5, and U + W = R⁸. What follows?

Theorem 2.43 gives 8 = 3 + 5 − dim(U ∩ W), so dim(U ∩ W) = 0 and U ∩ W = {0}; la-02's Theorem 1.45 then makes the sum direct. The third option miscounts: 3 + 5 = 8 exactly, with no excess, which is precisely why the intersection is trivial rather than one-dimensional.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose V is finite-dimensional and U is a subspace of V with dim U = dim V. Prove that U = V. (One containment is free. For the other, take a basis of U and ask what its length entitles you to say about it as a list in V.) — cf. Axler §2.C, Exercise 1, p. 48

Reveal model answer

Proof. U ⊆ V holds because U is a subspace of V; only V ⊆ U needs work.

Since U is a subspace of a finite-dimensional space it is finite-dimensional (la-03, Theorem 2.26), so it has a basis u₁, …, um (Theorem 2.32), and m = dim U = dim V. Regard that list as a list in V. It is linearly independent there — independence is a statement about scalars and the vectors themselves, and does not change when the ambient space is enlarged — and its length is dim V. By Theorem 2.39 it is therefore a basis of V, and in particular spans V. Hence V = span(u₁, …, um) ⊆ U, the last inclusion because U is a subspace containing each uj (la-03, Theorem 2.7). Combining, U = V. ∎

The load-bearing step is the one that sounds like nothing: a list independent in U is still independent in V. Spanning does not transfer that way — u₁, …, um spans U, and whether it spans V is the whole question. Theorem 2.39 is what converts the half that does transfer into the half that does not, and it can only do so because the lengths match.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. A basis (Definition 2.27) is independent and spanning at once, which is equivalent to every vector having exactly one representation (Theorem 2.29). Bases are never scarce: every spanning list reduces to one (Theorem 2.31) and every independent list extends to one (Theorem 2.33), so every finite-dimensional space has one (Theorem 2.32) and every subspace has a complement (Theorem 2.34).
  2. Because a basis is both things at once, la-03's squeeze can be applied to a pair of bases from both sides, forcing all bases of a space to have equal length (Theorem 2.35). Only then is dimension (Definition 2.36) a legitimate definition. Its immediate consequences — dim U ≤ dim V (2.38), and "right length plus either property is enough" (2.39, 2.42) — are all the squeeze again.
  3. Counting extends to pairs of subspaces: dim(U₁ + U₂) = dim U₁ + dim U₂ − dim(U₁ ∩ U₂) (Theorem 2.43), proved by starting from a basis of the intersection and extending outwards into each. This turns dimension into a tool for proving that subspaces must intersect, and — with la-02's Theorem 1.45 — for certifying direct sums by arithmetic alone.
