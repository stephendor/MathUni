Inner product spaces (la-13)

Geometry enters: length and angle from pure algebra.

Twelve units of linear algebra have said nothing about how long a vector is or what angle two vectors make. Nothing could: the axioms of a vector space mention addition and scaling and no measurement whatever. Length and angle have to be added, and the thing that adds them is an inner product — one extra operation, taking two vectors to a scalar, subject to five conditions.

From that single addition everything geometric follows. The norm is √⟨v, v⟩. Orthogonality is ⟨u, v⟩ = 0. Pythagoras becomes a two-line computation, and so do the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities. This is also the point at which the module reaches the mission's data: a point cloud is a finite set of vectors in an inner product space, and every distance in a filtration is a norm computed exactly as below.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Every inner product produces a norm, ‖v‖ = √⟨v, v⟩. Ask the converse. A norm is any function ‖·‖ that vanishes only at 0, satisfies ‖λu‖ = |λ|‖u‖, and obeys the triangle inequality. Do you predict that every norm arises from some inner product in this way? Commit to yes or no — and if you say no, try to name a norm on R² that could not. The verdict arrives in Segment 3.

Why this matters for the mission: Point clouds live in inner product spaces; distances build filtrations.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

The inner product

Definition 6.3 (inner product). An inner product on V takes each ordered pair (u, v) to a scalar ⟨u, v⟩ ∈ F and satisfies:
positivity: ⟨v, v⟩ ≥ 0 for all v;
definiteness: ⟨v, v⟩ = 0 if and only if v = 0;
additivity in the first slot: ⟨u + v, w⟩ = ⟨u, w⟩ + ⟨v, w⟩;
homogeneity in the first slot: ⟨λu, v⟩ = λ⟨u, v⟩;
conjugate symmetry: ⟨u, v⟩ = conj⟨v, u⟩. — Axler §6.A, Definition 6.3, p. 166

Positivity is a claim that needs conjugate symmetry to make sense at all: ⟨v, v⟩ = conj⟨v, v⟩ forces it to be real, and only then can "≥ 0" be asserted. Over R the conjugation is invisible and conjugate symmetry is plain symmetry. Note also that the asymmetry between the slots is a convention — many physicists put the homogeneity in the second slot instead — and that consistency, not the choice, is what matters. — Axler §6.A, p. 166

Worked example — the standard inner products (fully worked).

Euclidean. On Fⁿ, ⟨(w₁, …, wn), (z₁, …, zn)⟩ = w₁conj(z₁) + ⋯ + wnconj(zn). The conjugates are what make ⟨v, v⟩ = |z₁|² + ⋯ + |zn|² a nonnegative real; without them positivity fails over C at once.

Weighted. For positive c₁, …, cn, the same formula with each term multiplied by cj is again an inner product — so a space carries many inner products, and "the" geometry of Fⁿ is a choice.

Integral. On the continuous real-valued functions on [−1, 1], ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫−11 f(x)g(x) dx. Definiteness here is the genuinely analytic condition: a continuous function whose square integrates to zero must be identically zero. — Axler §6.A, Example 6.4, p. 166

Definition 6.5 and Notation 6.6. An inner product space is a vector space together with an inner product on it. From here on V denotes an inner product space over F, with Fⁿ carrying the Euclidean inner product unless said otherwise. — Axler §6.A, Definition 6.5 and Notation 6.6, p. 167
Theorem 6.7 (basic properties). For fixed u, the map v ↦ ⟨v, u⟩ is a linear functional on V; ⟨0, u⟩ = ⟨u, 0⟩ = 0; ⟨u, v + w⟩ = ⟨u, v⟩ + ⟨u, w⟩; and ⟨u, λv⟩ = conj(λ)⟨u, v⟩. — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.7, p. 167
Why it is true, and the one asymmetry to remember. The first slot is linear by two of the axioms outright, which is why v ↦ ⟨v, u⟩ is a linear functional in la-10's sense; ⟨0, u⟩ = 0 then follows from la-05's Theorem 3.11 rather than needing its own argument.

The second slot is not linear. Additivity survives — take conjugates of the first-slot statement — but homogeneity picks up a conjugate: ⟨u, λv⟩ = conj⟨λv, u⟩ = conj(λ⟨v, u⟩) = conj(λ)⟨u, v⟩. Over R nothing is lost, but over C forgetting the conjugate is the single most common error in the chapter. — Axler §6.A, pp. 167–168

Self-check 1. Over C, what is ⟨u, 3iv⟩ in terms of ⟨u, v⟩?

Theorem 6.7(e) pulls a scalar out of the second slot conjugated: conj(3i) = −3i. Out of the first slot it would come unchanged, which is the asymmetry the definition builds in deliberately. The third option conjugates the wrong thing — the modulus, not the scalar.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Norms, orthogonality, and Pythagoras

Definition 6.8 and Theorem 6.10 (norm). For v ∈ V, ‖v‖ = √⟨v, v⟩. Then ‖v‖ = 0 if and only if v = 0, and ‖λv‖ = |λ|‖v‖. — Axler §6.A, Definition 6.8, p. 168, and Theorem 6.10, p. 169

On Fⁿ this gives ‖(z₁, …, zn)‖ = √(|z₁|² + ⋯ + |zn|²), the familiar Euclidean length; on the continuous functions it gives √∫−11 f². The second is worth staring at — it is a notion of "how big a function is" produced by exactly the same definition. — Axler §6.A, Example 6.9, p. 168

A norm also bounds what an operator can do, and that turns into information about eigenvalues almost for free. If ‖Tv‖ ≤ ‖v‖ for every v, then no scalar of modulus greater than 1 can be an eigenvalue: Tv = λv would give |λ|‖v‖ ≤ ‖v‖, forcing ‖v‖ = 0. So T − λI has trivial null space and is injective. — Axler §3.B, Theorem 3.16, p. 61 On a finite-dimensional space that is already invertibility. — Axler §3.D, Theorem 3.69, p. 87 The argument is a problem in this unit's set, and it is the first sign that norms and spectra constrain each other — the thread Axler's Chapter 7 picks up and the reason spectral methods behave well on the symmetric operators la-15 studies.

Square first. Almost every proof about norms in this unit begins by squaring, because ‖v‖² = ⟨v, v⟩ turns a square root into an inner product that the axioms can be applied to. Axler's proof of ‖λv‖ = |λ|‖v‖ is the model: compute ‖λv‖² = λ conj(λ)⟨v, v⟩ = |λ|²‖v‖², then take square roots at the very end. Reaching for the square root first is the reliable way to get stuck. — Axler §6.A, p. 169
Definition 6.11 and Theorem 6.12 (orthogonal). Vectors u, v are orthogonal if ⟨u, v⟩ = 0. The vector 0 is orthogonal to every vector, and is the only vector orthogonal to itself. — Axler §6.A, Definition 6.11, p. 169, and Theorem 6.12, p. 170

The order does not matter, since ⟨u, v⟩ = 0 exactly when ⟨v, u⟩ = 0 by conjugate symmetry. In R² this coincides with perpendicularity in the sense of plane geometry, because ⟨u, v⟩ = ‖u‖‖v‖cos θ for the angle θ between the arrows — a fact the problem sets treat as an exercise rather than a definition, since the whole point is that orthogonality is defined algebraically and then turns out to be perpendicularity. — Axler §6.A, p. 169

Theorem 6.13 (Pythagorean Theorem). If u and v are orthogonal, then ‖u + v‖² = ‖u‖² + ‖v‖². — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.13, p. 170
Why it is true — three lines, 2,500 years later. ‖u + v‖² = ⟨u + v, u + v⟩ = ⟨u, u⟩ + ⟨u, v⟩ + ⟨v, u⟩ + ⟨v, v⟩, and the two middle terms vanish by orthogonality, leaving ‖u‖² + ‖v‖².

The proof also shows exactly what the conclusion is equivalent to: ⟨u, v⟩ + ⟨v, u⟩ = 2Re⟨u, v⟩ must be 0. Over R that is orthogonality, so the converse holds; over C it does not, since a purely imaginary ⟨u, v⟩ has zero real part without being zero. Noticing which half of a proof is reversible costs nothing and is where the converse comes from. — Axler §6.A, p. 170

Theorem 6.14 (orthogonal decomposition). Suppose u, v ∈ V with v ≠ 0. Put c = ⟨u, v⟩/‖v‖² and w = u − cv. Then ⟨w, v⟩ = 0 and u = cv + w. — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.14, p. 171
Worked example — where the formula comes from (fully worked). The scalar c is not guessed; it is solved for.

Write u = cv + (u − cv) for an unknown c. We want the second piece orthogonal to v, that is 0 = ⟨u − cv, v⟩ = ⟨u, v⟩ − c‖v‖², using additivity and homogeneity in the first slot. Rearranging, c = ⟨u, v⟩/‖v‖² — and the division is legal precisely because v ≠ 0 makes ‖v‖ ≠ 0, by definiteness. — Axler §6.A, p. 171

Geometrically cv is the shadow of u along v and w is what is left over. This is the whole of orthogonal projection in one line, and la-14 will run it repeatedly to build orthonormal bases.

Canvas: the orthogonal decomposition

v is fixed (amber); u (blue) sweeps around. The green vector is cv, the shadow of u along v, and the dashed vector from its tip to u is w — always at a right angle to v, whatever u does.

Self-check 2. Over C, does ‖u + v‖² = ‖u‖² + ‖v‖² imply that u and v are orthogonal?

The computation shows the identity holds exactly when ⟨u, v⟩ + ⟨v, u⟩ = 2Re⟨u, v⟩ vanishes. Over R that is orthogonality; over C an inner product like i has zero real part and is not zero. Theorem 6.13 is stated as an implication, not an equivalence, for exactly this reason.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Cauchy–Schwarz, and which norms are geometric

Theorem 6.15 (Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality). For u, v ∈ V, |⟨u, v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖, with equality if and only if one of u, v is a scalar multiple of the other. — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.15, p. 172
Why it is true — Pythagoras applied to the orthogonal decomposition. If v = 0 both sides are 0. Otherwise decompose u = cv + w as in Theorem 6.14, with c = ⟨u, v⟩/‖v‖² and w orthogonal to v. Pythagoras gives

‖u‖² = |c|²‖v‖² + ‖w‖² = |⟨u, v⟩|²/‖v‖² + ‖w‖² ≥ |⟨u, v⟩|²/‖v‖².

Multiply by ‖v‖² and take square roots. The only inequality used is ‖w‖² ≥ 0, so equality holds exactly when w = 0 — which by Theorem 6.14 says u = cv, a scalar multiple. The phrasing "one of u, v is a multiple of the other" rather than "u is a multiple of v" is what covers v = 0. — Axler §6.A, p. 172

Two familiar inequalities are instances. On Rⁿ: |x₁y₁ + ⋯ + xnyn|² ≤ (x₁² + ⋯ + xn²)(y₁² + ⋯ + yn²). On continuous functions: |∫−11 fg|² ≤ (∫−11 f²)(∫−11 g²). One theorem, proved once, in both settings. — Axler §6.A, Example 6.17, p. 172

Theorem 6.18 and Theorem 6.22 (triangle and parallelogram). ‖u + v‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖v‖, with equality if and only if one of u, v is a nonnegative multiple of the other. And ‖u + v‖² + ‖u − v‖² = 2(‖u‖² + ‖v‖²). — Axler §6.A, Theorem 6.18, p. 173, and Theorem 6.22, p. 174

The triangle inequality's proof squares, expands, replaces 2Re⟨u, v⟩ by 2|⟨u, v⟩| and then by 2‖u‖‖v‖ using Cauchy–Schwarz, and recognises the result as (‖u‖ + ‖v‖)². Two inequalities are used, so equality requires both to be equalities, which is where the nonnegative in the equality condition comes from. The parallelogram equality needs no inequality at all: expand both squares and the cross terms cancel. — Axler §6.A, p. 173

Resolving the prediction gate

Verdict: no — and the parallelogram equality is exactly the test. Define ‖(x, y)‖ = |x| + |y| on R². This is a norm: it vanishes only at the origin, scales correctly, and satisfies the triangle inequality one coordinate at a time.

Take u = (1, 0) and v = (0, 1). Then u + v = (1, 1) and u − v = (1, −1), so ‖u + v‖² + ‖u − v‖² = 2² + 2² = 8, while 2(‖u‖² + ‖v‖²) = 2(1 + 1) = 4. The parallelogram equality fails, so by Theorem 6.22 this norm cannot come from any inner product.

The converse is also true — a norm satisfying the parallelogram equality does come from an inner product, which Axler sets as an exercise — so Theorem 6.22 is not merely a consequence of having an inner product but a complete characterisation of which norms are geometric in the sense of this unit. — cf. Axler §6.A, Exercise 21, p. 177

What is being ruled out is worth naming. Under ‖(x, y)‖ = |x| + |y| there is a perfectly good notion of distance — it is the one a taxi driving on a grid experiences — and no notion of angle at all, because there is no inner product to take. Distance alone is weaker than geometry.

Self-check 3. ‖u‖ = 3, ‖v‖ = 4 and ⟨u, v⟩ = 12. What follows?

|⟨u, v⟩| = 12 = 3 · 4 = ‖u‖‖v‖, so Cauchy–Schwarz holds with equality and one vector is a scalar multiple of the other. The inner product being positive and equal to ‖u‖‖v‖ makes the multiple nonnegative, by the equality condition in Theorem 6.18. Far from consistent with anything, these numbers pin the configuration down completely.

Self-check 4. Why does the equality case in Cauchy–Schwarz say "one of u, v is a scalar multiple of the other" rather than "u is a scalar multiple of v"?

If v = 0 then equality holds trivially, and v = 0·u is a multiple of u — but a nonzero u is not any multiple of 0. The disjunctive phrasing is doing real work at that one degenerate point. The inequality's symmetry is true and is not the reason.

Self-check 5. A proof begins "‖u + v‖ = √⟨u + v, u + v⟩ = √(…)" and expands under the square root. What is the better opening move?

Squaring converts a norm into an inner product, which the axioms can act on directly; square roots obstruct every algebraic step until they are removed. Axler's proofs of Theorem 6.10(b), Theorem 6.13, Theorem 6.15 and Theorem 6.18 all follow this shape. The third option assumes what is not given, and the second discards information before the computation has begun.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose V is a real inner product space. (a) Show that ⟨u + v, u − v⟩ = ‖u‖² − ‖v‖² for all u, v ∈ V. (b) Deduce that if ‖u‖ = ‖v‖ then u + v is orthogonal to u − v. (c) Use (b) to show that the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular. (Say where realness is used — the computation in (a) is not valid over C.) — cf. Axler §6.A, Exercise 4, p. 175

Reveal model answer

(a) Expanding with additivity in both slots,
⟨u + v, u − v⟩ = ⟨u, u⟩ − ⟨u, v⟩ + ⟨v, u⟩ − ⟨v, v⟩ = ‖u‖² − ‖v‖² + (⟨v, u⟩ − ⟨u, v⟩).
In a real inner product space ⟨u, v⟩ = ⟨v, u⟩, so the bracket vanishes. That is the one and only place realness is used, and over C the bracket is ⟨v, u⟩ − conj⟨v, u⟩ = 2i Im⟨v, u⟩, which need not be 0.

(b) If ‖u‖ = ‖v‖ then the right-hand side of (a) is 0, so ⟨u + v, u − v⟩ = 0, which is orthogonality by Definition 6.11.

(c) Place a rhombus with one corner at the origin and let u, v be the two edge vectors from that corner. A rhombus is exactly a parallelogram with ‖u‖ = ‖v‖. Its diagonals are u + v (corner to opposite corner) and u − v (between the other two corners), so by (b) they are orthogonal — and in R² orthogonal means perpendicular. ∎

Part (c) is the whole method of this chapter in miniature: a statement of plane geometry is restated in vectors, becomes a one-line inner-product identity, and the geometry is recovered at the end. Note that the argument never uses that the rhombus is planar — the same proof shows the diagonals of a rhombus in Rⁿ are perpendicular, which is not something the classical proof gives for free.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. An inner product (Definition 6.3) is one extra operation subject to five conditions, linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second (Theorem 6.7). Vector spaces have no geometry until one is chosen, and a space admits many.
  2. It generates everything geometric. The norm ‖v‖ = √⟨v, v⟩ (Definition 6.8), orthogonality ⟨u, v⟩ = 0 (Definition 6.11), Pythagoras in three lines (Theorem 6.13), and the orthogonal decomposition u = cv + w with c solved for rather than guessed (Theorem 6.14).
  3. Pythagoras applied to that decomposition proves Cauchy–Schwarz (Theorem 6.15), which proves the triangle inequality (Theorem 6.18). The parallelogram equality (Theorem 6.22) needs no inequality and is more than a consequence: it characterises exactly which norms come from an inner product, and the taxicab norm on R² fails it.
