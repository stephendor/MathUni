Orthonormal bases and Gram-Schmidt (la-14)

Straightening a skewed coordinate system, one vector at a time.

la-04 said every finite-dimensional space has a basis and that all bases are equally legitimate. With an inner product in hand that stops being true: some bases are enormously better than others. In an orthonormal basis the coefficients of a vector are not solved for — they are read off, as aj = ⟨v, ej⟩ — and the norm of a vector is the root-sum-of-squares of those coefficients, exactly as in Rⁿ.

The Gram–Schmidt procedure is what makes this practical: it takes any independent list and straightens it into an orthonormal one with the same spans all the way up. Everything else in the unit falls out of it — existence of orthonormal bases, extension of orthonormal lists, Schur's theorem, and the Riesz representation theorem, which says the inner product already contains every linear functional there is. It is also the machinery PCA runs on, which is where a TDA pipeline usually starts.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Fix a vector u in an inner product space. The map v ↦ ⟨v, u⟩ is a linear functional — la-13 established that. Now consider a specific functional on 𝒫₂(R) with ⟨p, q⟩ = ∫−11 pq: define φ(p) = ∫−11 p(t)cos(πt) dt. The obvious candidate for u would be cos(πt) — which is not a polynomial, so it is not in the space. Do you predict that some u ∈ 𝒫₂(R) nevertheless satisfies φ(p) = ⟨p, u⟩ for every p? Commit before Segment 3.

Segment 1 · ~20 min

Orthonormal lists and bases

Definition 6.23 (orthonormal). A list e₁, …, em is orthonormal if each vector has norm 1 and each is orthogonal to all the others — that is, ⟨ej, ek⟩ is 1 when j = k and 0 otherwise. — Axler §6.B, Definition 6.23, p. 180

The standard basis of Fⁿ is the model, but orthonormal lists are common and easy to make: (1/√3, 1/√3, 1/√3), (−1/√2, 1/√2, 0) is one in F³, and a third vector (1/√6, 1/√6, −2/√6) extends it. — Axler §6.B, Example 6.24, p. 180

Theorems 6.25 and 6.26. If e₁, …, em is orthonormal then ‖a₁e₁ + ⋯ + amem‖² = |a₁|² + ⋯ + |am|² for all scalars. Consequently every orthonormal list is linearly independent. — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.25 and Theorem 6.26, p. 180
Why they are true, and how cheaply. The first is repeated Pythagoras (la-13, Theorem 6.13): the terms ajej are pairwise orthogonal, so their norms square and add, and ‖ajej‖ = |aj|‖ej‖ = |aj|.

Independence is then a corollary rather than a separate argument. If a₁e₁ + ⋯ + amem = 0, the identity gives |a₁|² + ⋯ + |am|² = 0, and a sum of nonnegative reals vanishes only when every term does. Compare the effort with la-03's independence proofs, which had to solve systems: here the geometry does it. — Axler §6.B, p. 181

Definition 6.27 and Theorem 6.28 (orthonormal basis). An orthonormal basis of V is an orthonormal list that is also a basis. Every orthonormal list of length dim V is an orthonormal basis. — Axler §6.B, Definition 6.27 and Theorem 6.28, p. 181

Theorem 6.28 is la-04's Theorem 2.39 with independence supplied free by Theorem 6.26 — so checking that a list of the right length is an orthonormal basis reduces to computing inner products, with no spanning check and no independence check. — Axler §2.C, Theorem 2.39, p. 45

Worked example — certifying an orthonormal basis of F⁴ (fully worked). Consider (½, ½, ½, ½), (½, ½, −½, −½), (½, −½, −½, ½), (−½, ½, −½, ½).

Each has norm √(¼ + ¼ + ¼ + ¼) = 1. For orthogonality take the first two: ¼ + ¼ − ¼ − ¼ = 0, and the same computation on each of the remaining five pairs gives 0. So the list is orthonormal, it has length 4 = dim F⁴, and Theorem 6.28 makes it an orthonormal basis without a further line of work. — Axler §6.B, Example 6.29, p. 181

Count what was avoided: proving this list spans F⁴ directly would mean solving a four-by-four system. Six inner products replaced it.

Theorem 6.30 (coefficients are read off). If e₁, …, en is an orthonormal basis of V and v ∈ V, then v = ⟨v, e₁⟩e₁ + ⋯ + ⟨v, en⟩en and ‖v‖² = |⟨v, e₁⟩|² + ⋯ + |⟨v, en⟩|². — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.30, p. 182
This is why orthonormal bases matter. For an arbitrary basis, finding the coefficients of v means solving a linear system — la-04's Theorem 2.29 guarantees a unique answer and offers no way to compute it. For an orthonormal basis each coefficient is a single inner product, computed independently of all the others. The proof is one line: expand v = Σakek, take the inner product of both sides with ej, and every term but the j-th vanishes, leaving ⟨v, ej⟩ = aj. — Axler §6.B, p. 182

Self-check 1. A list of 5 orthonormal vectors is given in a 5-dimensional inner product space. What still needs checking before calling it an orthonormal basis?

Orthonormality gives independence (Theorem 6.26), and an independent list of length dim V is a basis (la-04, Theorem 2.39). Both of the other options have already been discharged by the orthonormality hypothesis, which is exactly the labour-saving Theorem 6.28 is there to record.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Gram–Schmidt, and what it delivers

Theorem 6.31 (Gram–Schmidt Procedure). Suppose v₁, …, vm is linearly independent. Set e₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖, and for j ≥ 2 define ej to be vj − ⟨vj, e₁⟩e₁ − ⋯ − ⟨vj, ej−1⟩ej−1, divided by its own norm. Then e₁, …, em is orthonormal and span(v₁, …, vj) = span(e₁, …, ej) for every j. — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.31, p. 183
What the formula is doing, and the one thing that could go wrong. Each step is la-13's orthogonal decomposition run against everything already built: subtract from vj its shadow along each earlier ek, leaving a remainder orthogonal to all of them, then normalise. Checking orthogonality is immediate — ⟨ej, ek⟩ has numerator ⟨vj, ek⟩ − ⟨vj, ek⟩ = 0, since the other subtracted terms are orthogonal to ek.

The step that needs the hypothesis is the division. If vj lay in span(v₁, …, vj−1) = span(e₁, …, ej−1), the numerator would be 0 and the procedure would divide by zero. Independence of the original list is precisely what rules that out — and the problem set asks what happens when it is dropped. — Axler §6.B, p. 183

The span claim then follows by induction: the formula puts vj into span(e₁, …, ej), giving one inclusion, and both lists are independent of length j, so la-04's dimension count forces equality.

Canvas: one step of Gram–Schmidt

v₁ (blue) is normalised to e₁ (green). v₂ (amber) sweeps around; the dashed line is its shadow along e₁, and the red vector is what is left after that shadow is subtracted — always perpendicular to e₁, and normalised to give e₂.

Worked example — orthonormalising 1, x, x² (fully worked). On 𝒫₂(R) with ⟨p, q⟩ = ∫−11 pq, apply Gram–Schmidt to the standard basis 1, x, x².

‖1‖² = ∫−11 1 dx = 2, so e₁ = 1/√2. Then ⟨x, e₁⟩ = 0 because x is odd and the interval is symmetric — worth noticing, since it means the second step subtracts nothing and e₂ is just x normalised. Continuing produces an orthonormal basis whose members are the Legendre polynomials up to scaling. — Axler §6.B, Example 6.33, p. 184

The general lesson: an inner product defined by integration converts a symmetry of the interval into orthogonality for free, and the procedure is much lighter than the formula suggests whenever such a symmetry is present. The problem set runs the same computation on [0, 1], where no symmetry helps and every term must be computed.

Theorems 6.34, 6.35, 6.37 and 6.38 (four corollaries). Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. Every orthonormal list in one extends to an orthonormal basis. If T has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some basis, it has one with respect to some orthonormal basis. Hence (Schur's Theorem) every operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis. — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.34 and Theorem 6.35, p. 185, and Theorem 6.37 and Theorem 6.38, p. 186
Each is Gram–Schmidt applied to something already in hand. For 6.34: take any basis and orthonormalise it; the result has length dim V, so Theorem 6.28 applies. For 6.35: an orthonormal list is independent, so extend it to a basis (la-04's Theorem 2.33) and orthonormalise — and the procedure leaves the first vectors unchanged, because a vector already orthonormal to its predecessors has nothing subtracted and unit norm to begin with. That fixed-point property is what makes it an extension rather than a replacement.

For 6.37: la-12's Theorem 5.26 says an upper-triangular matrix means span(v₁, …, vj) is invariant for each j; Gram–Schmidt preserves those spans exactly, so the same invariance holds for the e's and Theorem 5.26 reads backwards. Schur's Theorem is then 6.37 fed by la-12's Theorem 5.27, which supplies the non-orthonormal basis over C. — Axler §6.B, pp. 185–186

Self-check 2. Gram–Schmidt is applied to a list whose third vector lies in the span of the first two. What happens?

The numerator v₃ − ⟨v₃, e₁⟩e₁ − ⟨v₃, e₂⟩e₂ is exactly v₃ minus its component in span(e₁, e₂) = span(v₁, v₂), which is 0 — and the formula then divides by ‖0‖ = 0. Nothing skips it: the procedure as stated has no case for this, which is precisely why Theorem 6.31 assumes independence.

Self-check 3. Why does Theorem 6.35 produce an extension rather than a different orthonormal basis?

Applied to e₁, …, em, v₁, …, vn, the formula subtracts nothing from each ej (it is already orthogonal to its predecessors) and divides by 1 (it already has norm 1), so the first m outputs are the inputs. The first option gives an extension to a basis, not an orthonormal one — that is la-04's Theorem 2.33, and it is only half the argument.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Riesz: every functional is an inner product

Definition 6.39 (linear functional, again). A linear functional on V is a linear map from V to F. — Axler §6.B, Definition 6.39, p. 187

la-10 studied these in the absence of an inner product, where the dual space V′ was isomorphic to V only after choosing a basis. An inner product changes that, and the change is the subject of this segment. — Axler §3.F, Definition 3.92, p. 101

Resolving the prediction gate

Theorem 6.42 (Riesz Representation Theorem). Suppose V is finite-dimensional and φ is a linear functional on V. Then there is a unique u ∈ V with φ(v) = ⟨v, u⟩ for every v ∈ V. — Axler §6.B, Theorem 6.42, p. 188
Verdict: yes — and Theorem 6.30 hands you the vector. The gate's functional φ(p) = ∫−11 p(t)cos(πt) dt on 𝒫₂(R) does have a representing polynomial, even though cos(πt) itself is not one.

Why one exists. Take an orthonormal basis e₁, …, en. For any v, Theorem 6.30 gives v = Σ⟨v, ej⟩ej, so linearity gives φ(v) = Σ⟨v, ej⟩φ(ej). Pulling each φ(ej) into the second slot of the inner product costs a conjugate (la-13, Theorem 6.7), so φ(v) = ⟨v, conj(φ(e₁))e₁ + ⋯ + conj(φ(en))en⟩. That bracketed vector is u. — Axler §6.B, p. 188

Why only one. If ⟨v, u₁⟩ = ⟨v, u₂⟩ for every v, then ⟨v, u₁ − u₂⟩ = 0 for every v; taking v = u₁ − u₂ gives ‖u₁ − u₂‖² = 0, so u₁ = u₂ by la-13's definiteness. The trick of testing a "for all v" statement at the one vector that makes it collapse is worth keeping.

For the gate's functional the formula produces u(x) = −(45/2π²)(x² − ⅓), after a little calculus. Note what has happened: the representing vector is a polynomial that looks nothing like cos(πt), and yet integrating any p ∈ 𝒫₂(R) against either gives the same number. The inner product cannot tell them apart within the subspace, and Riesz says the subspace's own vector always exists. — Axler §6.B, Example 6.41, p. 187

What Riesz says about la-10. la-10 found dim V′ = dim V and warned that the isomorphism V → V′ depended on a choice of basis. With an inner product the map u ↦ ⟨·, u⟩ is such an isomorphism without any choice — Theorem 6.42 says it is onto and its uniqueness clause says it is injective. An inner product is exactly the extra structure that makes a space and its dual identifiable, which is why the distinction between vectors and functionals is invisible in Rⁿ and unavoidable without a metric.

Self-check 4. The formula u = conj(φ(e₁))e₁ + ⋯ + conj(φ(en))en uses a chosen orthonormal basis. Does u depend on that choice?

The right-hand side looks basis-dependent, and the uniqueness half of the theorem proves it is not: any u representing φ is the only one, so every orthonormal basis must produce it. This is the same pattern as la-04's dimension — a construction that names a representative, proved afterwards not to depend on the choice.

Self-check 5. Where does the conjugate in conj(φ(ej)) come from?

la-13's Theorem 6.7(e) says ⟨u, λv⟩ = conj(λ)⟨u, v⟩, so writing φ(ej)⟨v, ej⟩ as ⟨v, conj(φ(ej))ej⟩ requires conjugating. Over R it is invisible because conjugation is the identity there; over C omitting it gives the wrong vector.

Guided proof practice

Write your own proof. Suppose e₁, …, em is an orthonormal list in V (not necessarily a basis) and v ∈ V. Prove that ‖v‖² = |⟨v, e₁⟩|² + ⋯ + |⟨v, em⟩|² if and only if v ∈ span(e₁, …, em). (Build the vector Theorem 6.30 would produce, subtract it from v, and show the remainder is orthogonal to everything in sight.) — cf. Axler §6.B, Exercise 2, p. 189

Reveal model answer

Proof. Put w = ⟨v, e₁⟩e₁ + ⋯ + ⟨v, em⟩em, which lies in span(e₁, …, em), and let r = v − w.

For each k, ⟨r, ek⟩ = ⟨v, ek⟩ − ⟨w, ek⟩ = ⟨v, ek⟩ − ⟨v, ek⟩ = 0, using orthonormality to evaluate ⟨w, ek⟩. So r is orthogonal to every ek, hence to every linear combination of them, hence to w.

By the Pythagorean Theorem (la-13, Theorem 6.13), ‖v‖² = ‖w + r‖² = ‖w‖² + ‖r‖², and Theorem 6.25 evaluates ‖w‖² = |⟨v, e₁⟩|² + ⋯ + |⟨v, em⟩|².

So the claimed identity holds if and only if ‖r‖² = 0, which by la-13's Theorem 6.10(a) holds if and only if r = 0, that is, if and only if v = w ∈ span(e₁, …, em). ∎

Two things fall out. Since ‖r‖² ≥ 0 always, the identity is an inequality in general — |⟨v, e₁⟩|² + ⋯ + |⟨v, em⟩|² ≤ ‖v‖², which is Bessel's inequality, obtained here for free. And w is the orthogonal projection of v onto the span, so the proof also shows that projection is the closest point: any other point of the span differs from v by more, since the difference picks up an extra orthogonal term.

Reveal the skeleton to check against
  1. An orthonormal list has ⟨ej, ek⟩ equal to 1 or 0 (Definition 6.23). Norms of combinations become sums of squared moduli (Theorem 6.25), which makes independence free (Theorem 6.26) and a list of the right length automatically a basis (Theorem 6.28). In such a basis the coefficients of a vector are read off as inner products (Theorem 6.30) rather than solved for.
  2. Gram–Schmidt (Theorem 6.31) converts any independent list into an orthonormal one with the same spans at every stage, by repeatedly subtracting shadows and normalising; independence is what stops it dividing by zero. Four results follow immediately: existence of orthonormal bases (6.34), extension of orthonormal lists (6.35, because the procedure fixes what is already orthonormal), upper-triangularity with respect to an orthonormal basis (6.37), and Schur's Theorem over C (6.38).
  3. The Riesz Representation Theorem (6.42): on a finite-dimensional inner product space every linear functional is ⟨·, u⟩ for exactly one u, constructed from any orthonormal basis and independent of the choice. An inner product is therefore precisely the structure that identifies a space with its dual without choosing a basis.
