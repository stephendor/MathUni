Vector spaces (la-01)

Polynomials, sequences, and functions are all secretly the same thing.

Here is a small conjuring trick. Take a polynomial like 3x² − x + 5, a sequence like (1, −1, 2, 0, 0, …), and a wobbling function f(t) = sin t. On the surface these look like three different species of mathematical object — one lives in algebra, one in number theory, one in analysis. But strip away the costumes and each is doing exactly the same two things: you can add two of them to get another of the same kind, and you can scale one of them by a number to get another of the same kind. Nothing else about "what they are" matters for this trick to work.

That is the entire idea of a vector space, and it is one of the most quietly radical ideas in undergraduate mathematics: forget what the objects are, and axiomatise only what you can do to them. Once you see it, arrows in the plane, lists of numbers, infinite sequences, and functions on an interval all become residents of the same house.

Prediction Gate: Commit to an answer before Segment 1. In any vector space, multiplying a vector v by the scalar 0 gives the zero vector: 0v = 0. Where does that fact come from?

A theorem. When you meet Definition 1.19 in Segment 2, check the list: nothing in it connects the scalar 0 to the vector 0. The link is forced anyway, by a two-line argument from the distributive law — which is the faded example you will complete. Notice how natural the wrong answer feels: the axiom list is shorter than your intuition expects, and knowing exactly which facts are assumed versus derived is most of what the problem set will test.

Why this matters for the mission: Homology groups over a field are vector spaces — this is their home.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Warm-up geometry: **R**n and **C**n

Before the abstract definition, Axler builds the familiar cases first — the plane and ordinary space — then generalises. We follow the same path, lightly, because the abstraction in Segment 2 will feel inevitable rather than arbitrary once you've seen where it comes from.

Lists, and the letter **F**

A list of length n is an ordered collection (x₁, …, xn), and two lists are equal exactly when they have the same length and the same entries in the same order — order and repetition matter, unlike in a set.

Definition 1.8. Suppose n is a nonnegative integer. A list of length n is an ordered collection of n elements separated by commas and surrounded by parentheses: (x₁, …, xn). Two lists are equal if and only if they have the same length and the same elements in the same order. — Axler §1.A, Definition 1.8, p. 5

Axler adopts a standing convention that saves a great deal of repetition: throughout the book, the letter F stands for either R or C. Any theorem proved "for F" is automatically proved twice — once for the reals, once for the complex numbers — for free.

Notation 1.6. Throughout this book, F stands for either R or C. — Axler §1.A, Notation 1.6, p. 4

R and C are the two running examples of a field — a set with two operations, addition and multiplication, satisfying the familiar commutative, associative, identity, inverse, and distributive laws. Axler is explicit that most of the theory in Chapters 1–3 works unchanged for any field, but the book only ever needs these two, so we won't chase the general definition further here — just enough field intuition to know that "**F**" is a placeholder you can always instantiate.

"A field is a set containing at least two distinct elements called 0 and 1, along with operations of addition and multiplication satisfying all the [usual arithmetic] properties." — Axler §1.A, "Digression on Fields", p. 10

**F**n: lists that add and scale

Fix a positive integer n. Generalising the plane R² and space R³, we get:

Definition 1.10. Fⁿ is the set of all lists of length n of elements of F: Fⁿ = {(x₁, …, xn) : xj ∈ F for j = 1, …, n}. — Axler §1.A, Definition 1.10, p. 5

Two operations make Fⁿ come alive. Addition adds corresponding coordinates; scalar multiplication multiplies every coordinate by the same number:

Definitions 1.12, 1.17. (x₁, …, xn) + (y₁, …, yn) = (x₁ + y₁, …, xn + yn). For λ ∈ F: λ(x₁, …, xn) = (λx₁, …, λxn). — Axler §1.A, Definitions 1.12 and 1.17, pp. 6, 10

Geometrically, in R²: adding x + y means sliding the arrow y so its tail sits at the head of x; the sum is the arrow from the origin to y's new head. Scaling λx stretches or shrinks the arrow by a factor of λ (and flips its direction if λ is negative). These two pictures — the parallelogram of addition, the stretching ray of scaling — are the entire geometric content of everything that follows. Once n ≥ 4 you lose the picture, Axler notes wryly, but not the algebra: "even if n is large, we can perform algebraic manipulations in Fⁿ as easily as in R² or R³." — Axler §1.A, p. 6

Worked example — commutativity of addition in Fⁿ (fully worked). Show that x + y = y + x for all x, y ∈ Fⁿ.

Proof. Suppose x = (x₁, …, xn) and y = (y₁, …, yn). Then

 x + y = (x₁, …, xn) + (y₁, …, yn) = (x₁ + y₁, …, xn + yn) = (y₁ + x₁, …, yn + xn) = y + x,

where the second and fourth equalities hold by the definition of addition in Fⁿ, and the third equality holds because addition of numbers in F is already commutative. ∎

— Axler §1.A, Theorem 1.13, p. 6

You try (solo). Find x ∈ R⁴ such that (4, −3, 1, 7) + 2x = (5, 9, −6, 8). (Isolate x the same way you would in ordinary algebra — coordinate by coordinate.) — Axler §1.A, Exercise 10, p. 11

Self-check 1. The lists (2, 5) and (5, 2) are…

Definition 1.8 is explicit: two lists are equal only if they have the same elements in the same order. Sets forget order; lists remember it. This is exactly why (2,5) ≠ (5,2) as elements of F², even though {2,5} = {5,2} as sets.

Self-check 2. Why does Axler introduce the symbol F rather than always writing R or C?

Notation 1.6 fixes F to mean "either R or C throughout this book". Proving a theorem for F proves it twice for free — once with F replaced by R, once with F replaced by C — without duplicating the argument.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The definition, and the zoo it unlocks

Everything about Fⁿ that we used above — commutativity, associativity, an additive identity, additive inverses, scaling behaving sensibly, distributive laws linking the two operations — can be listed as a checklist and imposed on any set, whether or not its elements look anything like lists of numbers. That checklist is the definition of a vector space.

Definition 1.19. A vector space is a set V along with an addition on V and a scalar multiplication on V such that the following properties hold: commutativity u + v = v + u for all u, v ∈ V; associativity (u + v) + w = u + (v + w) and (ab)v = a(bv) for all u, v, w ∈ V and a, b ∈ F; additive identity there exists 0 ∈ V such that v + 0 = v for all v ∈ V; additive inverse for every v ∈ V there exists w ∈ V with v + w = 0; multiplicative identity 1v = v for all v ∈ V; distributive properties a(u + v) = au + av and (a + b)v = av + bv for all a, b ∈ F and u, v ∈ V. — Axler §1.B, Definition 1.19, pp. 12–13

Notice what is not in this list: nothing about what the elements of V "are". Elements of a vector space are called vectors or points, purely by convention — the definition never asks them to be arrows, tuples, or anything geometric. — Axler §1.B, Definition 1.20, p. 13 A vector space over R is called a real vector space; over C, a complex vector space. — Axler §1.B, Definition 1.21, p. 13

The zoo

This is where the hook's conjuring trick gets cashed in. Fⁿ is a vector space over F, unsurprisingly — it motivated the definition. But so are these:

Example 1.22 — F∞. The set of all infinite sequences of elements of F: F∞ = {(x₁, x₂, …) : xj ∈ F for j = 1, 2, …}, with addition and scalar multiplication defined coordinatewise exactly as for Fⁿ. This is a vector space over F; its additive identity is the all-zero sequence. — Axler §1.B, Example 1.22, p. 13

Notation and Example 1.23–1.24 — FS. If S is a set, FS denotes the set of all functions from S to F. Addition is defined pointwise, (f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), and scalar multiplication pointwise, (λf)(x) = λf(x), for all x ∈ S. If S is nonempty, FS is a vector space over F; its additive identity is the zero function, and the additive inverse of f is (−f)(x) = −f(x). — Axler §1.B, Notation 1.23 and Example 1.24, pp. 13–14

Here is the punchline the hook promised. A list of length n is secretly a function from {1, 2, …, n} to F — the jth coordinate is just "the value at input j". A sequence is secretly a function from the positive integers to F. So Fⁿ = F{1,…,n} and F∞ = F{1,2,…} are both special cases of FS, not separate ideas. — Axler §1.B, p. 14 Polynomials, sequences, continuous functions on an interval — every one of them is some flavour of "function from a set into F", and the vector space structure doesn't care which flavour you picked.

Even the smallest possible vector space fits the definition: the one-point set {0}, with the only addition and scaling that could exist on it. — Axler §1.B, p. 13

Three free consequences

Once a set satisfies Definition 1.19, several facts follow immediately from the axioms alone — no extra structure needed. Two are proved fully below; the third is yours to finish.

Worked example — uniqueness of the additive identity (fully worked). A vector space has a unique additive identity.

Proof. Suppose 0 and 0′ are both additive identities for a vector space V. Then

 0′ = 0′ + 0 = 0 + 0′ = 0,

where the first equality holds because 0 is an additive identity, the second is commutativity, and the third holds because 0′ is an additive identity. Thus 0′ = 0, so V has only one additive identity. ∎

— Axler §1.B, Theorem 1.25, p. 15

Faded example — the number 0 times a vector (your turn to fill the gaps). Show that 0v = 0 for every v ∈ V (the 0 on the left is the scalar; the 0 on the right is the vector).

Proof. For v ∈ V, we have

Adding ⟨your step: name the vector you must add to both sides to cancel one copy of 0v⟩ to both sides of the equation above gives 0 = 0v, as desired. ∎

— Axler §1.B, Theorem 1.29, p. 16

You try (solo). Prove that (−1)v = −v for every v ∈ V. (Hint: show v + (−1)v = 0 using 1v = v, the distributive property (1 + (−1))v, and the fact 0v = 0 you just proved — then invoke uniqueness of the additive inverse.) — cf. Axler §1.B, Theorem 1.31, p. 17

Self-check 3. Which of these is not required by Definition 1.19?

The definition says nothing about what the elements of V "are" — only how addition and scalar multiplication must behave. That's precisely why function spaces, sequence spaces, and one-point sets all qualify.

Self-check 4. A list of length n, viewed inside the FS framework, is best understood as…

The jth coordinate of a list is just "the value at input j" — so Fⁿ is exactly F^{1,…,n}, a special case of F^S with S = {1,…,n}. Sequences are the same idea with S = the positive integers.

Self-check 5. True or false: {0} (the one-element set) is a vector space.

Axler notes this explicitly: the simplest vector space contains only one point. With the only possible addition (0+0=0) and scalar multiplication (λ·0=0), every axiom in Definition 1.19 holds trivially.

Guided Proof Practice

Prompt: The "You try" above told you to "invoke uniqueness of the additive inverse" — but Definition 1.19 only promises that at least one inverse exists for each vector, never that there is only one. Close that gap yourself: prove that every element of a vector space V has exactly one additive inverse. Hint: suppose w and w′ are both additive inverses of v, and build a chain of equalities from w to w′. Start by adding 0 to w, then replace that 0 with something more useful. Name the axiom justifying every single step — the whole exercise is noticing how few tools you actually have.

Click to reveal model answer

Model Answer: Let v ∈ V and suppose w, w′ ∈ V are both additive inverses of v, so v + w = 0 and v + w′ = 0. Then

 w = w + 0 = w + (v + w′) = (w + v) + w′ = (v + w) + w′ = 0 + w′ = w′ + 0 = w′.

Justifying each step in order: additive identity; v + w′ = 0 since w′ is an inverse of v; associativity of addition; commutativity; v + w = 0 since w is an inverse of v; commutativity again; additive identity. Hence w = w′, so the additive inverse of v is unique. ∎ — Axler §1.B, Theorem 1.26, p. 15

Why the two commutativity steps are not padding. Definition 1.19 states the additive identity axiom in only one order (v + 0 = v) and the inverse axiom in only one order (v + w = 0). Every time you need the other order you owe a commutativity citation. Being fussy about this now is what makes the later proofs — where commutativity is genuinely unavailable — readable.

With uniqueness in hand, the notation −v finally makes sense: it names the additive inverse, not merely an inverse. That is what licenses the "You try" above to conclude (−1)v = −v from v + (−1)v = 0.

Exit Ritual: Blank-Page Reconstruction

Shut the page and reconstruct the three load-bearing claims of this lesson from nothing:

  1. Definition 1.19 in full — the six families of requirement (commutativity, associativity, additive identity, additive inverse, multiplicative identity, distributivity) and, just as important, what the definition pointedly does not say about what the elements of V are.
  2. Why Fⁿ and F∞ are both special cases of FS — what set S is in each case, and what a list "really is" under that reading.
  3. The three facts the axioms hand you for free: the additive identity is unique, the additive inverse is unique, and 0v = 0 (scalar zero, vector zero). Recall which axiom opens each proof.
Check your mental skeleton

If you can state: (1) the six axiom families, with nothing anywhere about arrows or tuples; (2) Fⁿ = F{1,…,n} and F∞ = F{1,2,…}, a list being the function sending j to its jth coordinate; (3) 0′ = 0′ + 0 = 0 + 0′ = 0 for the identity, the w = w′ chain for the inverse, and 0v = (0 + 0)v = 0v + 0v then cancel — then you are ready for la-02, where subspaces ask you to check exactly this list against a candidate subset.
