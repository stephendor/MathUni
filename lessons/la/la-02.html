Subspaces, sums, direct sums (la-02)

When does a plane plus a line fill space? Direct sums as jigsaw pieces.

Take the xy-plane sitting inside R³, and the z-axis, a line through the origin poking straight through it. Add every point of the plane to every point of the line — all possible sums — and you get the whole of R³ back. Every point in space is some plane-point plus some line-point, and — this is the part that makes it a genuine jigsaw rather than a sloppy overlap — there is exactly one way to split any given point into its plane-piece and its line-piece. No ambiguity, no double-counting.

Contrast that with three flat pieces glued clumsily: if any two of your "puzzle pieces" already overlap in more than just the corner point, the decomposition stops being unique, and the jigsaw metaphor breaks. This lesson makes that difference precise. First we pin down exactly which subsets of a vector space deserve to be called subspaces — the legitimate jigsaw pieces — and then we pin down exactly when gluing them back together, via a sum, gives a direct sum: a reconstruction with no seams doubled and nothing wasted.

Why this matters for the mission: Cycles and boundaries sit inside chains as subspaces.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Subspaces: legitimate pieces of a vector space

Recall from la-01 that a vector space is a set with an addition and scalar multiplication satisfying Definition 1.19's eight properties. A subspace asks: what if a subset of an existing vector space is, on its own, also a vector space, using the same operations inherited from the bigger space?

Definition 1.32. A subset U of V is called a subspace of V if U is also a vector space (using the same addition and scalar multiplication as on V). — Axler §1.C, Definition 1.32, p. 18

Checking all eight vector-space axioms every time would be tedious — and unnecessary, because a subset of V already inherits commutativity, associativity, and the distributive laws for free (they hold on all of V, so they certainly hold on any subset). What can actually fail are just three things: does the subset contain the zero vector, is it closed under addition, and is it closed under scalar multiplication? That collapses the check to a three-item test.

Theorem 1.34 (subspace test). A subset U of V is a subspace of V if and only if U satisfies: additive identity 0 ∈ U; closed under addition u, w ∈ U implies u + w ∈ U; closed under scalar multiplication a ∈ F and u ∈ U implies au ∈ U. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.34, pp. 18–19

Axler flags a subtlety worth holding onto: the additive-identity condition could technically be replaced by "U is nonempty", since a nonempty U closed under scaling automatically contains 0 (take any u ∈ U and multiply by the scalar 0). — Axler §1.C, p. 18 But in practice, checking 0 ∈ U directly is almost always the fastest move, and it's usually the first thing that fails — so it earns its place as the first checklist item, not an afterthought.

The classic traps: examples and non-examples

The subspace test is easy to state and easy to misapply. The traps below are exactly the ones Cummings-style faded proofs are built to catch — read the non-examples as carefully as the examples.

Worked example — a genuine subspace (fully worked). Show that U = {(x₁, x₂, 0) : x₁, x₂ ∈ F} is a subspace of F³.

Proof. Additive identity: 0 = (0, 0, 0) has third coordinate 0, so 0 ∈ U. Closed under addition: if u = (a₁, a₂, 0) and w = (b₁, b₂, 0) are both in U, then u + w = (a₁ + b₁, a₂ + b₂, 0), which still has third coordinate 0, so u + w ∈ U. Closed under scalar multiplication: if λ ∈ F and u = (a₁, a₂, 0) ∈ U, then λu = (λa₁, λa₂, 0), still third coordinate 0, so λu ∈ U. All three conditions of Theorem 1.34 hold, so U is a subspace of F³. ∎

— Axler §1.C, Example 1.33, p. 18

Faded example — the classic offset trap (your turn to fill the gaps). Fix b ∈ F. Show that W = {(x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄) ∈ F⁴ : x₃ = 5x₄ + b} is a subspace of F⁴ if and only if b = 0.

Proof (the "if b = 0" direction). Suppose b = 0, so W = {(x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄) : x₃ = 5x₄}. Additive identity: for 0 = (0,0,0,0), check 0 = 5·0, so ⟨your step: state the conclusion for this condition⟩. Closed under addition: if (a₁,a₂,a₃,a₄) and (c₁,c₂,c₃,c₄) both satisfy the defining equation, so a₃ = 5a₄ and c₃ = 5c₄, then adding these two equations gives ⟨your step: write the equation the sum's third and fourth coordinates satisfy⟩, so the sum lies in W too. Closed under scalar multiplication: similarly, scaling a₃ = 5a₄ by λ gives ⟨your step: write the scaled equation⟩, so λ(a₁,a₂,a₃,a₄) ∈ W.

Proof (the "only if" direction — why b ≠ 0 fails). Suppose b ≠ 0. Then 0 = (0,0,0,0) would need to satisfy 0 = 5·0 + b, i.e. ⟨your step: what does this force b to equal, and why does that contradict b ≠ 0?⟩. So the additive identity condition fails, and by Theorem 1.34, W is not a subspace when b ≠ 0. ∎

— Axler §1.C, Example 1.35(a), p. 19

That offset trap is the single most common mistake: a set defined by a linear but inhomogeneous equation (one with a leftover constant, like "= b" instead of "= 0") almost never contains the zero vector unless the constant happens to be zero. Spot the "+b" or "= 4" in a defining equation, and check 0 first — it usually fails instantly.

(Hint: (c) is a trap of a different flavour — the zero vector survives, but one of the closure conditions doesn't. Try adding (1,0,0) and (0,1,0), both individually satisfying the equation.) — cf. Axler §1.C, Exercise 1, p. 24

Two more useful facts to hold onto before moving on: {0} alone is always a subspace (the smallest one), and V itself is always a subspace of V (the largest one). The empty set, meanwhile, is never a subspace of anything — a subspace must be a vector space, and every vector space contains at least one element, its additive identity. — Axler §1.C, p. 19

Self-check 1. A subset U of V contains 0, and is closed under addition, but is not closed under scalar multiplication. Is U a subspace of V?

Theorem 1.34 is an "if and only if" over all three conditions together. Failing closure under scalar multiplication alone is enough to disqualify U as a subspace, no matter how the other two conditions fare.

Self-check 2. {(x₁, x₂, x₃) ∈ F³ : x₁ + 2x₂ + 3x₃ = 4} fails to be a subspace of F³ because…

0 + 2·0 + 3·0 = 0 ≠ 4, so 0 ∉ the set — the additive-identity condition of Theorem 1.34 already fails, exactly the offset trap of Example 1.35(a) with b = 4 ≠ 0.

Self-check 3. Which of these is always true for any vector space V?

{0} is the smallest subspace and V is the largest — both always satisfy Theorem 1.34 trivially. The empty set fails because it doesn't even contain 0. And plenty of subsets contain 0 but fail closure (see the plane-minus-axes counterexamples in Axler's exercises).

Sums and direct sums: the jigsaw made precise

Subspaces are the pieces. Now we glue them back together. The sum of subspaces is the set of all ways of adding one element from each piece:

Definition 1.36. Suppose U₁, …, Um are subsets of V. The sum of U₁, …, Um, denoted U₁ + ⋯ + Um, is U₁ + ⋯ + Um = {u₁ + ⋯ + um : u₁ ∈ U₁, …, um ∈ Um}. — Axler §1.C, Definition 1.36, p. 20

Sums of subspaces play the role for vector spaces that unions play for sets: the sum U₁ + ⋯ + Um is provably the smallest subspace of V containing all of U₁, …, Um. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.39, pp. 20–21 Axler notes why we bother with sums rather than plain unions at all: "the union of subspaces is rarely a subspace" — union alone doesn't stay closed under addition, so sum is the operation that actually produces another vector space. — Axler §1.C, p. 20

But a sum can be generous to the point of redundancy — the same vector might be reachable as u₁ + ⋯ + um in more than one way. When it can't — when every vector in the sum has exactly one such decomposition — the sum earns a special name and a special symbol.

Definition 1.40. Suppose U₁, …, Um are subspaces of V. The sum U₁ + ⋯ + Um is called a direct sum if each element of U₁ + ⋯ + Um can be written in only one way as a sum u₁ + ⋯ + um, where each uj ∈ Uj. When it is, we write U₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Um. — Axler §1.C, Definition 1.40, p. 21

The ⊕ symbol — a plus sign wrapped in a circle — is a deliberate visual reminder that something extra beyond a mere sum is being promised: uniqueness of decomposition. — Axler §1.C, p. 22 This is precisely the jigsaw property from the hook: R³ = (xy-plane) ⊕ (z-axis) because every point splits into a plane-part and an axis-part in exactly one way.

Worked example — a genuine direct sum (fully worked). Let U = {(x, y, 0) ∈ F³ : x, y ∈ F} and W = {(0, 0, z) ∈ F³ : z ∈ F}. Show F³ = U ⊕ W.

Proof. Sum equals F³: any (x,y,z) ∈ F³ can be written (x,y,z) = (x,y,0) + (0,0,z), with the first summand in U and the second in W. So U + W = F³. Directness: by Theorem 1.44 it suffices to show 0 decomposes uniquely. Suppose 0 = u + w with u = (a,b,0) ∈ U and w = (0,0,c) ∈ W. Then (a,b,c) = (0,0,0), forcing a = b = c = 0, so u = w = 0 — the only decomposition of 0 is the trivial one. Hence F³ = U ⊕ W. ∎

— Axler §1.C, Example 1.41, p. 21

Checking directness via "0 decomposes uniquely" rather than checking every single vector is not a shortcut we invented for the example above — it's a theorem, and it's the tool that makes direct-sum verification tractable in general:

Theorem 1.44. Suppose U₁, …, Um are subspaces of V. Then U₁ + ⋯ + Um is a direct sum if and only if the only way to write 0 as u₁ + ⋯ + um, each uj ∈ Uj, is by taking every uj = 0. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.44, pp. 22–23

For exactly two subspaces this collapses further, to a test about intersection:

Theorem 1.45. Suppose U and W are subspaces of V. Then U + W is a direct sum if and only if U ∩ W = {0}. — Axler §1.C, Theorem 1.45, p. 23

Now the faded non-example — the case where the jigsaw pieces overlap and the sum, though it fills the space, is not direct. This is the exact classic trap: a sum that looks like a direct sum because it reconstructs the whole space, but fails uniqueness.

Faded example — a sum that is not direct (your turn to fill the gaps). Let U₁ = {(x,y,0)}, U₂ = {(0,0,z)}, U₃ = {(0,y,y)} (all inside F³, coordinates ranging over F). Show U₁ + U₂ + U₃ = F³ but the sum is not direct.

Solution. The sum is all of F³: any (x,y,z) = (x,y,0) + (0,0,z) + (0,0,0), with the pieces in U₁, U₂, U₃ respectively. Not direct: by Theorem 1.44 it suffices to exhibit two different ways to write 0 as u₁ + u₂ + u₃. One way is the trivial (0,0,0)+(0,0,0)+(0,0,0). For the other, try u₃ = (0,1,1) ∈ U₃. Then u₁ must supply ⟨your step: what vector in U₁ cancels the y-coordinate contributed by u₃, and what must its own z-coordinate be?⟩, and u₂ must supply ⟨your step: what vector in U₂ cancels the remaining z-coordinate?⟩. Check: u₁ + u₂ + u₃ = ⟨your step: add the three vectors and confirm the total is (0,0,0)⟩. Since 0 has two distinct decompositions — the trivial one and this one — Theorem 1.44 says the sum is not direct, even though it equals all of F³. ∎

— Axler §1.C, Example 1.43, pp. 21–22

The lesson embedded in that non-example: filling the whole space is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for a direct sum. Axler adds a sharper warning for three or more subspaces — pairwise intersections all equalling {0} is not enough either (exactly the U₁, U₂, U₃ above have Uᵢ ∩ Uⱼ = {0} for every pair, and yet the triple sum still isn't direct). Theorem 1.45's clean intersection test is a genuine special privilege of the two-subspace case, not a general pattern. — Axler §1.C, p. 24

You try (solo). Let U = {(x,x,y,y) ∈ F⁴ : x,y ∈ F}. Find a subspace W of F⁴ such that F⁴ = U ⊕ W. (Hint: U ties coordinates 1–2 together and coordinates 3–4 together; you need a W that supplies exactly the "independent wiggle room" U's constraints remove, and check U ∩ W = {0} via Theorem 1.45.) — cf. Axler §1.C, Exercise 20, p. 26

Self-check 4. Subspaces U and W of V satisfy U ∩ W = {0}. What can you conclude?

Theorem 1.45 says exactly this for two subspaces: the intersection being {0} is equivalent to directness. It says nothing about whether the sum fills all of V — that's a separate question. And two subspaces can never be fully disjoint since both always contain 0.

Self-check 5. To check whether U₁ + U₂ + U₃ (three subspaces) is a direct sum, which is the correct tool?

Axler's Example 1.43 is the cautionary tale here: three subspaces can be pairwise-{0}-intersecting and still fail to form a direct sum. Theorem 1.45's pairwise test is only valid for exactly two subspaces; for three or more, Theorem 1.44's "0 decomposes uniquely" test is the one that actually works.

Self-check 6. In Example 1.43, U₁+U₂+U₃ = F³ but the sum is not direct. What does this demonstrate?

The whole point of the jigsaw metaphor: reconstructing the full space is easy to arrange, but directness additionally demands that the reconstruction be unique. F³ = U₁ ⊕ U₂ ⊕ U₃ can be achieved with a better choice of pieces (e.g. Example 1.42's coordinate-slot subspaces) — it's this particular triple that fails.
