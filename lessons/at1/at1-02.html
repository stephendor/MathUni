CW complexes and operations on spaces (at1-02)

Build any space from cells, one dimension at a time — the data structure algebraic topology actually computes on.

Take a square of paper and glue its opposite edges: left to right, then top to bottom. You get a torus. Now look at what survived the gluing. All four corners became a single point. The four edges became two circles, because opposite pairs were identified. The interior of the square became one two-dimensional patch. A torus, described completely by the list: one point, two arcs, one patch — plus the instruction saying how the patch's boundary runs around the arcs.

That list is a finite object. You can type it into a computer, whereas you cannot type in a torus. Chapter 0's last big idea is that essentially every space you will meet admits such a list, and that the list is a legitimate definition of the space rather than a picture of it.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The house with two rooms from at1-01 is contractible: it has the homotopy type of a single point. Hatcher counts its cells and finds 29 zero-cells, 51 one-cells and 23 two-cells. A single point, described as a cell complex, has one cell. What is 29 − 51 + 23, and is the answer an accident of how the house happened to be cut up?

29 − 51 + 23 = 1, the same as the single cell of a point. And Hatcher says why it is not an accident: "the Euler characteristic of a cell complex depends only on its homotopy type, as we shall show in Theorem 2.44, so the fact that the house with two rooms has the homotopy type of a point implies that its Euler characteristic must be 1, no matter how it is represented as a cell complex" (p. 6). Notice the shape of that sentence — an arbitrary combinatorial count turning out to depend only on the topology. That is the whole programme of the next eight units, announced here in one line and proved in at1-08.

Why this matters for the mission: A CW structure turns a space into chain groups a persistence algorithm can chew: cells become generators.

What this unit's source does and does not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires, and it names two things chapter 0 does not contain.

There are no chain groups here. Hatcher builds the cells; the free abelian group on the n-cells — the cellular chain group whose generators the strip refers to — is constructed in §2.2 and taught in at1-08, and the boundary map that makes it a complex needs the degree theory of that section. What chapter 0 does supply is exactly the combinatorial input that construction consumes, and two visible shadows of it: the Euler characteristic as an alternating count of cells by dimension (p. 6), and the fact that Xn/Xn−1 is a wedge of one n-sphere per n-cell (p. 10). That second fact is the reason the cellular chain group is free on the n-cells, so the strip's "cells become generators" is genuinely prefigured in this unit — it is simply not proved here.

There is no persistence algorithm either, and no filtration. A persistence algorithm consumes a growing family of complexes, not one complex; that is tda1-01's filtration and tda1-02's matrix reduction. The word "chew" is doing a lot of work: the passage from a CW structure to something a machine reduces requires the boundary matrix of at1-08 and tda1-02, neither of which exists yet.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

The construction: skeleta, attaching maps, characteristic maps

Hatcher motivates it from surfaces. An orientable surface of genus g is built from a 4g-sided polygon by identifying pairs of edges; the 4g edges become 2g circles all meeting at one point, and the polygon's interior is a 2-cell attached to that wedge. "Thus the surface can be built up in stages: start with a point, attach 1-cells to this point, then attach a 2-cell." Generalise the recipe and you have the definition.

Cell complex (CW complex). Construct a space by the following procedure.

(1) Start with a discrete set X0, whose points are regarded as 0-cells.

(2) Inductively, form the n-skeleton Xn from Xn−1 by attaching n-cells enα via maps φα : Sn−1 → Xn−1. This means that Xn is the quotient space of the disjoint union Xn−1 ⊔α Dnα under the identifications x ∼ φα(x) for x ∈ ∂Dnα. Thus as a set, Xn = Xn−1 ⊔α enα, where each enα is an open n-disk.

(3) One can either stop at a finite stage, setting X = Xn, or continue indefinitely, setting X = ⋃n Xn. In the latter case X is given the weak topology: a set A ⊆ X is open (or closed) if and only if A ∩ Xn is open (or closed) in Xn for each n. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 5

Three clauses, and each is load-bearing. Clause (2) says an n-cell is glued on only along its boundary sphere, and the gluing map φα may be as wild as you like — not injective, not surjective, nothing. The cell itself, enα, is an open disk, so the cells partition X as a set even though their closures overlap badly. Clause (3)'s weak topology is what makes infinite-dimensional examples behave: a map out of X is continuous exactly when its restriction to every skeleton is.

If X = Xn for some n, X is finite-dimensional and the smallest such n is the dimension of X, the maximum dimension of cells of X (p. 6).

Characteristic map. Each cell enα in a cell complex X has a characteristic map Φα : Dnα → X which extends the attaching map φα and is a homeomorphism from the interior of Dnα onto enα. One takes Φα to be the composition Dnα ↪ Xn−1 ⊔α Dnα → Xn ↪ X, where the middle map is the quotient map defining Xn. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 7

Keep the two maps apart. The attaching map is defined on the boundary sphere and tells you the gluing; the characteristic map is defined on the whole disk and is the closest thing to a parametrisation of the cell. The characteristic map is essentially never injective on the boundary, and its image is the closure of the cell — a fact Problem 3 turns on.

Worked example: real projective space, one cell in each dimension

Claim. ℝPn has a cell structure e0 ∪ e1 ∪ ⋯ ∪ en with exactly one cell in each dimension i ≤ n.

Step 1 — three descriptions of the same space. ℝPn is defined as the space of all lines through the origin in ℝn+1, topologised as the quotient of ℝn+1 − {0} by v ∼ λv for λ ≠ 0. Each line is determined by a nonzero vector, unique up to scalar multiplication. Restricting to vectors of length 1, ℝPn is also the quotient Sn/(v ∼ −v): the sphere with antipodal points identified. Restricting once more, to a closed hemisphere, it is the quotient of a disk Dn with antipodal points of ∂Dn identified — because each antipodal pair on Sn has at least one representative in the hemisphere, and exactly one unless it lies on the equator.

Step 2 — recognise the induction. Take the third description. The equator ∂Dn = Sn−1 with antipodal points identified is, by the same definition one dimension down, exactly ℝPn−1. So ℝPn is the quotient of ℝPn−1 ⊔ Dn under x ∼ φ(x) for x ∈ ∂Dn, where φ : Sn−1 → ℝPn−1 is the quotient projection identifying antipodal points.

Step 3 — that is clause (2), verbatim. Compare with the definition: a single n-cell, attached to the previous skeleton by the map φ. So ℝPn is obtained from ℝPn−1 by attaching one n-cell, with attaching map the two-to-one covering projection Sn−1 → ℝPn−1.

Step 4 — induct. ℝP0 is a single point, one 0-cell. Adding one cell per dimension gives ℝPn = e0 ∪ e1 ∪ ⋯ ∪ en. A characteristic map for the cell ei is the quotient map Di → ℝPi ⊆ ℝPn identifying antipodal points of ∂Di.

Step 5 — read off the Euler characteristic. One cell in each dimension 0 through n means χ = 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ ± 1, an alternating sum of n+1 ones. So χ(ℝPn) = 1 when n is even and 0 when n is odd.

Step 6 — the infinite case comes free. Since ℝPn is obtained from ℝPn−1 by attaching an n-cell, the union ℝP∞ = ⋃n ℝPn is a cell complex with one cell in each dimension, by clause (3). It is the space of lines through the origin in ℝ∞ = ⋃n ℝn.

Faded example: complex projective space

ℂPn is the space of complex lines through the origin in ℂn+1, that is, 1-dimensional vector subspaces, topologised as the quotient of ℂn+1 − {0} by v ∼ λv for λ ≠ 0. Equivalently it is the quotient of the unit sphere S2n+1 ⊆ ℂn+1 by v ∼ λv for |λ| = 1.

Step 1 — find the disk. The vectors in S2n+1 whose last coordinate is real and non-negative are exactly those of the form (w, √(1 − |w|2)) ∈ ℂn × ℂ with |w| ≤ 1. These form the graph of w ↦ √(1 − |w|2), a disk D2n+ bounded by the sphere S2n−1 of vectors (w, 0) with |w| = 1.

Self-check 1. In the two-cell structure on Sn (Example 0.3), the n-cell is attached by the constant map Sn−1 → e0. What is its characteristic map?

Hatcher gives exactly this on p. 7: "in the canonical cell structure on Sn described in Example 0.3, a characteristic map for the n-cell is the quotient map Dn → Sn collapsing ∂Dn to a point." Note the last distractor is the tempting wrong answer: a characteristic map is never required to be injective on the boundary — only a homeomorphism from the interior onto the cell. Here it collapses the entire boundary to one point and is still perfectly good.

Self-check 2. Why does clause (3) specify the weak topology rather than, say, the topology of the union with no further comment?

Right. When X = Xn stops at a finite stage, clause (2)'s quotient topology already determines everything. When the process runs forever, ⋃n Xn is only a set, and clause (3) supplies the topology: A is open exactly when every A ∩ Xn is. The practical consequence, which Hatcher leans on in Proposition 0.16's proof, is that a map out of X is continuous if and only if its restriction to each skeleton is — which is what lets an infinite concatenation of homotopies, one per skeleton, be continuous at the limit.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Subcomplexes, and the operations cells survive

Subcomplex; CW pair. A subcomplex of a cell complex X is a closed subspace A ⊆ X that is a union of cells of X. Since A is closed, the characteristic map of each cell in A has image contained in A, and in particular the image of the attaching map of each cell in A is contained in A, so A is a cell complex in its own right. A pair (X, A) consisting of a cell complex X and a subcomplex A is called a CW pair. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 7

Two words are doing all the work: closed and union of cells. Drop "closed" and the argument in the definition collapses. Drop "union of cells" and you can take half of a cell, which is the failure mode Hatcher exhibits.

Each skeleton Xn is a subcomplex, and so are ℝPk ⊆ ℝPn and ℂPk ⊆ ℂPn for k ≤ n — in fact Hatcher notes these are the only subcomplexes of ℝPn and ℂPn, which the one-cell-per-dimension structure makes believable.

Now the instructive failure. There are natural inclusions S0 ⊆ S1 ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Sn, but these subspheres are not subcomplexes of Sn in its usual two-cell structure — there simply are not enough cells to build them from, since the only unions of cells available are the empty set, the 0-cell, the n-cell, and everything. The repair is to change the structure, not the subspace: regard each Sk as obtained from the equatorial Sk−1 by attaching two k-cells, the two components of Sk − Sk−1. Now every Sk is a subcomplex, and S∞ = ⋃n Sn becomes a cell complex too.

Hatcher's last caution here is worth stating separately, because it contradicts the mental picture most people form: in the examples so far the closure of each cell is a subcomplex, and "most naturally arising cell structures have this property, but it need not hold in general". Attach a 2-cell to S1 by a map S1 → S1 whose image is a nontrivial subarc, and the closure of the 2-cell contains only part of the 1-cell — so it is not a union of cells (p. 8).

The operations

Hatcher's summary of why this definition earns its keep: cell complexes have "a very nice mixture of rigidity and flexibility, with enough rigidity to allow many arguments to proceed in a combinatorial cell-by-cell fashion and enough flexibility to allow many natural constructions to be performed on them" (p. 8). Here are the constructions, each with its effect on cells.

OperationDefinitionCells of the result
Product X × Y—products emα × enβ
Quotient X/A, (X,A) a CW pair—cells of X − A, plus one new 0-cell
Cone CX(X × I)/(X × {0})one new 0-cell; dimension rises by one
Suspension SXX × I with each end collapsed to a pointtwo 0-cells; one (n+1)-cell per n-cell
Join X ∗ Yquotient of X × Y × I collapsing each endX, Y as subcomplexes, plus X × Y × (0,1)
Wedge X ∨ YX ⊔ Y with x0, y0 identifiedcells of both, sharing one 0-cell
Smash X ∧ YX × Y / X ∨ Ycells of the product, minus the wedge

Two of these need their fine print. For the product, the cell-complex topology on X × Y is sometimes strictly finer than the product topology, though the two coincide if either factor has finitely many cells or both have countably many. For the quotient, the attaching map of the cell of X/A corresponding to enα is the composite Sn−1 → Xn−1 → Xn−1/An−1. The join has a memorable special case: the join of n points is a convex polyhedron of dimension n − 1 called a simplex, and taking the n points to be the standard basis vectors of ℝn realises it as Δn−1 = {(t1, …, tn) ∈ ℝn : Σti = 1 and ti ≥ 0}. That is the object tda1-01 will filter.

Worked example: the quotient of consecutive skeleta

Claim. For any cell complex X, the quotient Xn/Xn−1 is a wedge sum ⋁α Snα of n-spheres, with one sphere for each n-cell of X.

Step 1 — list the cells of the quotient. (Xn, Xn−1) is a CW pair, so the quotient rule applies: the cells of Xn/Xn−1 are the cells of Xn − Xn−1, which are precisely the n-cells of X, together with one new 0-cell, the image of Xn−1.

Step 2 — find the attaching maps. For an n-cell attached by φα : Sn−1 → Xn−1, the corresponding cell of the quotient is attached by the composite Sn−1 → Xn−1 → Xn−1/Xn−1. The target is a single point, so the composite is constant — whatever φα was.

Step 3 — recognise each piece. A 0-cell with an n-cell attached by a constant map is exactly Example 0.3's description of Sn. So each n-cell of X contributes a sphere.

Step 4 — they meet only at the 0-cell. The n-cells are disjoint as open cells, and every attaching map lands in the single new 0-cell, so any two of these spheres intersect exactly there. That is the definition of the wedge sum. Hence Xn/Xn−1 = ⋁α Snα, one sphere per n-cell. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 10

Why this one matters most. Of everything in this segment, this is the fact at1-08 will consume. A wedge of k spheres in dimension n has, as you will prove there, homology a free abelian group of rank k in degree n. So the n-th cellular chain group turns out to be free on the n-cells — the "cells become generators" of this unit's mission strip. The generation happens here; the proof that it does happens in at1-08.

Self-check 3. S1 in its minimal structure has one 0-cell and one 1-cell. Which of these subspaces of S1 is a subcomplex?

A subcomplex must be both a union of cells and closed. The available unions are ∅, {e0}, {e1} and {e0, e1}, and of those, {e1} alone is not closed — its closure picks up the 0-cell. So three subcomplexes remain. A closed semicircle is closed but is not a union of cells: it contains part of the open 1-cell, not all of it. That is the same failure as Hatcher's example on p. 8, where the closure of a 2-cell meets only a subarc of the 1-cell and so fails to be a subcomplex.

Self-check 4. The suspension SX of a cell complex has two 0-cells and one (n+1)-cell for each n-cell of X. The reduced suspension ΣX collapses the segment {x0} × I. How do the two structures differ in cell count?

Hatcher states exactly this on p. 12: "In SX there are two 0-cells (the two suspension points) and an (n+1)-cell en × (0,1) for each n-cell en of X, whereas in ΣX there is a single 0-cell and an (n+1)-cell for each n-cell of X other than the 0-cell x0." Collapsing the segment {x0} × I merges the two suspension points into one and removes the 1-cell that segment contributed. The two spaces are homotopy equivalent — that is Example 0.10, and it is an instance of the collapsing criterion of Segment 3, the segment being a contractible subcomplex.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Two criteria: collapse a piece, or move the glue

Building spaces is only half the value of the definition. The other half is that CW structure makes homotopy equivalences constructible: Hatcher gives two criteria, each turning a contractible piece into a simplification.

The collapsing criterion. If (X, A) is a CW pair consisting of a CW complex X and a contractible subcomplex A, then the quotient map X → X/A is a homotopy equivalence. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 11

This is the special case of Proposition 0.17 (p. 15) in which the homotopy extension property comes from Proposition 0.16 (p. 15), which supplies it for every CW pair. Both were stated in at1-01; here is where they get used.

The attaching criterion. If (X1, A) is a CW pair and the two attaching maps f, g : A → X0 are homotopic, then X0 ⊔f X1 ≃ X0 ⊔g X1. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 13; proved as Proposition 0.18, p. 16

Here X0 ⊔f X1 is X0 with X1 attached along A ⊆ X1 via f : A → X0 — the quotient of X0 ⊔ X1 identifying each a ∈ A with f(a). When (X1, A) = (Dn, Sn−1) this is exactly attaching an n-cell, so the criterion says: homotopic attaching maps give homotopy equivalent results. Proposition 0.18 in fact proves the stronger statement, that the equivalence may be taken rel X0.

Hatcher's proof of Proposition 0.18 is short and worth knowing, because it is the mapping-cylinder trick from at1-01 in a new costume. Given a homotopy F : A × I → X0 from f to g, form X0 ⊔F (X1 × I). This contains both X0 ⊔f X1 and X0 ⊔g X1. A deformation retraction of X1 × I onto X1 × {0} ∪ A × I — which Proposition 0.16 provides — induces a deformation retraction of the big space onto X0 ⊔f X1, and symmetrically onto X0 ⊔g X1. Both restrict to the identity on X0. So the third space containing both as deformation retracts is exhibited outright — which is precisely the situation Corollary 0.21 says characterises homotopy equivalence.

The mapping cone

Attaching gives a second construction alongside the mapping cylinder. Closely related to Mf is the mapping cone Cf = Y ⊔f CX, where CX is the cone (X × I)/(X × {0}) attached to Y along X × {1} via (x,1) ∼ f(x). When X is a sphere Sn−1, Cf is exactly Y with an n-cell attached via f. Equivalently, Cf is the quotient Mf/X of the mapping cylinder with X = X × {0} collapsed (p. 13). Example 0.13 makes the connection precise: if (X, A) is a CW pair then X/A ≃ X ∪ CA, the mapping cone of the inclusion A ↪ X, because CA is a contractible subcomplex of X ∪ CA and X/A = (X ∪ CA)/CA.

Guided proof. Write it out before revealing the model answer.

Let X be the space obtained from S2 by attaching the two ends of an arc A to the north and south poles, and let B be an arc in S2 joining those two poles. Prove that S2/S0 and S1 ∨ S2 are homotopy equivalent, naming every result you use and saying where the homotopy extension property comes from.

Reveal the model answer

Setting up the CW structure. Take as 0-cells the two endpoints of A and B — that is, the two poles. Take as 1-cells the interiors of A and of B. Take as the single 2-cell the rest of S2, that is S2 minus B and its endpoints. This is a legitimate cell structure: the 2-cell is an open disk attached along its boundary to the 1-skeleton A ∪ B.

Both A and B are contractible subcomplexes. Each is a single 1-cell together with its two 0-cells, hence a closed union of cells — a subcomplex — and each is an arc, hence contractible.

Collapsing each. (X, A) and (X, B) are CW pairs, so by Proposition 0.16 each has the homotopy extension property. Proposition 0.17 then applies to each — homotopy extension property plus contractible subcomplex — giving that the quotient maps X → X/A and X → X/B are both homotopy equivalences.

Identifying the two quotients. Collapsing A pulls the two poles of S2 together and leaves nothing else changed, so X/A is S2 with two points identified, that is S2/S0. Collapsing B leaves the sphere alone as a space but closes the external arc A into a loop meeting the sphere in a single point, so X/B is S1 ∨ S2.

Conclusion. S2/S0 ≃ X and X ≃ S1 ∨ S2. Since homotopy equivalence is an equivalence relation (at1-01), and in particular symmetric and transitive, S2/S0 ≃ S1 ∨ S2. ∎

Remark. Hatcher calls this "a fact which may not be entirely obvious at first glance" (p. 11), and Example 0.14 generalises it: if (X, A) is a CW pair and A is contractible in X, then X/A ≃ X ∨ SA, giving Sn/Si ≃ Sn ∨ Si+1 for i < n. Setting i = 0 and n = 2 returns the result just proved.

Self-check 5. The attaching criterion requires (X1, A) to be a CW pair. In the proof sketched above, where exactly is that hypothesis consumed?

Forming the quotient needs no hypothesis; the homotopy F is given. The single place the CW condition is used is Proposition 0.16, whose conclusion is precisely that for a CW pair (X1, A) the subspace X1 × {0} ∪ A × I is a deformation retract of X1 × I. Everything else in the argument is formal. This is worth noticing generally: in both criteria the CW hypothesis enters only through Proposition 0.16, which is why Hatcher states Propositions 0.17 and 0.18 with the homotopy extension property rather than with CW pairs.

Blank page. Look away and reconstruct the argument skeleton: three load-bearing claims and why each forces the next.

Reveal the skeleton

1. A space can be specified by a finite list. Start with a discrete set of 0-cells; inductively attach n-cells along maps φα : Sn−1 → Xn−1, taking the quotient of Xn−1 ⊔ Dnα; stop, or continue with the weak topology. The attaching map is arbitrary, the cells are open disks partitioning X, and the characteristic map extends φα over the whole disk. Everything computable about the space will come out of this list.

2. The structure is stable under the constructions one actually performs. Subcomplexes — closed unions of cells — are cell complexes in their own right, and products, quotients, cones, suspensions, joins, wedges and smash products all inherit cell structures with predictable cells. The critical instance is Xn/Xn−1 = ⋁ Sn, one sphere per n-cell, because all attaching maps become constant in the quotient. That is the fact at1-08 turns into "the cellular chain group is free on the n-cells".

3. The structure makes homotopy equivalences constructible. Collapse a contractible subcomplex (Proposition 0.17), or replace an attaching map by a homotopic one (Proposition 0.18). Both criteria draw their CW hypothesis solely from Proposition 0.16, which gives every CW pair the homotopy extension property; and Proposition 0.18's proof exhibits a third space containing both results as deformation retracts, which is exactly the situation Corollary 0.21 identified with homotopy equivalence in at1-01.
