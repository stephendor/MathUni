The fundamental group (at1-03)

Loops you can't shrink detect holes you can't see — and they compose into a group.

Stand in a room with a pillar in the middle, holding one end of a rope whose other end is nailed to your feet. Walk a circuit round the pillar and back. Now try to reel the rope in without lifting it over the pillar or letting go of either end. You cannot. Walk two circuits and you can reel in even less. Walk one circuit clockwise and one anticlockwise and the whole thing comes free.

Circuits that cancel; circuits that accumulate; a circuit that does nothing. Adding, inverting, an identity — that is a group, and it was there in the room the whole time. at1-01 gave you homotopy of maps; this unit points it at loops and reads off the algebra. What makes the construction delicate is that the elements are not loops but classes of loops, and every group axiom has to be checked at the level of classes.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Let f, g, h be three paths that compose. The product path f · g traverses f on the first half of the time interval and g on the second. Is (f · g) · h equal to f · (g · h)?

They are genuinely different functions. In (f · g) · h the path f is traversed during [0, 1/4], g during [1/4, 1/2] and h during [1/2, 1]; in f · (g · h) the timings are [0, 1/2], [1/2, 3/4], [3/4, 1]. At time s = 1/3 the two maps are at different points. So concatenation is not associative on the nose, and that is not a technicality to be waved away — it is the reason π₁ is defined on homotopy classes rather than on loops. The two products differ by a reparametrisation of the time interval, and Hatcher's proof of Proposition 1.3 shows reparametrisation never changes a homotopy class. Every group axiom in this unit is bought the same way.

Why this matters for the mission: Pi_1 is the first algebraic invariant of shape; H_1 is its abelianisation, whose rank is the Betti-1 you will compute.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Its first clause is this unit's business. Its second and third are not, and one of them lands outside the whole module.

That H₁ is the abelianisation of π₁ is Hatcher's Theorem 2A.1, in §2.A, printed p. 166: regarding loops as singular 1-cycles gives a homomorphism h : π₁(X, x0) → H₁(X), and for path-connected X this h is surjective with kernel the commutator subgroup, so it induces an isomorphism from the abelianisation of π₁(X) onto H₁(X). Neither of this unit's sources contains it: Hatcher §1.1 has no homology, and Dexter §2.3 has none either. It is worth being exact about where it does live, because §2.A is not among the resources of any at1 unit — at1-07 reads §2.1, at1-08 and at1-09 read §2.2, at1-10 reads §2.3. So on the syllabus as written, the abelianisation theorem is asserted by this unit's mission strip and proved by no unit of the degree. That is a resource gap rather than a mathematical one, and it is recorded for the syllabus pass; this lesson simply does not claim the theorem.

That its rank is a Betti number you will compute is further off still. Ranks of homology groups are at1-08, and computing them from data is tda1-02's matrix reduction over 𝔽₂ — a different coefficient field, in which "rank" means dimension. Nothing here computes anything: as Hatcher says on p. 28, "It is not so easy to show that a space has a nontrivial fundamental group since one must somehow demonstrate the nonexistence of homotopies between certain loops." This unit builds the group and proves it is a group. The first space with a proven non-trivial π₁ arrives in at1-04, with Theorem 1.7.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Paths up to homotopy

Hatcher begins one notch more general than loops, because the extra generality costs nothing and the change-of-basepoint argument in Segment 3 needs it.

Path; homotopy of paths. By a path in a space X we mean a continuous map f : I → X, where I is the unit interval [0,1]. A homotopy of paths in X is a family ft : I → X, 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, such that

(1) the endpoints ft(0) = x0 and ft(1) = x1 are independent of t;

(2) the associated map F : I × I → X defined by F(s, t) = ft(s) is continuous.

When two paths f0 and f1 are connected in this way by a homotopy ft, they are said to be homotopic, written f0 ≃ f1. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 25

Clause (2) is at1-01's joint-continuity clause again, now on the square I × I. Clause (1) is new and is the whole point. Without it the notion is worthless: in Problem 1 you will show that if endpoints are free to move, every path in S1 is homotopic to a constant one, so nothing is detected. Nailing the endpoints is what turns homotopy into an instrument.

Two conventions worth keeping straight. The letter s is the position along the path; t is the time of the deformation. F(s, t) = ft(s). Hatcher's pictures always have s horizontal and t vertical.

Worked example: linear homotopies and convexity

Claim (Example 1.1). Any two paths f0, f1 in ℝn with the same endpoints x0, x1 are homotopic, via ft(s) = (1 − t)f0(s) + t f1(s).

Step 1 — what the formula does. During this homotopy each point f0(s) travels along the line segment to f1(s) at constant speed. The line through f0(s) and f1(s) is linearly parametrised as f0(s) + t[f1(s) − f0(s)] = (1 − t)f0(s) + t f1(s), with the segment covered by t running from 0 to 1.

Step 2 — clause (1), the endpoints. At s = 0 we get ft(0) = (1 − t)x0 + t x0 = x0, for every t; likewise ft(1) = x1. So the endpoints are independent of t, and each ft is a path from x0 to x1. Hatcher notes the mechanism: if f1(s) happens to equal f0(s), the segment degenerates to a point and ft(s) = f0(s) for all t — which is exactly what happens at s = 0 and s = 1.

Step 3 — clause (2), continuity. Continuity of ft as a map I × I → ℝn follows from continuity of f0 and f1, since the algebraic operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication in the formula are continuous.

Step 4 — the generalisation, and where it is used. For a convex subspace X ⊆ ℝn, all paths in X with given endpoints are homotopic — because if f0 and f1 lie in X then so does the whole homotopy ft, each ft(s) being a convex combination of two points of X. Convexity is doing precisely one job: keeping the homotopy inside the space. Take the same two paths in ℝ2 ∖ {0} and the formula may push a point straight through the puncture.

Consequence (Example 1.4). For a convex set X in ℝn with basepoint x0, π₁(X, x0) = 0, the trivial group: any two loops at x0 are homotopic by the same linear formula.

Proposition 1.2. The relation of homotopy on paths with fixed endpoints in any space is an equivalence relation. The equivalence class of a path f is denoted [f] and called the homotopy class of f. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 26

Proof. Reflexivity is evident, since f ≃ f by the constant homotopy ft = f. Symmetry is easy: if f0 ≃ f1 via ft, then f1 ≃ f0 via the inverse homotopy f1−t. For transitivity, if f0 ≃ f1 via ft and f1 = g0 with g0 ≃ g1 via gt, then f0 ≃ g1 via the homotopy ht equal to f2t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1/2 and g2t−1 for 1/2 ≤ t ≤ 1. The two definitions agree at t = 1/2 because f1 = g0.

Continuity of H(s,t) = ht(s) comes from the elementary fact — which Hatcher says will be used frequently without explicit mention — that a function defined on the union of two closed sets is continuous if it is continuous when restricted to each of the closed sets separately. Here H(s,t) = F(s, 2t) on I × [0, 1/2] and H(s,t) = G(s, 2t−1) on I × [1/2, 1]; both pieces are closed and H is continuous on each, so H is continuous on I × I. ∎

That gluing lemma is worth flagging, because it is the workhorse of the entire unit. Every construction below is defined piecewise on the time interval, and every continuity check is this same appeal.

Self-check 1. Why does the transitivity argument reparametrise the two homotopies to run on [0, 1/2] and [1/2, 1] rather than simply writing "do ft then gt"?

The reparametrisation is bookkeeping forced by the definition: the time parameter of a homotopy runs over [0,1], so running two homotopies one after the other requires rescaling each to half the interval. Continuity is then a separate matter, settled by the closed-sets gluing lemma — and it works precisely because f1 = g0 makes the two formulas agree on the overlap I × {1/2}. The same squeeze reappears immediately in the product path f · g, where it is applied to the position variable s instead of the time variable t.

Self-check 2. Example 1.4 shows π₁ of a convex set is trivial. Which step of the argument fails for the annulus {1 ≤ |x| ≤ 2} in ℝ2?

Steps 1 to 3 of the worked example never mention the ambient space and go through unchanged; only Step 4 uses convexity, and only for one purpose — to guarantee that each ft(s), being a convex combination of two points of X, lies in X. For the annulus that fails: the segment joining a point on the inner circle to the diametrically opposite point passes through the hole. Note carefully what this does and does not show. It shows the argument breaks, not that π₁ of the annulus is non-trivial — proving that needs the non-existence of every homotopy, which is at1-04's Theorem 1.7.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The group

Product path; loop; basepoint; π₁. Given paths f, g : I → X with f(1) = g(0), the composition or product path f · g traverses first f and then g:

(f · g)(s) = f(2s) for 0 ≤ s ≤ 1/2, and (f · g)(s) = g(2s − 1) for 1/2 ≤ s ≤ 1.

Paths f : I → X with f(0) = f(1) = x0 are called loops, and x0 is the basepoint. The set of all homotopy classes [f] of loops at x0 is denoted π₁(X, x0). — Hatcher §1.1, p. 26

The product respects homotopy classes: if f0 ≃ f1 and g0 ≃ g1 via ft, gt, and f0(1) = g0(0) so that f0 · g0 is defined, then ft · gt is defined for every t and provides a homotopy f0 · g0 ≃ f1 · g1. So [f][g] = [f · g] is unambiguous.

Proposition 1.3. π₁(X, x0) is a group with respect to the product [f][g] = [f · g]. This group is called the fundamental group of X at the basepoint x0. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 26

Restricting to a fixed basepoint is what makes the product of any two elements defined at all — for general paths, f · g exists only when f ends where g begins, so the collection of all paths is not a group but something looser. The proof has one preliminary and then three axioms.

Reparametrisation. A reparametrisation of a path f is a composition fφ where φ : I → I is any continuous map with φ(0) = 0 and φ(1) = 1. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 27

Lemma R (reparametrisation preserves the class). fφ ≃ f, for any reparametrisation.

Proof. Put φt(s) = (1 − t)φ(s) + ts, so φ0 = φ and φ1(s) = s. Since (1 − t)φ(s) + ts is a convex combination of φ(s) ∈ I and s ∈ I, it lies between them and hence lies in I — so the composition fφt is defined. It is jointly continuous, and φt(0) = 0, φt(1) = 1 for every t, so the endpoints do not move. Hence fφt is a homotopy of paths from fφ to f. ∎

This is Hatcher's preliminary step, stated here as a named lemma because both the associativity and the identity axioms are corollaries of it and the problem set uses it directly.

Worked example: associativity and the identity, from Lemma R alone

Associativity. Given f, g, h with f(1) = g(0) and g(1) = h(0), both (f · g) · h and f · (g · h) are defined. Tabulate when each path is traversed:

(f · g) · h :   f on [0, 1/4],   g on [1/4, 1/2],   h on [1/2, 1]
f · (g · h) :   f on [0, 1/2],   g on [1/2, 3/4],   h on [3/4, 1]

The two traverse the same three paths in the same order, differing only in how the time is apportioned. So f · (g · h) is a reparametrisation of (f · g) · h by the piecewise-linear φ that sends 1/2 ↦ 1/4 and 3/4 ↦ 1/2, linearly on each of [0, 1/2], [1/2, 3/4], [3/4, 1]:

φ(s) = s/2 on [0, 1/2];   φ(s) = s − 1/4 on [1/2, 3/4];   φ(s) = 2s − 1 on [3/4, 1].

Check the joins: at s = 1/2 the first two give 1/4 and 1/4; at s = 3/4 the last two give 1/2 and 1/2; and φ(0) = 0, φ(1) = 1. By Lemma R, (f · g) · h ≃ f · (g · h). Restricting to loops at x0, the product in π₁(X, x0) is associative. (Hatcher gives only the graph of φ; the formula is worked out here because the problem set asks for it.)

Identity. Let c be the constant path at f(1), c(s) = f(1) for all s. Then f · c traverses f at double speed on [0, 1/2] and stands still on [1/2, 1] — which is f reparametrised by φ(s) = min(2s, 1). That φ is continuous with φ(0) = 0, φ(1) = 1, so Lemma R gives f · c ≃ f. Similarly c · f ≃ f with c the constant path at f(0), using φ(s) = max(2s − 1, 0). Taking f to be a loop, the class of the constant path at x0 is a two-sided identity in π₁(X, x0).

Faded example: the inverse

Reparametrisation cannot produce inverses — a reparametrisation of f · f̄ still traverses f and comes back, and no φ turns that into a constant. Hatcher's construction retracts the path along itself instead.

For a path f from x0 to x1, the inverse path is f̄(s) = f(1 − s). Define ht = ft · gt, where ft equals f on [0, 1 − t] and is stationary at f(1 − t) on [1 − t, 1], and gt is the inverse path of ft.

Step 1 — a closed formula. ft(s) = f(min(s, 1 − t)). This is f composed with (s,t) ↦ min(s, 1 − t), continuous as a minimum of two continuous functions.

Step 2 — the ends. At t = 0, f0 = f and g0 = f̄, so h0 = f · f̄. At t = 1, f1 is the constant path c at x0. ⟨your step: so h1 = c · c̄ — why is that the constant path rather than a longer journey?⟩

Step 3 — the other side. ⟨your step: replacing f by f̄ gives f̄ · f ≃ c for c the constant path at x1. Why does the same argument apply verbatim?⟩

Step 4 — read off the axiom. ⟨your step: take f to be a loop at x0 and state what has been proved about [f̄]⟩

Visual: the inverse homotopy, both of Hatcher's pictures

Left, the retraction: at time t the journey runs out only as far as f(1 − t) and comes straight back, so at t = 1 nothing is left. Right, the same homotopy as a map on the square I × I, with s horizontal and t vertical. The bottom edge carries f · f̄. Below the "V", H is independent of t; above it, H is independent of s. Self-check 3. Why can reparametrisation prove associativity and the identity axiom but not the inverse axiom?

A reparametrisation is fφ: the same map f, read at different times. So fφ and f have exactly the same image in X. Associativity and the identity are both instances of "same journey, different schedule", which is why Lemma R settles them. But f · f̄ sweeps out the whole image of f, while the constant path sweeps out a single point, so no reparametrisation can connect them. Hatcher's inverse homotopy therefore has to do something genuinely different — it shortens the journey, moving through paths with progressively smaller images until nothing is left.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Losing the basepoint, and gaining a functor

The definition required a choice, and a good invariant should not depend on one. Hatcher's answer is that it does not, up to isomorphism — with a caveat worth chasing down.

Since π₁(X, x0) involves only the path-component of X containing x0, one can hope to relate π₁(X, x0) and π₁(X, x1) only when x0 and x1 lie in the same path-component. So let h be a path from x0 to x1, with inverse h̄(s) = h(1 − s), and associate to each loop f at x1 the loop h · f · h̄ at x0.

Change of basepoint. βh : π₁(X, x1) → π₁(X, x0), βh[f] = [h · f · h̄]. This is well defined: if fi is a homotopy of loops based at x1, then h · fi · h̄ is a homotopy of loops based at x0. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 28

Hatcher notes the triple product needs no bracketing convention, since the two choices are homotopic and only classes matter; alternatively one may define an n-fold product f1 · ⋯ · fn in which fi is traversed on [(i−1)/n, i/n].

Proposition 1.5. The map βh : π₁(X, x1) → π₁(X, x0) is an isomorphism. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 28

Proof. It is a homomorphism, since βh[f · g] = [h · f · g · h̄] = [h · f · h̄ · h · g · h̄] = βh[f]βh[g] — the middle equality inserting the class of h̄ · h, which is the identity by Proposition 1.3. And it is an isomorphism with inverse βh̄, since βhβh̄[f] = βh[h̄ · f · h] = [h · h̄ · f · h · h̄] = [f], and similarly βh̄βh[f] = [f]. ∎

So if X is path-connected, π₁(X, x0) is independent of the basepoint up to isomorphism, and one writes π₁(X). The caveat is in those last three words. The isomorphism depends on the chosen path h, and Problem 4 asks you to compute the discrepancy: two paths h, h′ give isomorphisms differing by conjugation by the class of the loop h̄′ · h. So the isomorphism is canonical exactly when π₁ is abelian. This is a real limitation, not pedantry — it is why basepoints persist in the subject rather than being quietly dropped.

Simply-connected. A space is simply-connected if it is path-connected and has trivial fundamental group. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 28

Proposition 1.6. A space X is simply-connected if and only if there is a unique homotopy class of paths connecting any two points in X. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 28

Proof. Path-connectedness is the existence of paths connecting every pair of points, so only uniqueness is at issue. Suppose π₁(X) = 0 and let f, g be two paths from x0 to x1. Then f ≃ f · ḡ · g ≃ g: the first step because ḡ · g is homotopic to a constant, the second because f · ḡ is a loop at x0 and so is nullhomotopic by the assumption π₁(X, x0) = 0. Conversely, if there is only one homotopy class of paths from x0 to itself, all loops at x0 are homotopic to the constant loop and π₁(X, x0) = 0. ∎

π₁ as a functor

Dexter's notes add the structural statement that Hatcher defers. First the setting.

Based space (Dexter). A based space is a pair (X, x0) of a space and a point, the basepoint. A map of based spaces f : (X, x0) → (Y, y0) is a continuous map f : X → Y with f(x0) = y0. A based homotopy is a homotopy rel {x0}. — Dexter, Algebraic Topology, §2.3, Defn (Based space)

Functoriality of π₁ (Dexter). To a based map f : (X, x0) → (Y, y0) there is an associated function f∗ = π₁(f) : π₁(X, x0) → π₁(Y, y0), defined by [γ] ↦ [f ∘ γ], satisfying: (i) π₁(f) is a homomorphism of groups; (ii) if f ≃∗ f′ then π₁(f) = π₁(f′); (iii) π₁(k ∘ h) = π₁(k) ∘ π₁(h); (iv) π₁(idX) = id. In category-theoretic language, π₁ is a functor. — Dexter, §2.3, the functoriality Prop

A note on clause (ii). Dexter writes it as "if f ≃ f′", with the bare symbol; the ≃∗ above is this lesson's notation, not the notes'. The based reading is forced, and it is the only reading under which the clause is true. Dexter defines based homotopy — a homotopy rel {x0} — in the definition immediately preceding this proposition, and the proposition is about based maps throughout, so a bare ≃ inside it means the based relation. Read it as an unrestricted homotopy and the clause fails twice over: an unbased homotopy moves f(x0), so π₁(f) and π₁(f′) need not even share a target group; and in the case where the endpoint returns to y0 along a loop, the two induced maps differ by conjugation — a change-of-basepoint isomorphism of the kind Proposition 1.5 supplies, not the identity.

Dexter states this and leaves all four clauses as an exercise — the proof in the notes reads, in its entirety, "Exercise." So it is not proved in either source for this unit, and the problem set sets three of the four clauses as work for you. It is recorded here because the word functor is the bridge out of this module: cat-03 defines functors properly, at1-10 shows homology is one, and cat-07 shows persistence is one too.

Two housekeeping notes on reading the two sources together. Dexter runs the change of basepoint in the opposite direction to Hatcher, sending a loop γ at x0 to u−1 · γ · u for a path u from x0 to x1; that is Hatcher's βū, and Problem 4 asks you to say so precisely. And Dexter writes γ−1 where Hatcher writes f̄ for the reverse path.

Guided proof. Write it out before revealing the model answer.

Prove clause (i) of the functoriality proposition — that π₁(f) is a group homomorphism — including the well-definedness that the statement takes for granted. Then prove clause (iii).

Reveal the model answer

Well-definedness. First, f ∘ γ is a loop at y0 whenever γ is a loop at x0, since (f ∘ γ)(0) = f(x0) = y0 = (f ∘ γ)(1) — this is where the based condition on f is used. Next, suppose γ ≃ γ′ via a homotopy of loops γt, with associated continuous Γ : I × I → X. Then f ∘ Γ : I × I → Y is continuous as a composite, and its restrictions to the two ends are f ∘ γ and f ∘ γ′; its endpoint values are f(x0) = y0, independent of t. So f ∘ Γ is a homotopy of loops and [f ∘ γ] = [f ∘ γ′]. Hence [γ] ↦ [f ∘ γ] is well defined on homotopy classes.

Clause (i). Let γ₀, γ₁ be loops at x0. Directly from the definition of the product path,

(f ∘ (γ₀ · γ₁))(s) = f(γ₀(2s)) for s ≤ 1/2,   = f(γ₁(2s − 1)) for s ≥ 1/2,

which is precisely the formula for (f ∘ γ₀) · (f ∘ γ₁). The two maps are equal — not merely homotopic — so

f∗([γ₀][γ₁]) = f∗[γ₀ · γ₁] = [(f ∘ γ₀) · (f ∘ γ₁)] = f∗[γ₀] f∗[γ₁].

So f∗ is a homomorphism. ∎

Clause (iii). Let h : (A, a) → (B, b) and k : (B, b) → (C, c). For any loop γ at a,

π₁(k ∘ h)[γ] = [(k ∘ h) ∘ γ] = [k ∘ (h ∘ γ)] = π₁(k)[h ∘ γ] = π₁(k)(π₁(h)[γ]),

using only associativity of composition of functions. Hence π₁(k ∘ h) = π₁(k) ∘ π₁(h). ∎

Remark. Clause (iv) is the same one-liner with the identity map, and clause (ii) is the one with real content — it needs the homotopy f ≃ f′ to be a based homotopy, since an unbased one moves f(x0) and the two induced maps would not even share a target group.

Self-check 4. Proposition 1.5 says any two basepoints in one path-component give isomorphic π₁. Why does the subject nonetheless keep writing the basepoint?

βh is always an isomorphism, so the group is determined up to isomorphism. But for two paths h and h′ from x0 to x1, the composite βh′−1βh = βh̄′βh sends [f] to [h̄′ · h · f · h̄ · h′], which is conjugation by the class of the loop h̄′ · h at x1 — an inner automorphism. Inner automorphisms are trivial exactly when the group is abelian (aa-23), so only then is there a single preferred isomorphism. This is also why the functoriality of Dexter's proposition is stated for maps of based spaces.

Self-check 5. After all of this, which spaces have you proved to have non-trivial fundamental group?

This is the honest accounting, and Hatcher makes it himself on p. 28: "It is not so easy to show that a space has a nontrivial fundamental group since one must somehow demonstrate the nonexistence of homotopies between certain loops." Every tool in this unit — linear homotopies, reparametrisation, the retraction of a path along itself, βh — builds a homotopy, and so can only ever prove a group element is trivial or two elements equal. Ruling out all homotopies at once needs a genuinely different device, the covering space ℝ → S1, which is why Theorem 1.7 waits for at1-04.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Fixing the endpoints is what makes homotopy informative. A homotopy of paths keeps ft(0) and ft(1) independent of t, with F(s,t) jointly continuous. Drop that and everything becomes homotopic to a constant. Proposition 1.2 makes it an equivalence relation, so classes [f] exist; the proof runs on the gluing lemma for two closed sets, which is then used everywhere.

2. The classes form a group, and reparametrisation buys most of the axioms. Concatenation is not associative on the nose — only after reparametrising the schedule — so π₁ must be defined on classes. Lemma R says reparametrisation preserves the class, which gives associativity and the two-sided identity. Inverses need more, because a reparametrisation cannot change the image: Hatcher retracts the path along itself, ft(s) = f(min(s, 1−t)), shrinking f · f̄ to a point.

3. The basepoint is almost removable, and π₁ is a functor. βh[f] = [h · f · h̄] is an isomorphism (Proposition 1.5), so π₁ is basepoint-independent up to isomorphism — but only up to conjugation by the choice of h, hence canonically only in the abelian case. Based maps induce homomorphisms functorially (Dexter, stated as an exercise), which is the bridge to cat-03 and at1-10. What is still missing is any example of a non-trivial π₁, because every method here constructs homotopies and none rules them out; that is at1-04's Theorem 1.7.
