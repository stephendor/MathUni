Exact sequences: Mayer–Vietoris and coefficients (at1-09)

at1-08 gave you a machine that computes the homology of a space you have already cut into cells. This unit gives you two machines of a different kind. The first computes the homology of a space from the homology of two pieces that overlap — no cell structure required, and the pieces may be anything you happen to understand. The second changes what the answer is made of: replace ℤ by any abelian group and every proof in §2.1 goes through unaltered, but the groups that come out the other end can differ so sharply that a class appears in a degree where the integral homology is zero.

Predict before we start. H4(ℝP4) = 0, as at1-08 computed. Predict what H4(ℝP4; 𝔽2) is.

Non-zero. Over ℤ the top boundary map d4 is multiplication by 2 and is injective, so Ker d4 = 0. Over a field of characteristic 2 that same map is zero, so its kernel is everything. Example 2.50 gives Hk(ℝPn; F) ≅ F for every k from 0 to n when char F = 2. Predicting "0" is the natural guess and it is wrong: coefficients do not merely coarsen the integral answer, they can produce classes it does not have.

Why this matters for the mission: Mayer-Vietoris and the coefficient choice (aa-06's fields vs rings) are exactly what TDA fixes when it picks F_2.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires, and it bundles together two things this unit is going to spend its time keeping apart.

Mayer–Vietoris is not a coefficient choice, and no coefficient choice settles it. Hatcher is explicit on p. 153: all the theory of §2.1 "carries over directly to general coefficient groups G with no change in the proofs", and "the same is true for Mayer–Vietoris sequences". So the sequence exists over ℤ, over 𝔽2, over ℚ, over ℤ6 — over every abelian group, with identical proofs. Choosing 𝔽2 does not buy you Mayer–Vietoris; you already had it. What the choice changes is the groups the sequence is a sequence of, and that is a real change, as Example 2.50 will show. Two distinct facts, and the strip's "and" hides the distinction.

"Fields vs rings" is narrower than Hatcher's setting. Hatcher's coefficients are an arbitrary abelian group G — not a ring, not a field. Nothing in the definition of Cn(X; G), in ∂2 = 0, or in the transfer of §2.1 uses multiplication. Fields enter only when one wants to compute, as in Example 2.50, or later when one wants vector spaces. Whether that generality lines up with aa-06's fields-versus-rings distinction is the syllabus's identification and not Hatcher's; treat it as a pointer.

"Exactly what TDA fixes when it picks 𝔽2" is a forward pointer to tda1 and is not established here. Neither Hatcher nor this unit contains a filtration, a persistence module or a barcode. What is established here, and is worth carrying forward, is Hatcher's own reason for 𝔽2 — with no signs there is no orientation to track, so 𝔽2 homology "is often the most natural tool in the absence of orientability" (p. 153) — together with the exact price, which Example 2.50 makes concrete rather than abstract. This closes a loop opened in at1-07, whose warn block asserted the 𝔽2 behaviour of ℝP2 from a direct reduction of Hatcher's matrices; Example 2.50 is the source's own general statement, and it agrees.

Machinery borrowed from §2.1. The long exact sequence attached to a short exact sequence of chain complexes, Proposition 2.21 on chains small with respect to a cover, and the five-lemma. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence is built entirely from the first two and cannot be derived without them; they are cited at each use and itemised in the footer. As in at1-08, this unit's resources line names §2.2 alone, and the gap is recorded in the branch plan.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Mayer–Vietoris: manufacturing a long exact sequence

Mayer–Vietoris sequence. For A, B ⊂ X with X the union of the interiors of A and B, there is a long exact sequence
··· → Hn(A ∩ B) →Φ Hn(A) ⊕ Hn(B) →Ψ Hn(X) →∂ Hn−1(A ∩ B) → ··· → H0(X) → 0. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 149

Read the hypothesis carefully. It is not X = A ∪ B; it is that the interiors already cover. Two closed semicircles cover S1 but their interiors do not, and the derivation below breaks at the step that needs chains to be subdividable into A-chains and B-chains.

The sequence is not proved directly. It is manufactured, by feeding a short exact sequence of chain complexes into the algebraic machine at1-07 and §2.1 already built. Let Cn(A + B) ⊂ Cn(X) be the chains that are sums of a chain in A and a chain in B — in general a proper subgroup. Then ∂ carries Cn(A + B) into Cn−1(A + B), so these form a chain complex, and Proposition 2.21 of §2.1 says the inclusion Cn(A + B) ↪ Cn(X) induces isomorphisms on homology. That is the whole topological content, and it is where the interior hypothesis is spent: it is barycentric subdivision that makes a general chain into a sum of small ones.

The short exact sequence. 0 → Cn(A ∩ B) →φ Cn(A) ⊕ Cn(B) →ψ Cn(A + B) → 0, with φ(x) = (x, −x) and ψ(x, y) = x + y. — Hatcher §2.2, pp. 149–150

Exactness in three lines. Ker φ = 0, since a chain in A ∩ B that is zero as a chain in A is the zero chain. Im φ ⊂ Ker ψ, since ψφ = 0. And Ker ψ ⊂ Im φ: if x + y = 0 then x = −y, so x is a chain lying in both A and B, hence in A ∩ B, and (x, y) = (x, −x). Exactness at Cn(A + B) is immediate from the definition of that group.

The minus sign in φ(x) = (x, −x) is not decoration. With φ(x) = (x, x) the composite ψφ would be x ↦ 2x rather than 0, and the sequence would fail exactness at the middle term immediately.

The connecting map can be made explicit, and it is worth doing so, because it is the part of the sequence that actually moves information between degrees. A class α ∈ Hn(X) is represented by a cycle z; by subdivision, write z = x + y with x a chain in A and y a chain in B. Neither x nor y need be a cycle. But ∂(x + y) = 0 forces ∂x = −∂y, and that common chain lies in A ∩ B and is a cycle. It represents ∂α ∈ Hn−1(A ∩ B). The whole content of the boundary map is: split a cycle, and take the boundary of either half.

There is a reduced version, obtained by augmenting the short exact sequence in the obvious way, and it is the one you will almost always want. There is also a variant that drops the interior hypothesis: if A and B are deformation retracts of neighbourhoods U and V with U ∩ V deformation retracting onto A ∩ B, the five-lemma shows nothing is lost. For a CW complex with A and B subcomplexes this always applies, so in practice one decomposes along subcomplexes and stops worrying.

Self-check 1. Why does the derivation use Cn(A + B) rather than Cn(X) in the third slot?

Surjectivity. A general singular simplex in X need not lie in A or in B, so the image of ψ is exactly Cn(A + B), a proper subgroup in general — and a short exact sequence needs surjectivity. Proposition 2.21 repairs it after the fact by showing the inclusion is a homology isomorphism, which is precisely where the interior hypothesis and barycentric subdivision are consumed. Without that step you get a long exact sequence with the wrong group in it.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Computing with it

Worked, then fading: the spheres, by induction. — Example 2.46, Hatcher p. 150

Take X = Sn with A and B the northern and southern hemispheres, slightly enlarged so their interiors cover. Then A and B are contractible and A ∩ B ≃ Sn−1. In the reduced Mayer–Vietoris sequence the middle terms H̃i(A) ⊕ H̃i(B) vanish in every degree, so each connecting map is squeezed between two zeros:

0 → H̃i(Sn) →∂ H̃i−1(Sn−1) → 0, an isomorphism for every i.

Now induct. The base case is S0, two points, with H̃0(S0) ≅ ⟨ … ⟩ and H̃i(S0) = 0 for i ≠ 0. Applying the isomorphism n times shifts the single non-zero group up by ⟨ … ⟩ degrees, giving H̃i(Sn) ≅ ⟨ … ⟩ for i = n and 0 otherwise.

One thing to notice before moving on: the argument fails with unreduced homology. At n = 1 the unreduced middle term is H0(A) ⊕ H0(B) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ, which is ⟨ zero or not? ⟩, so the squeeze does not happen. Reduced homology is not a convenience here; it is what makes the induction close.

Hatcher records a structural observation about the sequence that is worth pausing on. If A ∩ B is path-connected, the H1 terms of the reduced sequence give H1(X) ≅ (H1(A) ⊕ H1(B))/Im Φ. That is the abelianised statement of van Kampen — Theorem 1.20 from at1-05 — with the free product replaced by a direct sum and the normal subgroup N replaced by the image of Φ. Note what has vanished: van Kampen needed the triple intersections path-connected in order to control the kernel, and Mayer–Vietoris has no triple-intersection hypothesis at all. Abelianising has already collapsed the difficulty that hypothesis was there to manage. The comparison does lean on H1 being the abelianisation of π₁, which Hatcher proves in §2.A and which — as recorded in at1-03 and at1-07 — no unit of this module reads.

Guided proof. Compute H∗(K) for the Klein bottle by Mayer–Vietoris, decomposing K as the union of two Möbius bands A and B glued along their boundary circles. You will need one geometric fact and one change of basis. Write your argument before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

A and B are Möbius bands, each deformation retracting onto its core circle, and A ∩ B is their common boundary circle. So all three are homotopy equivalent to S1, and the interesting part of the reduced sequence is

0 → H2(K) → H1(A ∩ B) →Φ H1(A) ⊕ H1(B) → H1(K) → H̃0(A ∩ B) = 0,

that is, 0 → H2(K) → ℤ →Φ ℤ ⊕ ℤ → H1(K) → 0.

The geometric fact: the boundary circle of a Möbius band wraps twice around the core circle. So the generator of H1(A ∩ B) maps to twice a generator in each factor, with a sign from the orientations: Φ(1) = (2, −2).

Φ is injective, since (2, −2) ≠ 0 and ℤ is torsion-free. Exactness at the second term then gives H2(K) = 0.

For H1, take the cokernel of Φ. Choose (1, 0) and (1, −1) as a basis for ℤ ⊕ ℤ — legitimate, since the change-of-basis matrix has determinant −1. In that basis Φ(1) = (2, −2) = 2·(1, −1) + 0·(1, 0), so the quotient is ℤ ⊕ ℤ2. Hence H1(K) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ2. The earlier part of the sequence kills all higher groups. ∎

What to notice. All of the topology sits in one integer, the 2 in (2, −2), and that integer is a degree computed exactly as in at1-08. Mayer–Vietoris does not replace degree calculations; it reorganises what you do with them. Compare the answer with at1-08's cellular computation of H∗(Ng) at g = 2: ℤ1 ⊕ ℤ2, the same group by a completely different route.

Self-check 2. In the Klein bottle computation, what would change if the boundary circle wrapped once around the core?

The torsion would vanish. With Φ(1) = (1, −1) one can take (1, 0) and (1, −1) as a basis again, and the quotient is a free ℤ. The ℤ2 in H1(K) is entirely the "2", which is the degree of the boundary-to-core map — the same phenomenon as the coefficient 2 in at1-07's ℝP2 and the dk = 2 of at1-08's ℝPn. Torsion in this module has so far come from exactly one source.

You try. Use Mayer–Vietoris to compute H∗(S1 ∨ S1), choosing A and B to be open neighbourhoods of the two circles that each deformation retract onto one of them, so that A ∩ B is contractible. Then check your answer against at1-05's π₁ = ℤ ∗ ℤ, and say what the check does and does not confirm.

Segment 3 · ~30 min

Coefficients: same theorems, different answers

Homology with coefficients. Fix an abelian group G. Let Cn(X; G) be the group of chains Σi niσi with σi singular n-simplices and ni ∈ G, with the same boundary formula ∂(Σi niσi) = Σi,j (−1)j ni σi|[v0, ···, v̂j, ···, vn]. The same calculation as before gives ∂2 = 0, and the homology of this complex is Hn(X; G). — Hatcher §2.2, p. 153

Note what G is not required to be. It is an abelian group. Not a ring, not a field: nothing in the definition, in ∂2 = 0, or in what follows uses multiplication of coefficients. Fields appear only when one wants to compute conveniently, or, much later, when one wants the chain groups to be vector spaces.

The case G = ℤ2 is the one to hold on to. A chain has coefficients 0 or 1, so throwing away the zero terms makes a chain literally a finite union of simplices; and since −1 = 1, the boundary formula has no signs at all. Signs are the algebraic record of orientation, so with no signs there is nothing orientable to keep track of. That is Hatcher's reason, in his words, that ℤ2 homology "is often the most natural tool in the absence of orientability".

Now the sentence that the mission strip needs. All of the §2.1 theory carries over to arbitrary G with no change in the proofs, and so do Mayer–Vietoris sequences. Cellular homology carries over too: the chain group Hn(Xn, Xn−1; G) is a direct sum of copies of G, one per n-cell, the agreement theorem of at1-08 extends to HnCW(X; G) ≅ Hn(X; G), and the cellular boundary formula has the same integer coefficients dαβ. So the machinery is coefficient-blind. Only the answers are not.

Lemma 2.49. If f : Sk → Sk has degree m, then f∗ : Hk(Sk; G) → Hk(Sk; G) is multiplication by m. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 154

Proof. A homomorphism φ : G1 → G2 induces maps on chains commuting with ∂, hence on homology, and these are natural. Take φ : ℤ → G sending 1 to a chosen g ∈ G, and the square comparing f∗ over ℤ with f∗ over G. The outer squares commute by the inductive computation of the groups, which reduces to k = 0 where commutativity is obvious. The top map sends 1 to m, so the bottom sends g to mg. ∎

This is what licenses the previous paragraph's claim that the cellular boundary coefficients are unchanged. Without it, "the same formula" would be an assertion about numbers computed in a different category.

So the theorems are the same. Here is what is not.

Example 2.50: ℝPn over a field. With coefficients in a field F, at1-08's cellular chain complex becomes ··· →0 F →2 F →0 F →2 F →0 F → 0, the integers 2 and 0 unchanged by Lemma 2.49. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 154

If char F = 2, every map "multiplication by 2" is the zero map, so every boundary map vanishes and Hk(ℝPn; F) ≅ F for all 0 ≤ k ≤ n.

If char F ≠ 2, every map "multiplication by 2" is an isomorphism, so Hk(ℝPn; F) is F for k = 0 and for k = n odd, and 0 otherwise.

Read the ℤ column against the char-2 column in degree 4. Integrally H4(ℝP4) = 0, because d4 is multiplication by 2 and injective. Over a field of characteristic 2 that same map is zero, its kernel is everything, and a class appears where there was none. A coefficient change is not a coarsening of the integral answer; it is a different answer.

Now read the char-2 column against the ℚ column in degree 1. Over ℚ the map "multiplication by 2" is invertible, so H1(ℝP4; ℚ) = 0 and the space looks rationally like a point. Over 𝔽2 the class is there. Neither field sees what ℤ sees, and they fail to see different things. This is the same computation that at1-07's warn block did by hand for ℝP2, reducing Hatcher's boundary matrices mod 2; Example 2.50 is the general statement, and the two agree.

Hatcher gives one more reason coefficients matter, and it is stronger than a computation. Let X be a Moore space M(ℤm, n) — Sn with a cell en+1 attached by a map of degree m — and let f : X → X/Sn = Sn+1 be the quotient map. On reduced ℤ-homology f∗ is trivial, because the non-zero groups of X and Sn+1 sit in different dimensions and there is nowhere for anything to go. With ℤm coefficients the long exact sequence of the pair (X, Sn) begins 0 = H̃n+1(Sn; ℤm) → H̃n+1(X; ℤm) →f∗ H̃n+1(X/Sn; ℤm), so f∗ is injective and non-zero. Hence f is not homotopic to a constant map — a conclusion ℤ coefficients cannot reach.

Self-check 3. Does the choice of coefficient group change which exact sequences are available to you?

No. This is the distinction the mission strip blurs. The machinery is coefficient-blind: every exact sequence in the module exists over every abelian group, with identical proofs. What the choice of G changes is the groups appearing in those sequences — and Example 2.50 shows that change can be drastic. Keep the two apart; the second is a real fact and the first is not.

Self-check 4. Which is the best coefficient choice for computing the homology of ℝP4?

Malformed. ℚ loses everything but connectivity here. 𝔽2 gains a class in degree 4 that is not there integrally and cannot report that the degree-1 class has order two. ℤ keeps the most, and is the reason the fourth option is tempting — Hatcher does say in §3.A that ℤ coefficients determine the rest — but "determines" is a statement about a formula, not about what a given computation shows you. The honest summary is that a coefficient choice is a choice about what you are willing not to see.

Self-check 5. Why is Lemma 2.49 needed at all?

The boundary coefficients. Hatcher says the cellular boundary formula over G is dn(Σ nαenα) = Σ dαβnαen−1β with the same dαβ. Those numbers were defined as degrees of maps of spheres, computed with ℤ coefficients; Lemma 2.49 is what says the induced map over G is still multiplication by that integer. Without it, reusing at1-08's numbers in Example 2.50 would be an unjustified transfer.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Mayer–Vietoris is manufactured, not proved. The short exact sequence of chain complexes 0 → Cn(A ∩ B) → Cn(A) ⊕ Cn(B) → Cn(A + B) → 0, with φ(x) = (x, −x) and ψ(x, y) = x + y, is exact by three one-line checks. Feed it to the long-exact-sequence machine of §2.1 and the result has Cn(A + B) in it; Proposition 2.21 replaces that by Cn(X), and that single step is where the interior hypothesis and barycentric subdivision are spent. The connecting map is: split a cycle as x + y, take ∂x = −∂y.

2. It computes by reduction to pieces, and the topology collapses into small integers. Spheres by induction, because both hemispheres are contractible and the reduced sequence squeezes each connecting map between zeros. The Klein bottle from two Möbius bands, where the entire answer is the 2 in Φ(1) = (2, −2) — a degree, computed as in at1-08 — and a determinant-(−1) change of basis turns the cokernel into ℤ ⊕ ℤ2. The H1 terms give the abelianised van Kampen, with the triple-intersection hypothesis gone.

3. Coefficients change the answers and not the machinery. Cn(X; G) for any abelian group G, same ∂, same ∂2 = 0; all of §2.1 and Mayer–Vietoris carry over with no change in the proofs; Lemma 2.49 keeps the cellular boundary integers dαβ fixed. But Example 2.50 splits ℝPn three ways: F in every degree when char F = 2, almost nothing when char F ≠ 2, and the integral answer with its ℤ2's in between. In degree n = 4, char 2 produces a class that is not there over ℤ. And Example 2.51 shows a map that ℤ coefficients cannot distinguish from a constant while ℤm coefficients can. A coefficient choice is a choice about what to stop seeing.
