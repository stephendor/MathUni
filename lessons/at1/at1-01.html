Homotopy, homotopy equivalence, and deformation retracts (at1-01)

A coffee mug and a doughnut, made rigorous — but the rigour arrives somewhere you would not have guessed.

Write the letters of the alphabet twice: once as thin strokes, once fattened into solid regions of the plane. The thin letter sits inside the thick one. Hatcher's picture for shrinking the thick letter onto the thin one is to cut the thick letter into line segments, one from each boundary point to a unique point of the thin subletter, and then slide every point down its own segment. Points already on the thin letter never move.

That is the whole idea, and the definition is going to be exactly this picture with the time made explicit: a family of maps indexed by t in the interval [0,1]. What takes the rest of the lesson is noticing that this notion is not symmetric — the thick letter shrinks onto the thin one, but nothing shrinks the thin one onto the thick — and repairing that.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The thin letters O, P and A are three different graphs: a circle, a circle with one whisker, and a circle with two whiskers joined at a point. Each of the three can be shrunk onto a circle. Now: is each of the three a deformation retract of each of the others?

Correct, and this is the crack the whole lesson widens. "A is a deformation retract of X" presupposes A ⊆ X. P is not a subspace of O, so the statement "O is a deformation retract of P" is not false — it is not even well formed. Hatcher makes exactly this contrast on p. 3: three graphs that "are all homotopy equivalent since they are deformation retracts of the same space", yet "none of the three is a deformation retract of any other". By Segment 2 you will have the symmetric replacement, and by Segment 3 you will know the one extra hypothesis that buys back the deformation retraction.

Why this matters for the mission: Homology is a homotopy invariant — that invariance is exactly why topological features survive noise.

What this unit's source does and does not establish. The mission strip above is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires, and it reaches past this unit's source in two places.

Hatcher chapter 0 contains no homology at all. It builds the equivalence relation — homotopy equivalence — that homology will later be invariant under, and it flags the payoff twice without proving it: on p. 6 he says the Euler characteristic of a cell complex "depends only on its homotopy type, as we shall show in Theorem 2.44", and on p. 11 he says what one would like is "some sort of algebraic object associated to spaces, depending only on their homotopy type". That invariant is built in at1-03 and at1-07; the homotopy invariance of homology itself is Hatcher's Theorem 2.10, taught in at1-07.

Nothing in chapter 0 concerns noise, perturbation, or data. The claim that features survive noise is a quantitative statement about persistence diagrams under perturbation of the input, and it is the stability theorem of tda1-07 — proved there from Oudot chapter 3 and Edelsbrunner VIII.2, in the specific metric those texts use. Homotopy invariance is a necessary ingredient in that story and is not the same statement: invariance says the answer is unchanged under an exact homotopy equivalence, stability says the answer changes by a controlled amount under an inexact perturbation. This lesson establishes the first notion only.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Sliding: deformation retraction, homotopy, retraction

Hatcher's thick-letter picture parametrises the shrinking by time. Here is the definition it produces, word for word in content.

Deformation retraction. A deformation retraction of a space X onto a subspace A is a family of maps ft : X → X, t ∈ I, such that f0 = 𝟙 (the identity map), f1(X) = A, and ft|A = 𝟙 for all t. The family ft should be continuous in the sense that the associated map X × I → X, (x, t) ↦ ft(x), is continuous. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 2

Three conditions and a continuity clause, and the continuity clause is the one that carries the content. It is not the statement that each ft is continuous. It is the stronger statement that the single map on the product X × I is continuous — jointly in the point and the time. Every verification you do in this unit will turn on that distinction (top-04).

Worked example: the punctured plane onto the circle

Claim. ℝn ∖ {0} deformation retracts onto Sn−1, via ft(x) = (1 − t)x + t·x/|x|.

Step 0 — rewrite the formula so one scalar controls everything. Both x and x/|x| are multiples of x, so

ft(x) = λ(t, x)·x,   where   λ(t, x) = (1 − t) + t/|x|.

Step 1 — the map lands where it should. For t ∈ [0,1] both (1 − t) ≥ 0 and t/|x| ≥ 0, and they are not both zero: if t = 0 the first is 1, and if t > 0 the second is positive. So λ > 0 strictly. Since x ≠ 0 and λ ≠ 0, ft(x) = λx ≠ 0. The map really does send ℝn ∖ {0} into itself. Skipping this step is the commonest error in this exercise, because the formula looks self-evidently harmless.

Step 2 — f0 = 𝟙. λ(0, x) = 1, so f0(x) = x.

Step 3 — f1(X) = A. λ(1, x) = 1/|x|, so f1(x) = x/|x|, which has norm 1. Hence f1(X) ⊆ Sn−1. For the reverse inclusion, any point of Sn−1 is fixed by f1 (Step 4), so it is in the image. Therefore f1(X) = Sn−1.

Step 4 — ft|A = 𝟙. If |x| = 1 then λ(t, x) = (1 − t) + t = 1 for every t, so ft(x) = x. Points of the sphere are stationary for the entire motion, exactly as Hatcher's points of the thin letter are.

Step 5 — joint continuity. The map (x, t) ↦ |x| is continuous on ℝn × I and is non-zero on (ℝn ∖ {0}) × I, so (x, t) ↦ t/|x| is continuous there as a quotient with non-vanishing denominator. Adding (1 − t) keeps continuity, giving λ continuous on (ℝn ∖ {0}) × I. Finally scalar multiplication ℝ × ℝn → ℝn is continuous, and (x, t) ↦ ft(x) is the composite (x, t) ↦ (λ(t,x), x) ↦ λx. Composites of continuous maps are continuous. Note where the puncture was needed: at |x| = 0 the function 1/|x| is not defined, let alone continuous.

Consequence. The same formula does not work on all of ℝn, and Step 1 already shows why: at x = 0 the formula is undefined. This is not a technicality to be patched. In at1-04 you will prove that no map whatever gives a deformation retraction of the disk onto its boundary sphere.

Homotopy, retraction, and homotopy rel A

Hatcher now generalises in the obvious direction: keep the family, drop everything special about it.

Homotopy; homotopic. A homotopy is any family of maps ft : X → Y, t ∈ I, such that the associated map F : X × I → Y given by F(x, t) = ft(x) is continuous. Two maps f0, f1 : X → Y are homotopic if there exists a homotopy ft connecting them, written f0 ≃ f1. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 3

Retraction. A retraction of X onto A is a map r : X → X such that r(X) = A and r|A = 𝟙. Equivalently, one may regard a retraction as a map X → A restricting to the identity on the subspace A ⊆ X. From a more formal viewpoint a retraction is a map r : X → X with r2 = r, since this equation says exactly that r is the identity on its image. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 3

Homotopy rel A. A homotopy ft : X → Y whose restriction to a subspace A ⊆ X is independent of t is called a homotopy relative to A, or a homotopy rel A. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 3

These three definitions let Hatcher repackage the first one, and the repackaging is worth memorising: a deformation retraction of X onto A is a homotopy rel A from the identity map of X to a retraction of X onto A. Every clause of the original definition is accounted for — f0 = 𝟙 is the starting map, f1(X) = A makes the ending map a retraction, and ft|A = 𝟙 is the rel A.

Hatcher asserts the r2 = r characterisation without proof. Since the problem set uses it, here it is in full.

Lemma A (the idempotent characterisation). Let r : X → X be a map. Then r(X) = A and r|A = 𝟙 for some subspace A if and only if r2 = r; and in that case A = r(X) necessarily.

Proof. (⟹) Let x ∈ X. Then r(x) ∈ r(X) = A, and r is the identity on A, so r(r(x)) = r(x). As x was arbitrary, r2 = r.

(⟸) Suppose r2 = r and set A = r(X). Let a ∈ A, so a = r(x) for some x ∈ X. Then r(a) = r(r(x)) = r2(x) = r(x) = a. Hence r|A = 𝟙, and r(X) = A holds by the definition of A. ∎

Note what the lemma does not say. It says nothing about deformation retractions. Every space retracts onto each of its points, via the constant map — which is idempotent — so retractions are cheap. The next lemma shows deformation retractions are not.

Lemma B (slices of a homotopy are paths). Let ft : X → Y be a homotopy and fix x ∈ X. Then γ : I → Y, γ(t) = ft(x), is continuous. Consequently, a space that deformation retracts onto a point is path-connected.

Proof. The inclusion I → X × I, t ↦ (x, t), is continuous, being the identity in the second coordinate and constant in the first. Composing with the continuous F : X × I → Y gives γ = F ∘ (t ↦ (x,t)), continuous as a composite. This is precisely the step that the weaker hypothesis — each ft continuous separately — would not support.

Now suppose ft is a deformation retraction of X onto {x0}. For any x ∈ X the path γ above has γ(0) = f0(x) = x and γ(1) = f1(x) = x0, since f1(X) = {x0}. So every point is joined by a path to x0, and concatenating two such paths joins any two points. ∎

Hatcher draws the moral on p. 3: a space always retracts onto any point via the constant map, but a space that deformation retracts onto a point must be path-connected. He adds that it is "less trivial to show that there are path-connected spaces that do not deformation retract onto a point", and expects the letters with holes — A, B, D, O, P, Q, R — to be examples. Proving it needs an invariant, which is chapter 1's business and at1-03's.

Self-check 1. Which single clause of the definition of deformation retraction is the reason a discrete two-point space {0, 1} fails to deformation retract onto {0}?

By Lemma B, a space that deformation retracts onto a point is path-connected, and {0,1} discrete is not. Traced back to the definition, the obstruction sits at f1: the path t ↦ ft(1) would have to run continuously from 1 to 0 inside a discrete space, which forces it to be constant — so f1(1) = 1 ≠ 0 and the image is not A. Note that the constant map is a perfectly good retraction; it just is not the end of any homotopy starting at the identity.

Self-check 2. In Step 5 of the worked example, why was it not enough to observe that each individual ft is a continuous map ℝn ∖ {0} → ℝn ∖ {0}?

Right. Hatcher writes the continuity clause on the associated map X × I → X deliberately. Separate continuity in each variable does not imply joint continuity in general, and Lemma B shows what joint continuity buys that slice-wise continuity does not: the other family of slices, the paths t ↦ ft(x), come out continuous too. That is the ingredient the path-connectedness argument runs on.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

Repairing the asymmetry: homotopy equivalence

Now the crack from the prediction gate. Hatcher extracts what a deformation retraction gives, in a form that no longer mentions subspaces.

If X deformation retracts onto A via ft, write r : X → A for the resulting retraction and i : A → X for the inclusion. Then two things hold: ri = 𝟙A, because r is the identity on A; and ir ≃ 𝟙X, the homotopy being ft itself. Generalise by forgetting that A sat inside X:

Homotopy equivalence; homotopy type. A map f : X → Y is called a homotopy equivalence if there is a map g : Y → X such that fg ≃ 𝟙 and gf ≃ 𝟙. The spaces X and Y are said to be homotopy equivalent or to have the same homotopy type, written X ≃ Y. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 3

The gain is immediate: the definition is visibly symmetric in f and g, so it can hold between two spaces neither of which is a subspace of the other. That is the repair. Hatcher calls checking that it is an equivalence relation "an easy exercise", "in contrast with the nonsymmetric notion of deformation retraction". The exercise is Problem 3, and it needs one lemma he does not isolate.

Lemma C (homotopy is compatible with composition). Let f0 ≃ f1 : X → Y. Then for any map g : Y → Z we have gf0 ≃ gf1, and for any map h : W → X we have f0h ≃ f1h.

Proof. Let F : X × I → Y be continuous with F(·, 0) = f0 and F(·, 1) = f1.

For the first claim, g ∘ F : X × I → Z is continuous as a composite, and (g ∘ F)(·, 0) = gf0, (g ∘ F)(·, 1) = gf1. So g ∘ F is a homotopy from gf0 to gf1.

For the second, h × 𝟙I : W × I → X × I is continuous, so F ∘ (h × 𝟙I) : W × I → Y is continuous, and it takes (w, 0) to f0(h(w)) and (w, 1) to f1(h(w)). So it is a homotopy from f0h to f1h. ∎

With Lemma C the transitivity of ≃ is a computation. Suppose f : X → Y has homotopy inverse g, and f′ : Y → Z has homotopy inverse g′. The only candidate inverse for f′f is gg′, and

(gg′)(f′f) = g (g′f′) f ≃ g 𝟙Y f = gf ≃ 𝟙X,

where the first ≃ applies Lemma C twice to the homotopy g′f′ ≃ 𝟙Y — once on the left by g and once on the right by f. The other composite is symmetric. Reflexivity takes g = 𝟙X; symmetry is free, since the definition treats f and g alike.

Worked example: the mapping cylinder

Mapping cylinder. For a map f : X → Y, the mapping cylinder Mf is the quotient space of the disjoint union (X × I) ⊔ Y obtained by identifying each (x, 1) ∈ X × I with f(x) ∈ Y. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 2

Claim. Mf deformation retracts onto the subspace Y, for every map f — no hypothesis on f at all.

Step 1 — the formula, upstairs. Define on the disjoint union, before quotienting:

f̃t(x, s) = (x, s + t(1 − s))   on X × I,    f̃t(y) = y   on Y.

The cylinder coordinate s moves towards 1 at a rate proportional to how far it has left to go; at t = 1 it arrives, whatever it started at.

Step 2 — it respects the identification. This is the only real step. The identification glues (x, 1) to f(x). Apply the formula to each side. On the cylinder side, f̃t(x, 1) = (x, 1 + t(1 − 1)) = (x, 1), which is glued to f(x). On the Y side, f̃t(f(x)) = f(x). The two results are the same point of Mf, so the map descends.

Step 3 — continuity of the descended map. f̃ is continuous on ((X × I) ⊔ Y) × I, since a map out of a disjoint union is continuous exactly when its restriction to each summand is, and each piece here is built from continuous arithmetic. By the universal property of the quotient — a map out of a quotient is continuous exactly when its composite with the quotient map is (top-13) — the descended ft : Mf × I → Mf is continuous.

Step 4 — the three conditions. f0(x, s) = (x, s) and f0(y) = y, so f0 = 𝟙. At t = 1, f1(x, s) = (x, 1) ∼ f(x) ∈ Y and f1(y) = y, so f1(Mf) = Y. And ft|Y = 𝟙 by construction. All three hold.

Step 5 — cash it in. By the ri = 𝟙, ir ≃ 𝟙 observation at the top of this segment, the inclusion Y ↪ Mf is a homotopy equivalence. This is Hatcher's main early tool: "the main tool we used for constructing homotopy equivalences was the fact that a mapping cylinder deformation retracts onto its 'target' end" (p. 10).

Contractible spaces

Contractible; nullhomotopic. A space having the homotopy type of a point is called contractible. This amounts to requiring that the identity map of the space be nullhomotopic, that is, homotopic to a constant map. In general, this is slightly weaker than saying the space deformation retracts to a point. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 4

Hold that last sentence up to Segment 1's repackaging and the gap becomes visible. A deformation retraction onto {x0} is a homotopy from 𝟙X to the constant map rel {x0}. Contractibility asks only for a homotopy from 𝟙X to a constant map, with no rel condition — so the point x0 is allowed to wander while the contraction runs. That single dropped clause is the whole of the difference, and Hatcher's chapter-0 exercises supply a space realising it.

Hatcher's showpiece is the house with two rooms (p. 4): a box divided into two chambers, each reachable from outside only through a tunnel punched down through the other, with two support walls. It is contractible — indeed it deformation retracts to a point — but not visibly so. The argument is worth its length: a closed ε-neighbourhood N(X) deformation retracts onto X and is homeomorphic to the ball D3, so X ≃ N(X) = D3 ≃ point, and contractibility follows because homotopy equivalence is an equivalence relation. Hatcher then remarks it is "quite a challenging exercise to see exactly what this deformation retraction looks like" — the space contracts, but no one can picture it doing so.

Self-check 3. A student claims: "Mf deformation retracts onto Y, and it also contains X as the subspace X × {0}. So X and Y are both deformation retracts of Mf, hence X ≃ Y for every map f whatsoever." Where is the error?

The two ends of the cylinder are not symmetric. Step 4 retracts onto Y with no hypothesis on f, because the identification is at the Y end and the slide runs towards it. Nothing yet retracts Mf onto X × {0}, and the claim would prove every two spaces homotopy equivalent, which is absurd — take f : S1 → point. Segment 3's Corollary 0.21 supplies exactly the missing condition: X is a deformation retract of Mf precisely when f is a homotopy equivalence. The student has assumed the conclusion.

Self-check 4. Let X be contractible and W any space. How many homotopy classes of maps W → X are there?

Let H be a nullhomotopy of 𝟙X, ending at the constant map c. For any g : W → X, Lemma C's second half applied to 𝟙X ≃ c on the right by g gives g = 𝟙X ∘ g ≃ c ∘ g, and c ∘ g is itself constant. So every map into X is homotopic to the same constant map, and hence any two are homotopic to each other. Note the direction: maps into a contractible space collapse. Maps out of one collapse too, by the first half of Lemma C, but that is a separate application.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

The other end of the cylinder

Segment 2 left a debt. The mapping cylinder retracts onto Y for free; the question is when it also retracts onto X. Hatcher pays the debt in the last section of chapter 0, and the currency is a technical hypothesis.

Homotopy extension property. A pair (X, A) has the homotopy extension property if every pair of maps X × {0} → Y and A × I → Y that agree on A × {0} can be extended to a map X × I → Y. Equivalently, (X, A) has the homotopy extension property if and only if X × {0} ∪ A × I is a retract of X × I. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 14

Read it as an upgrade device. You are handed a homotopy that lives only on the subspace A, plus a map on all of X at time zero, and the property lets you extend the homotopy over all of X. That is precisely the missing ingredient identified in Segment 2's discussion of contractibility: the difference between a bare homotopy and a homotopy rel a subspace is control over what happens on the subspace, and this property is what supplies it. Hatcher's counterexample (pp. 14–15) is the pair (I, A) with A = {0, 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, …}, where the property fails because of "the bad structure of (X, A) near 0".

Three results follow, and the problem set uses all three.

Proposition 0.16. If (X, A) is a CW pair, then X × {0} ∪ A × I is a deformation retract of X × I, hence (X, A) has the homotopy extension property. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 15

The proof begins with a retraction r : Dn × I → Dn × {0} ∪ ∂Dn × I given by radial projection from the point (0, 2) ∈ Dn × ℝ, then sets rt = tr + (1 − t)𝟙 and builds up over skeleta, performing the n-th stage during the t-interval [1/2n+1, 1/2n] so that infinitely many concatenated homotopies still finish in unit time. The cell-complex machinery it runs on is at1-02's subject; this unit uses only the conclusion.

Proposition 0.17. If the pair (X, A) satisfies the homotopy extension property and A is contractible, then the quotient map q : X → X/A is a homotopy equivalence. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 15

This is the precise form of the hope Hatcher floats on p. 11 — that collapsing a subspace which already has the homotopy type of a point should not change the homotopy type of the whole. Note that contractibility of A alone is not the hypothesis; the extension property is there too, and Problem 5 asks you to say why.

Corollary 0.20. If (X, A) satisfies the homotopy extension property and the inclusion A ↪ X is a homotopy equivalence, then A is a deformation retract of X. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 16

This is the converse direction of Segment 2's opening observation, and it is the sentence that closes the loop the prediction gate opened. Going one way is free: a deformation retract is a homotopy equivalence. Coming back — from an abstract homotopy equivalence to an actual sliding motion that fixes A pointwise throughout — costs the homotopy extension property.

Corollary 0.21. A map f : X → Y is a homotopy equivalence if and only if X is a deformation retract of the mapping cylinder Mf. Hence, two spaces X and Y are homotopy equivalent if and only if there is a third space containing both X and Y as deformation retracts. — Hatcher, ch. 0, p. 16; proof on p. 17

Hatcher's proof is short because the work is already done. In the mapping cylinder, write i : X → Mf and j : Y → Mf for the inclusions and r : Mf → Y for the canonical retraction of Segment 2, so that f = ri and i ≃ jf. Since j and r are homotopy equivalences, f is a homotopy equivalence if and only if i is — using that a composite of homotopy equivalences is one, and that a map homotopic to a homotopy equivalence is one. Then apply Corollary 0.20 to the pair (Mf, X), which has the homotopy extension property by Example 0.15, using the neighbourhood X × [0, 1/2] of X in Mf.

So the answer to the prediction gate, in full. The three graphs are homotopy equivalent; none is a deformation retract of any other, because none is a subspace of any other; and Corollary 0.21 supplies the honest symmetric statement that replaces the failed one — there is a third space containing any two of them as deformation retracts, and one may always take it to be a mapping cylinder.

Prove that if X deformation retracts onto A, then the inclusion i : A ↪ X is a homotopy equivalence. Then state, in one sentence, exactly what Corollary 0.20 adds to this — that is, what extra hypothesis converts the converse into a theorem, and why the converse is not free.

Reveal the model answer

Claim. If ft is a deformation retraction of X onto A, then i : A ↪ X is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof. Let r : X → A be the map given by r(x) = f1(x); this is well defined into A because f1(X) = A, and continuous because f1 is. First, ri = 𝟙A: for a ∈ A we have r(i(a)) = f1(a) = a, since ft|A = 𝟙 for every t, in particular at t = 1. Second, ir ≃ 𝟙X: the composite ir : X → X is exactly f1, and the family ft is by hypothesis a homotopy X × I → X from f0 = 𝟙X to f1. So ft witnesses 𝟙X ≃ ir. Both conditions of the definition of homotopy equivalence hold, with g = r. ∎

What Corollary 0.20 adds. It supplies the converse, and the extra hypothesis is the homotopy extension property for the pair (X, A): given only that i : A ↪ X is a homotopy equivalence, one has some homotopy from ir to 𝟙X, but nothing forces that homotopy to hold A fixed while it runs — and a deformation retraction is by definition a homotopy rel A. The homotopy extension property is precisely the device for upgrading a homotopy prescribed on the subspace to one defined on all of X, which is what lets the rel A condition be imposed rather than merely hoped for.

Self-check 5. Corollary 0.21 concludes that X ≃ Y if and only if some third space contains both as deformation retracts. Which of the two directions is the one that needed the whole homotopy-extension apparatus?

The reverse direction is cheap: if Z contains X and Y as deformation retracts then both inclusions are homotopy equivalences by the guided proof above, and ≃ is an equivalence relation, so X ≃ Y. The forward direction is the expensive one. Given a homotopy equivalence f, one takes Z = Mf; Segment 2 gives the Y end for free, but the X end requires Corollary 0.20 applied to (Mf, X), which needs that pair to have the homotopy extension property — supplied by Example 0.15 via the neighbourhood X × [0, 1/2].

Reveal the skeleton

1. Deformation retraction is the picture, and it is not symmetric. A family ft : X → X with f0 = 𝟙, f1(X) = A, ft|A = 𝟙, jointly continuous on X × I. Equivalently: a homotopy rel A from 𝟙X to a retraction. The relation presupposes A ⊆ X, so it cannot be symmetric between two spaces — and Hatcher's three graphs show the failure is real, not an artefact.

2. Homotopy equivalence is the symmetric replacement, and a deformation retraction produces one. f : X → Y with g such that fg ≃ 𝟙 and gf ≃ 𝟙. If X deformation retracts onto A then ri = 𝟙 and ir ≃ 𝟙, so the inclusion is a homotopy equivalence. This forces the previous step's problem to go away, because the definition never mentions subspaces. The mapping cylinder makes the new relation constructive: Mf deformation retracts onto Y for every f, with no hypothesis.

3. The converse costs a hypothesis, and Corollary 0.21 is the payoff. Going from an abstract homotopy equivalence back to an actual motion fixing A pointwise needs the homotopy extension property (Corollary 0.20), because only that lets a homotopy given on the subspace be extended over the whole space. Applied to (Mf, X) it yields Corollary 0.21: f is a homotopy equivalence exactly when X too is a deformation retract of Mf — so X ≃ Y precisely when a third space contains both as deformation retracts. That is the honest symmetric statement the first step could not make.
