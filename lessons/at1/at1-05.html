Van Kampen's theorem (at1-05)

You know π₁ of a circle. That is one space. Van Kampen turns it into thousands.

at1-04 cost you a whole unit of lifting arguments to establish a single fact: π₁(S1) ≅ ℤ. At that rate the subject is hopeless — there are a lot of spaces. What you actually want is a rule that says: if you can cut a space into pieces you already understand, the group of the whole is assembled from the groups of the pieces.

Van Kampen's theorem is that rule, and by the end of this unit it will have given you π₁ of every wedge of circles, every graph, every closed surface, every complement of a torus knot — and, remarkably, a space with any prescribed group whatsoever as its fundamental group. The price is that the assembly is not a product. Pieces of a space do not commute with each other, so the algebra needed is the free product, and half this unit is spent building it.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. Write S1 as the union of two open arcs A and B, each a proper sub-arc of the circle, with A ∪ B = S1. Both are contractible, so π₁(A) = π₁(B) = 0. What does that tell you about π₁(S1)?

This is Hatcher's own counterexample, on p. 44, and he gives it immediately after the theorem precisely so that nobody reads the hypotheses as decoration. Two proper open arcs covering the circle must overlap in two disjoint arcs, not one — go round the circle and check. So A ∩ B is not path-connected, and the surjectivity half of the theorem does not apply. The moral to carry through the unit: van Kampen's hypotheses are about the intersections, and every single one of them is doing work. Problem 3 makes you find where each is consumed in the proof.

Why this matters for the mission: Local-to-global: the same 'assemble from pieces' logic Mayer-Vietoris and the nerve theorem later run on.

What this unit's source does and does not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Its first clause is exactly this unit. The comparison in the second clause is a forward pointer, and Hatcher §1.2 — the only resource for at1-05 — contains neither of the two things being pointed at.

Mayer–Vietoris does not appear anywhere in §1.2. It is a long exact sequence in homology, and it is at1-09's business. The structural analogy is real and worth stating in advance, but stating it is all this lesson can do: van Kampen assembles a possibly non-abelian group from a two-set open cover using a free product and a normal subgroup, while Mayer–Vietoris assembles abelian groups from a two-set cover using an exact sequence. Whether those are "the same logic" is a judgement you will be in a position to make in at1-09 and not before. Nothing in this lesson depends on the comparison.

The nerve theorem is further away still: it is tda2-07, from Dey chapter 9, and it says that a good cover's nerve is homotopy equivalent to the covered space. It is genuinely a local-to-global theorem about covers, but it is a statement about homotopy type rather than about π₁, its hypothesis is contractibility of all finite intersections rather than path-connectedness of the double and triple ones, and no part of it is proved or used here.

So: this lesson teaches van Kampen's theorem, in the generality Hatcher proves it, and flags the two later theorems as destinations rather than claiming anything about them.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Free products: the algebra the theorem speaks

Start with the space that motivates everything. Let X be two circles A and B meeting at a single point x0, the figure eight. Each circle contributes an infinite cyclic group — a generated by going once round A, b once round B. Products of powers of a and b, such as a5b2a−3ba2, then give elements of π₁(X). The question is whether distinct words give distinct elements, and van Kampen will say yes.

Hatcher's point on p. 40 is that the group of all such words is a construction worth having in its own right. Given groups Gα, we want a single group containing them all as subgroups. The direct product ∏α Gα and the direct sum ⊕α Gα both do that — but in both, elements of different Gα commute. For non-abelian groups that commutativity is, in his word, unnatural. The free product is the non-abelian version of the direct sum.

Free product. As a set, the free product ∗α Gα consists of all words g1g2 ··· gm of arbitrary finite length m ≥ 0, where each letter gi belongs to a group Gαi and is not the identity of that group, and adjacent letters belong to different groups: αi ≠ αi+1. Such words are called reduced. The empty word is allowed and is the identity element. The group operation is juxtaposition, followed by reduction: if gm and h1 lie in the same Gα they are combined into the single letter gmh1, and if that letter is the identity it is cancelled, which may allow gm−1 and h2 to combine in turn. — Hatcher §1.2, p. 41

Two conditions, and each is doing something. "Not the identity" removes the padding; "adjacent letters in different groups" removes the ambiguity that would let one element be written two ways. Together they make the reduced form a normal form, so that two reduced words are equal in the group exactly when they are equal as strings.

The free product of any number of copies of ℤ is a free group. Its elements are uniquely reduced words in powers of one generator per copy; those generators are a basis, and their number is the rank. Hatcher records on p. 42 the fact that makes rank usable as an invariant: the abelianisation of a free group is free abelian on the same generators, and the rank of a free abelian group is well defined independent of basis, so the rank of a free group is too. You will use this three times in the problem set, and each time it is the only thing standing between you and a false conclusion.

Worked example: why the multiplication is associative

Juxtapose-then-reduce looks like it might fail to be associative, and checking directly is, in Hatcher's word, tedious — reducing (uv)w and u(vw) means tracking cascades of cancellation. Here is his indirect argument, which is worth studying because the technique recurs.

Step 1. Let W be the set of reduced words, including the empty one. For g ∈ Gα define Lg : W → W by left multiplication, Lg(g1 ··· gm) = g g1 ··· gm, combining g with g1 if g1 ∈ Gα. Note this is a function on the set W; no group structure on W is being assumed. That is the trick.

Step 2. Lgg′ = LgLg′ for g, g′ in the same Gα. This is a special case of associativity, but a trivial one: it follows from associativity inside Gα, which we already have.

Step 3. Hence Lg is invertible, with inverse Lg−1, so g ↦ Lg is a homomorphism Gα → P(W), the group of all permutations of W.

Step 4. Extend to L : W → P(W) by L(g1 ··· gm) = Lg1 ··· Lgm. This L is injective, because the permutation L(g1 ··· gm) sends the empty word to g1 ··· gm — so the word can be recovered from the permutation.

Step 5. By Step 2, the product operation on W corresponds under L to composition in P(W). Composition of functions is associative for free. L is injective, so the product on W inherits associativity. ∎

What was gained. The hard verification was transported into a structure where the answer is free, and then pulled back along an injection. Notice how little was needed: only that L is injective and intertwines the two operations. Hatcher does not even name this a lemma.

Two examples worth having in hand. ℤ ∗ ℤ is the figure-eight group, free of rank 2. And ℤ2 ∗ ℤ2 is a free product that is not free: since a2 = e = b2, no powers are needed and the elements are exactly the alternating words a, b, ab, ba, aba, bab, ··· together with the empty word. Sending a word to its length mod 2 is a surjection onto ℤ2 whose kernel is infinite cyclic, generated by ab; the group is the infinite dihedral group. Problem 1(d) asks you to check this, including the part that is easy to skip — that length mod 2 is well defined.

Universal property of the free product. Any collection of homomorphisms φα : Gα → H extends uniquely to a homomorphism φ : ∗α Gα → H. The value on a word g1 ··· gn with gi ∈ Gαi must be φα1(g1) ··· φαn(gn), and using this as the definition gives a well-defined homomorphism, since reducing an unreduced product does not change its image. — Hatcher §1.2, p. 42

This is the single most important sentence in the segment, because it is what makes the map Φ of the next segment exist at all. Everything van Kampen says is a statement about one particular application of this property.

Self-check 1. The inclusions G ↪ G × H and H ↪ G × H induce, by the universal property, a homomorphism G ∗ H → G × H. What is it doing?

Surjective: the pair (g, h) is the image of the two-letter word gh. Not injective as soon as both groups are non-trivial: the word ghg−1h−1 is a non-empty reduced word of length four, so it is not the identity of G ∗ H, but its image is the identity of G × H. Hatcher records the surjectivity on p. 42 as an illustration of the universal property. The right way to hold the two constructions in mind is that G × H is the abelianised-across-factors version of G ∗ H: it is exactly G ∗ H with every commutator [g, h] killed.

Segment 2 · ~30 min

The theorem

Set the stage. Suppose X is the union of path-connected open sets Aα, each containing the basepoint x0. The inclusions Aα ↪ X induce jα : π₁(Aα) → π₁(X), so by the universal property there is a single homomorphism

Φ : ∗α π₁(Aα) → π₁(X).

Some of the kernel is forced on us before we know anything. Write iαβ : π₁(Aα ∩ Aβ) → π₁(Aα) for the map induced by inclusion. A loop ω living in Aα ∩ Aβ can be regarded as living in Aα or in Aβ, and either way it is the same loop in X, so jαiαβ = jβiβα. Hence every element iαβ(ω) iβα(ω)−1 is in ker Φ. The theorem says that under mild hypotheses there is nothing else.

Theorem 1.20 (van Kampen). If X is the union of path-connected open sets Aα each containing the basepoint x0 ∈ X and if each intersection Aα ∩ Aβ is path-connected, then the homomorphism Φ : ∗α π₁(Aα) → π₁(X) is surjective. If in addition each intersection Aα ∩ Aβ ∩ Aγ is path-connected, then the kernel of Φ is the normal subgroup N generated by all elements of the form iαβ(ω) iβα(ω)−1 for ω ∈ π₁(Aα ∩ Aβ), and hence Φ induces an isomorphism π₁(X) ≅ ∗α π₁(Aα)/N. — Hatcher §1.2, p. 43

Read the shape of it: two hypotheses, two conclusions, each hypothesis buying exactly one conclusion. Double intersections path-connected buys surjectivity. Triple intersections path-connected buys the kernel. Hatcher supplies a counterexample for each — the two open arcs on S1 from the prediction gate for the first, and the suspension of three points for the second — and Problem 3 works both.

In practice the cover usually has just two sets. Then there are no distinct triples to worry about, the second hypothesis is vacuous, and one obtains π₁(X) ≅ (π₁(Aα) ∗ π₁(Aβ))/N provided only that Aα ∩ Aβ is path-connected. Hatcher notes on p. 44 that the proof in this case is virtually identical to the general one, so nothing is lost by picturing two sets throughout.

That is the picture behind the whole unit. A is an open set containing the left circle and a little of the right; B contains the right circle and a little of the left; A ∩ B is a small neighbourhood of x0 shaped like a cross, which deformation retracts to the point. So π₁(A ∩ B) = 0, the normal subgroup N is trivial, and Theorem 1.20 delivers π₁(figure eight) ≅ ℤ ∗ ℤ. Each factor of ℤ is Theorem 1.7, from at1-04. Without that unit, van Kampen assembles nothing out of nothing.

Wedges, graphs, and a distinction that is easy to lose

Example 1.21 generalises the picture. For a wedge sum ⋁α Xα, suppose each basepoint xα is a deformation retract of an open neighbourhood Uα in Xα. Then Aα = Xα ⋁ ⋁β ≠ α Uβ is open and deformation retracts onto Xα, and the intersection of two or more distinct Aα is ⋁α Uα, which retracts to a point. All intersections are simply-connected, N is trivial, and Φ : ∗α π₁(Xα) → π₁(⋁α Xα) is an isomorphism. In particular π₁ of a wedge of circles is free.

Read that statement carefully, because it has two moving parts and the second is the one that catches people. The hypothesis is about each Xα on its own; the conclusion is about ⋁α Xα, which is by definition a quotient of the disjoint union. Example 1.25 is the standard trap. It is the shrinking wedge: the union in ℝ2 of the circles Cn of radius 1/n centred at (1/n, 0), and Hatcher opens it by remarking that at first glance one might confuse this space with the wedge sum of an infinite sequence of circles. Now check the hypothesis on it. Nothing whatever goes wrong — each Cn is an ordinary circle, and the origin is a deformation retract of an open arc Un ⊂ Cn, exactly as required. The failure is not there. It is that the shrinking wedge is not the wedge sum. It carries the subspace topology from ℝ2, in which every neighbourhood of the origin contains all but finitely many of the circles entirely; in ⋁n Cn the set ⋁n Un is a neighbourhood of the origin meeting each circle in a small arc and nothing more. The same points, two different topologies. So Example 1.21 is silent here — not because a hypothesis failed, but because it is a theorem about a different space.

The difference is not cosmetic. On p. 49 Hatcher builds, for any sequence of integers kn, a loop that wraps kn times around Cn during the time interval [1 − 1/n, 1 − 1/(n+1)]; and the very fact that every neighbourhood of the origin swallows almost every circle is what makes that infinite concatenation continuous at time 1. So π₁ of the shrinking wedge surjects onto the uncountable group ∏∞ ℤ, whereas the fundamental group of a wedge of countably many circles is countably generated, hence countable. Two spaces with the same underlying set, and fundamental groups not even of the same cardinality.

The control is Exercise 20, on p. 55: the union in ℝ2 of the circles of radius n centred at (n, 0). The same circles, still meeting only at the origin — escaping to infinity now instead of shrinking to a point. There π₁ is the free group ∗n π₁(Cn), and the space is genuinely homotopy equivalent to ⋁∞ S1, though Hatcher notes it is still not homeomorphic to it. Nothing about the individual circles changed between the two examples. Only the topology at the point where they all meet.

Example 1.22 does a graph. Take the twelve edges of a cube; seven of them form a maximal tree T, a contractible subgraph containing every vertex. For each edge eα outside T take an open neighbourhood Aα of T ∪ eα that deformation retracts onto it. Any two or more Aα intersect in a set retracting onto T, hence contractible; each Aα retracts onto a circle. So π₁ is free on the five edges outside the tree, with an explicit generator for each: travel in T to one end of eα, cross it, return through T.

You try. Before reading Segment 3, do the count that makes Example 1.22 a general method. A finite connected graph has V vertices and E edges. A maximal tree contains every vertex. How many edges does a tree on V vertices have, and therefore what is the rank of the free group π₁ of the graph, in terms of V and E? Check your formula against the cube, and then against a single circle regarded as one vertex and one edge.

Then the harder half: the argument above needs the intersections of the Aα to be contractible, which came from T being contractible. Where would the argument break if you chose a subgraph containing every vertex that was not a tree?

How the proof goes

The surjectivity half is not new. Hatcher proved it in the previous section, as the tool that would be needed here:

Lemma 1.15. If a space X is the union of a collection of path-connected open sets Aα each containing the basepoint x0 ∈ X, and if each intersection Aα ∩ Aβ is path-connected, then every loop in X at x0 is homotopic to a product of loops each of which is contained in a single Aα. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 35

Its proof is a compactness argument you should be able to reproduce. Each point s ∈ I has a neighbourhood mapped by the loop f into some Aα; shrink it to an interval whose closure maps into a single Aα; extract a finite subcover by compactness of I; the endpoints give a partition 0 = s0 < ··· < sm = 1 with f([si−1, si]) inside a single Ai. The pieces fi are paths, not loops, so they are not yet elements of anything — and this is where the first hypothesis is spent. Since Ai ∩ Ai+1 is path-connected, choose a path gi in it from x0 to f(si). Inserting ḡigi at each junction turns the product of paths into a product of loops, each inside one Aα, without changing the homotopy class.

The kernel half is the harder part, and Hatcher gives it in full on pp. 44–46. The apparatus is factorisations: a formal product [f1] ··· [fk] of classes of loops each lying in a single Aα, whose product in π₁(X) is the given [f]. Two moves are allowed on a factorisation — combining or splitting adjacent terms that happen to lie in the same Aα, and reinterpreting a term that lies in Aα ∩ Aβ as living in the other set — and two factorisations are equivalent if a finite sequence of such moves connects them. The theorem then reduces to a single claim: any two factorisations of the same element are equivalent. To prove it, one takes a homotopy F : I × I → X between two loops, subdivides the square into small rectangles each mapped into a single Aij, and pushes a path across the square one rectangle at a time. At each vertex of the subdivision one must choose a connecting path gv lying in the intersection of the two or three sets meeting there — and that is precisely where the triple-intersection hypothesis is spent.

Guided proof. Write it out before revealing the model answer.

Prove the surjectivity half of Theorem 1.20 from Lemma 1.15 — that is, deduce that Φ : ∗α π₁(Aα) → π₁(X) is onto. Then explain, in two or three sentences, why the same argument gives no information whatever about the kernel.

Reveal the model answer

Surjectivity. Let [f] ∈ π₁(X, x0) be arbitrary. The hypotheses of Theorem 1.20 include those of Lemma 1.15, so f is homotopic to a product of loops f1 ··· fk, each fi a loop at x0 contained in a single Aαi. Because fi lies in Aαi it determines a class [fi]A ∈ π₁(Aαi), and by construction jαi[fi]A = [fi] in π₁(X). Now let w = [f1]A ··· [fk]A, a word in ∗α π₁(Aα) — it need not be reduced, but reduction does not change the element. Since Φ is a homomorphism restricting to jα on each factor,

Φ(w) = jα1[f1]A ··· jαk[fk]A = [f1] ··· [fk] = [f1 ··· fk] = [f].

So every element of π₁(X) is in the image, and Φ is onto. ∎

Why this says nothing about the kernel. The argument is entirely constructive in one direction: given a loop, it produces a word mapping to it. To control the kernel you need the opposite kind of statement — that any two words with the same image are related by the allowed moves — and nothing above compares two factorisations of the same class. Indeed the word w produced is not canonical: it depends on the partition of I, on which Aα was picked when a piece lay in several, and on the connecting paths gi. Every one of those choices is a way of getting a different word with the same image, so the construction actively manufactures kernel elements rather than describing them. Pinning the kernel down requires the homotopy F : I × I → X and the subdivision argument, which is why that part of the proof is three pages rather than one paragraph — and why it needs a hypothesis the surjectivity half does not.

Self-check 2. In the two-set case with A ∩ B simply-connected, what does Theorem 1.20 reduce to?

N is generated by the elements iαβ(ω) iβα(ω)−1 for ω ∈ π₁(A ∩ B). If π₁(A ∩ B) = 0 there is only ω = 1 to feed in, so N is trivial and Φ is an isomorphism. The direct product is the wrong answer for a reason worth naming: it would say that a loop in A commutes with a loop in B, and in the figure eight it plainly does not — going round A then B is not homotopic to going round B then A. As for the last option, with only two sets there are no distinct triples, so the hypothesis is vacuously satisfied rather than unverifiable.

Self-check 3. Hatcher's second counterexample takes X = the suspension of three points, with Aα, Aβ, Aγ the complements of the three points. Using the cover of two of them gives π₁(X) ≅ ℤ ∗ ℤ; using all three would give ℤ ∗ ℤ ∗ ℤ. What is the force of this example?

The two groups are not isomorphic: they abelianise to ℤ2 and ℤ3, and the rank of a free abelian group is well defined, which is exactly why Hatcher bothers to record that fact on p. 42. So the illegal application produces a false statement, not merely an unjustified one. That distinction matters when you are using a theorem as a computational tool: a hypothesis whose failure only breaks the proof can sometimes be worked around, and a hypothesis whose failure breaks the conclusion cannot. Here X is the theta graph — two vertices joined by three arcs — and the triple intersection is the whole graph minus one interior point of each arc, which falls into two components, one around each vertex.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Cell complexes: from a picture to a presentation

The theorem becomes a machine once it is pointed at cell complexes. Attach 2-cells e2α to a path-connected X along maps φα : S1 → X, giving Y. The attaching maps are loops, but at scattered basepoints φα(s0), so choose a path γα in X from x0 to each and form the honest loop γαφαγ̄α at x0. Each such loop becomes nullhomotopic once its cell is glued on, so the normal subgroup N ⊂ π₁(X, x0) they generate lies in the kernel of π₁(X) → π₁(Y).

Proposition 1.26. (a) If Y is obtained from X by attaching 2-cells as above, then the inclusion X ↪ Y induces a surjection π₁(X, x0) → π₁(Y, x0) whose kernel is N; thus π₁(Y) ≅ π₁(X)/N. (b) If Y is obtained from X by attaching n-cells for a fixed n > 2, the inclusion induces an isomorphism π₁(X, x0) ≅ π₁(Y, x0). (c) For a path-connected cell complex X, the inclusion of the 2-skeleton X2 ↪ X induces an isomorphism π₁(X2, x0) ≅ π₁(X, x0). — Hatcher §1.2, p. 50

Part (b) is the part that should feel surprising, and its proof explains it in one line. The proof of (a) enlarges Y to a space Z that deformation retracts onto it, covers Z by A = Z minus a point of each cell and B = Z − X, and applies Theorem 1.20; the cover works because A retracts onto X and B is contractible. Running the same plan with n-cells, the only change is that the relevant piece deformation retracts onto Sn−1 instead of S1, and π₁(Sn−1) = 0 once n > 2. So the intersection is simply-connected, N is trivial, and nothing happens to π₁. Cells of dimension three and above are invisible to the fundamental group. That single sentence is why part (c) holds, and it is also the first honest indication that π₁ is not going to be enough — a fact this module spends chapter 2 responding to.

Worked example: the closed orientable surface of genus g

at1-02 built Mg as a cell complex: one 0-cell, 2g 1-cells, one 2-cell. Now convert it to a group.

Step 1 — the 1-skeleton. The 1-skeleton is 2g circles wedged at the single 0-cell. By Example 1.21 with Theorem 1.7 for each factor, π₁ of it is free on 2g generators a1, b1, ···, ag, bg.

Step 2 — the attaching map. The 2-cell is attached along the loop spelling the product of commutators [a1, b1] ··· [ag, bg].

Step 3 — Proposition 1.26(a). With one cell there is one relator, and no choice of γα to worry about since the attaching map is already based at the 0-cell. So

π₁(Mg) ≅ ⟨ a1, b1, ···, ag, bg | [a1, b1] ··· [ag, bg] ⟩,

where ⟨ gα | rβ ⟩ means the free group on the gα modulo the normal subgroup generated by the words rβ.

Step 4 — extract an invariant. A presentation is hard to compare with another presentation, so abelianise. Every commutator dies, the single relator dies with them, and the abelianisation is free abelian of rank 2g. Rank is well defined, so it distinguishes the surfaces.

Corollary 1.27. The surface Mg is not homeomorphic, or even homotopy equivalent, to Mh if g ≠ h. — Hatcher §1.2, p. 51

Proof. The abelianisation of π₁(Mg) is the direct sum of 2g copies of ℤ. If Mg ≃ Mh then π₁(Mg) ≅ π₁(Mh), so the abelianisations are isomorphic, so g = h. ∎

Three lines, and they settle a question that is not obviously finite. Note what is being ruled out: not merely homeomorphism but homotopy equivalence, which is a weaker relation and therefore a stronger thing to exclude. Note also the division of labour — van Kampen produced the presentation, and abelianisation converted it into something comparable. Neither step alone would have done it.

The non-orientable surfaces go the same way. Attaching a 2-cell to a wedge of g circles along a12 ··· ag2 gives Ng, with π₁(Ng) ≅ ⟨ a1, ···, ag | a12 ··· ag2 ⟩. Hatcher computes the abelianisation on p. 52 by rechoosing generators as a1, ···, ag−1 and a1 + ··· + ag, the last satisfying 2(a1 + ··· + ag) = 0; the result is ℤ2 plus g − 1 copies of ℤ. Hence no two Ng are homotopy equivalent, and no Ng is homotopy equivalent to any Mh — the M's abelianise to free abelian groups and the N's all carry torsion. N1 is ℝP2 and N2 is the Klein bottle.

Every group is a fundamental group

Corollary 1.28. For every group G there is a 2-dimensional cell complex XG with π₁(XG) ≅ G. — Hatcher §1.2, p. 52

Proof. Choose a presentation G = ⟨ gα | rβ ⟩, which exists since every group is a quotient of a free group: take the gα to be generators of that free group and the rβ to generate the kernel of the map onto G. Construct XG from ⋁α S1α by attaching 2-cells e2β along the loops spelling the words rβ. ∎

Take a moment with this. It is a two-line proof of an extremely strong statement, and it is worth being precise about what it costs, because the tempting overstatement is false. Corollary 1.28 says that the assignment from 2-dimensional cell complexes to groups is onto: no group is excluded. It does not say that π₁ is uninformative. π₁ still separates S1 from S2, still distinguishes the surfaces Mg from one another, and in at1-06 it will classify the covering spaces of a decent space outright. The loss is at the level of the class, not of the individual space: knowing that X is a 2-complex places no restriction at all on π₁(X), so π₁ can never certify that a given group fails to arise, nor that some space is not a 2-complex. Compare the graph case, where π₁ is always free. There "free" is a genuine necessary condition — a connected graph with non-free π₁ does not exist — and a necessary condition is exactly the thing Corollary 1.28 denies you in dimension 2. Problem 5(e) asks you to draw the moral, and the moral is one of the reasons this module moves on to homology: the target of Hn is abelian groups, which are classifiable in a way that arbitrary finitely presented groups are not, and a coarser invariant you can actually compare beats a finer one you cannot.

Example 1.29 makes the construction concrete. For G = ℤn = ⟨ a | an ⟩, XG is a circle with one 2-cell attached by z ↦ zn. At n = 2 this is ℝP2. For n > 2 it is not a surface at all: n sheets of the 2-cell meet at each point of the circle. Hatcher describes the n = 3 neighbourhood on p. 52 as the product of a three-pronged graph with an interval, its ends glued with a one-third twist.

Self-check 4. Why does Proposition 1.26(b) mean π₁ can never distinguish S2 from S3?

Sn is a 0-cell with one n-cell attached. For n = 2, Proposition 1.26(a) applies with X a point, so π₁(S2) is the trivial group modulo N, which is trivial. For n = 3, part (b) applies and says π₁ does not change from that of the point. Both are trivial, and they stay trivial for every higher sphere. So π₁ sees nothing whatever above dimension two — the last option is wrong, and knowing that it is wrong is precisely what needs an invariant π₁ cannot supply. That invariant is Hn, and distinguishing the spheres is one of the first things at1-07 does with it.

Self-check 5. Corollary 1.28 gives a 2-complex with any prescribed fundamental group. Which of the following does it therefore not establish?

Corollary 1.28 is a statement about surjectivity of the assignment "space ↦ group", not injectivity. It builds at least one complex for each group and says nothing at all about whether two complexes sharing a group must be related. They need not be: ℝP2 and the XG of ⟨a | a2⟩ happen to coincide, but in general the choice of presentation changes the complex while leaving the group fixed, and different presentations of one group can give non-homotopy-equivalent complexes. The other three options are all direct readings of the corollary — including the third, since one can present the trivial group with generators and relations to spare.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Pieces of a space do not commute, so the algebra is the free product. ∗α Gα is reduced words: no identity letters, no two adjacent letters from the same factor. Associativity comes free by embedding W in the permutation group P(W) via left multiplication and pulling back. The one property that gets used everywhere is the universal property: homomorphisms out of each factor glue uniquely to a homomorphism out of the free product. That is what makes Φ exist.

2. Theorem 1.20: two hypotheses, two conclusions, each buying one. Double intersections path-connected gives surjectivity of Φ, via Lemma 1.15 — compactness of I partitions a loop into pieces, and connecting paths in the double intersections turn the pieces into loops. Triple intersections path-connected gives that ker Φ is exactly N, the normal subgroup generated by iαβ(ω)iβα(ω)−1, via the subdivided-square argument where connecting paths are chosen at each vertex. Both hypotheses have counterexamples on p. 44, and the second one's counterexample returns a wrong answer, not merely an unproved one.

3. Pointed at cell complexes, the theorem becomes a presentation machine — and then hits its ceiling. Proposition 1.26: attaching 2-cells quotients π₁ by the normal closure of the attaching loops; attaching cells of dimension above 2 does nothing at all. So π₁(Mg) and π₁(Ng) are read straight off their cell structures, and abelianising turns the presentations into comparable invariants — Corollary 1.27. Corollary 1.28 then says every group is somebody's π₁, which is impressive and, as a classification tool, a warning: an invariant that takes every value constrains nothing, and cells above dimension two are invisible to it. Homology is the response.
