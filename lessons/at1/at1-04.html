The fundamental group of the circle and applications (at1-04)

π₁(S¹) = ℤ: winding number is a theorem, and it proves Brouwer's fixed point and the fundamental theorem of algebra.

at1-03 built a group and could not name a single non-trivial element of one. Hatcher was blunt about why: to show a fundamental group is non-trivial "one must somehow demonstrate the nonexistence of homotopies between certain loops", and every tool in that unit constructed homotopies rather than ruling them out.

The device that breaks the deadlock is almost embarrassingly concrete. Roll the real line up into a helix sitting above the circle, and let a loop downstairs drag a path upstairs behind it. A loop must return to where it started; its lifted path need not. The integer it fails by cannot change under a homotopy, because the endpoint of a lifted homotopy is pinned. That is the whole proof, and it delivers the fundamental theorem of algebra and Brouwer's fixed point theorem within two pages.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. A loop in S¹ lifts to a path in ℝ starting at 0 and ending at an integer n. Suppose you now deform the loop continuously, through loops based at the same point. What can the endpoint n do?

Pinned — and notice that "it is an integer, and integers cannot vary continuously" is not the argument, tempting as it is. The real reason is at1-03's clause (1): a homotopy of paths has ft(0) and ft(1) independent of t, full stop. What lifting supplies is fact (b): the lifted homotopy is again a homotopy of paths, so its endpoint is independent of t before you know anything about where endpoints live. Being integers is what makes the invariant useful; being pinned is what makes it an invariant. Problem 2(d) asks you to say what breaks if fact (b) delivered only a homotopy.

Why this matters for the mission: The first nontrivial computation — a hole detected algebraically, the template for all of persistent H_1.

What this unit's source does and does not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim. Its first two clauses are exactly this unit; the third reaches well past it.

What is proved here is that π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ — a fundamental group, not a homology group. Hatcher §1.1 contains no H₁ at all, and the relation between the two (H₁ is the abelianisation of π₁, Theorem 2A.1, §2.A, printed p. 166) is outside the resources of every at1 unit, as at1-03 recorded. Since π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ is already abelian the two agree for the circle, but that is a coincidence of this example rather than something established here.

There is no persistence in this unit and nothing that resembles a template for it. The hole is detected by a covering-space lift, which has no analogue in a persistence computation; persistent H₁ is computed by reducing a boundary matrix over 𝔽₂ across a filtration (tda1-02, tda1-09), and the filtration itself is tda1-01. The honest common thread is narrower and worth stating: both this proof and persistence detect a hole by producing an algebraic invariant that cannot change under the allowed deformation, and in both cases the whole force of the argument lies in showing the invariant is well defined. That is a shared strategy, not a template.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Lifting

Theorem 1.7. π₁(S¹) is an infinite cyclic group generated by the homotopy class of the loop ω(s) = (cos 2πs, sin 2πs) based at (1,0). — Hatcher §1.1, p. 29

Hatcher restates it in the form the proof will actually establish. Writing ωn(s) = (cos 2πns, sin 2πns), one has [ω]n = [ωn], so the theorem is equivalent to: every loop in S¹ based at (1,0) is homotopic to ωn for a unique n ∈ ℤ. Existence gives generation, uniqueness gives infinite order.

The comparison map is p : ℝ → S¹, p(s) = (cos 2πs, sin 2πs). Hatcher's picture: embed ℝ in ℝ³ as the helix s ↦ (cos 2πs, sin 2πs, s), and p is the restriction to the helix of the projection (x, y, z) ↦ (x, y). The loop ωn is the composition pω̃n, where ω̃n(s) = ns starts at 0 and ends at n, winding round the helix |n| times — upward if n > 0, downward if n < 0. The relation ωn = pω̃n is expressed by saying that ω̃n is a lift of ωn.

Covering space. Given a space X, a covering space of X consists of a space X̃ and a map p : X̃ → X satisfying: for each point x ∈ X there is an open neighbourhood U of x in X such that p−1(U) is a union of disjoint open sets each of which is mapped homeomorphically onto U by p. Such a U is called evenly covered. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 29

For p : ℝ → S¹, any open arc in S¹ is evenly covered.

Note how local the condition is, and that it must be. The whole circle is not evenly covered by p: ℝ is connected, so it is not a disjoint union of several open pieces, and it is not homeomorphic to S¹ either. Only small arcs work — and small arcs are all the proof needs, because I is compact.

The two lifting facts. For a covering space p : X̃ → X:

(a) For each path f : I → X starting at a point x0 ∈ X and each x̃0 ∈ p−1(x0) there is a unique lift f̃ : I → X̃ starting at x̃0.

(b) For each homotopy ft : I → X of paths starting at x0 and each x̃0 ∈ p−1(x0) there is a unique lifted homotopy f̃t : I → X̃ of paths starting at x̃0. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 29

Both are special cases of one statement, which Hatcher proves in general because "most of the arguments will apply in much greater generality":

(c) The general lifting property. Given a map F : Y × I → X and a map F̃ : Y × {0} → X̃ lifting F|Y × {0}, there is a unique map F̃ : Y × I → X̃ lifting F and restricting to the given F̃ on Y × {0}. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 30

Fact (a) is the case Y a point. Fact (b) is the case Y = I: the homotopy gives F(s,t) = ft(s); a unique lift on I × {0} comes from (a); then (c) gives a unique F̃ on I × I. The restrictions F̃|{0} × I and F̃|{1} × I are paths lifting constant paths, so by the uniqueness clause of (a) they are themselves constant — which is exactly why f̃t(s) = F̃(s,t) is a homotopy of paths and not merely a homotopy.

The construction of (c) is local-then-glue. Every point (y0, t) has a product neighbourhood Nt × (at, bt) whose image under F lies in an evenly covered neighbourhood; by compactness of {y0} × I finitely many cover it, so one may choose a single N and a partition 0 = t0 < t1 < ⋯ < tm = 1 with F(N × [ti, ti+1]) inside an evenly covered Ui. Then define F̃ inductively on N × [ti, ti+1] as the composition of F with the homeomorphism p−1 : Ui → Ũi. Uniqueness on a single segment {y} × I uses connectedness of [ti, ti+1] to force the two candidate lifts into the same sheet Ũi, where p is injective. Uniqueness on segments then makes the locally constructed lifts agree on overlaps, giving a well-defined lift on all of Y × I.

The sentence in that construction to remember is the one about constant paths, since it is where fact (b) earns its "of paths". The two vertical edges of the square lift to constant paths, so the lifted homotopy's endpoints cannot move. The prediction gate's answer lives there.

Visual: the helix, and a loop dragging its lift

Left, a loop in S¹ winding round n times. Right, the same loop lifted to the helix over the circle: it starts at 0 and ends at the integer n, and the projection downward is p. Change n and watch the endpoint move by whole units — it can never land between them.

Self-check 1. Where in the proof of (c) is the compactness of I used, and what would go wrong without it?

Each point (y0, t) gets its own product neighbourhood, and a priori the neighbourhoods Nt shrink as t varies, with no single N working for all of them. Compactness of {y₀} × I extracts a finite subcover, and a finite intersection of neighbourhoods of y₀ is still a neighbourhood — which is what makes the inductive construction along a finite partition possible. Without it the induction has no base to run along. Uniqueness, by contrast, uses connectedness of [ti, ti+1] rather than compactness.

Self-check 2. Fact (a) asserts existence and uniqueness of the lift. Which does the proof of Theorem 1.7 use for uniqueness of n?

Given a homotopy ft from ωm to ωn, fact (b) lifts it to some f̃t starting at 0. To conclude m = n you must know that f̃0 is the lift of ωm starting at 0, namely ω̃m, and similarly at the other end — and that is precisely the uniqueness clause of (a). Existence alone would leave f̃0 as some unidentified lift, with no reason for its endpoint to be m.

Segment 2 · ~25 min

The theorem, and the fundamental theorem of algebra

Worked example: the proof of Theorem 1.7

Surjectivity. Let f : I → S¹ be a loop at x0 = (1,0), representing a given element of π₁(S¹, x0). By (a) there is a lift f̃ starting at 0. This path ends at some integer n, since pf̃(1) = f(1) = x0 and p−1(x0) = ℤ ⊂ ℝ.

Another path in ℝ from 0 to n is ω̃n, and f̃ ≃ ω̃n via the linear homotopy (1 − t)f̃ + tω̃n — legitimate because ℝ is convex, which is at1-03's Example 1.1. Composing this homotopy with p gives a homotopy f ≃ ωn, so [f] = [ωn]. Note that composing with p preserves the property of being a homotopy of paths, because p is continuous and the endpoints upstairs were already fixed.

Uniqueness. Suppose f ≃ ωn and f ≃ ωm, so ωm ≃ ωn. Let ft be a homotopy from ωm = f0 to ωn = f1. By (b) this lifts to a homotopy f̃t of paths starting at 0, and the uniqueness part of (a) implies f̃0 = ω̃m and f̃1 = ω̃n. Since f̃t is a homotopy of paths, the endpoint f̃t(1) is independent of t. At t = 0 that endpoint is m and at t = 1 it is n. So m = n. ∎

What was actually used. Convexity of ℝ, for the existence half; clause (1) of a homotopy of paths, for the uniqueness half. Nothing else. The difficulty was never in the algebra — it was in constructing the covering space and proving the lifting property, which is why Hatcher warns in advance that "the proof will involve some genuine work".

Theorem 1.8 (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). Every nonconstant polynomial with coefficients in ℂ has a root in ℂ. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 31

Faded: the proof. We may assume the polynomial has the form p(z) = zn + a₁zn−1 + ⋯ + an. Suppose it has no roots in ℂ.

Step 1 — a family of loops. For each real r ≥ 0, the formula

fr(s) = [ p(re2πis)/p(r) ] / | p(re2πis)/p(r) |

defines a loop in the unit circle S¹ ⊂ ℂ based at 1. ⟨your step: where exactly is the hypothesis "no roots" used in making this well defined, and why is the basepoint 1?⟩

Step 2 — the class is zero. As r varies, fr is a homotopy of loops based at 1. Since f0 is the trivial loop, [fr] ∈ π₁(S¹) is zero for all r. ⟨your step: substitute r = 0 and check f0 really is constant⟩

Step 3 — the class is n. Fix r bigger than |a₁| + ⋯ + |an| and bigger than 1. Then for |z| = r,

|zn| > (|a₁| + ⋯ + |an|)|zn−1| ≥ |a₁zn−1| + ⋯ + |an| ≥ |a₁zn−1 + ⋯ + an|.

From |zn| > |a₁zn−1 + ⋯ + an| it follows that pt(z) = zn + t(a₁zn−1 + ⋯ + an) has no roots on |z| = r for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. ⟨your step: why does that inequality rule out roots of pt?⟩

Step 4 — collide the two. Replacing p by pt in the formula for fr and letting t run from 1 to 0 gives a homotopy from fr to ωn(s) = e2πins. By Theorem 1.7, ωn represents n times a generator of the infinite cyclic group π₁(S¹). ⟨your step: combine with Step 2 and conclude n = 0, hence that the only root-free polynomials are constants⟩

You try. The proof needs π₁(S¹) to be infinite cyclic, not merely non-trivial. Find the exact sentence where that matters, and construct the following counterfactual: if π₁(S¹) had turned out to be ℤ/5ℤ instead, what would the argument have proved about n, and would the theorem still follow?

Self-check 3. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra proof derives [ωn] = 0 and concludes n = 0. Which property of ℤ is being used?

In a cyclic group of order 5, n·[ω] = 0 would give only n ≡ 0 mod 5, and the argument would conclude nothing about a degree-7 polynomial. "Infinite cyclic" is exactly the hypothesis that makes n·[ω] = 0 imply n = 0 — which is the you-try above. This is why Theorem 1.7 is stated as "infinite cyclic generated by [ω]" rather than the weaker "non-trivial": the applications need different amounts of it, and Brouwer, as Segment 3 shows, needs only non-triviality.

Segment 3 · ~20 min

Brouwer, Borsuk–Ulam, and the ceiling

Theorem 1.9 (Brouwer, dimension 2). Every continuous map h : D² → D² has a fixed point, that is, a point x ∈ D² with h(x) = x. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 31

Proof. Suppose on the contrary that h(x) ≠ x for all x ∈ D². Then define r : D² → S¹ by letting r(x) be the point of S¹ where the ray in ℝ² starting at h(x) and passing through x leaves D². Continuity of r is clear since small perturbations of x produce small perturbations of h(x), hence also small perturbations of the ray through these two points. Note that the ray is well defined precisely because h(x) ≠ x gives it a direction.

The crucial property of r, besides continuity, is that r(x) = x if x ∈ S¹. Thus r is a retraction of D² onto S¹ — at1-01's notion, arriving with a job at last. It remains to show no such retraction exists.

Let f0 be any loop in S¹. In D² there is a homotopy of f0 to a constant loop, for example the linear homotopy ft(s) = (1 − t)f0(s) + tx0 where x0 is the basepoint of f0. Since the retraction r is the identity on S¹, the composition rft is a homotopy in S¹ from rf0 = f0 to the constant loop at x0. But this contradicts the fact that π₁(S¹) is nonzero. ∎

Two debts are settled by that argument. at1-01 asserted that no map gives a deformation retraction of the disk onto its boundary sphere and deferred the proof here; what has just been proved is stronger, since it rules out every retraction, and a deformation retraction would yield one by taking t = 1. And at1-03 could produce no space with non-trivial π₁; now the consequences start arriving.

Hatcher's historical notes are worth keeping. Brouwer proved this around 1910, and in fact for Dn; the generalisation is obtained as Corollary 2.15 "using homology groups in place of π₁", or alternatively with the higher homotopy group πn. Brouwer's own proof used neither, since neither had been invented, and instead used the degree of maps Sn → Sn, defined in §2.2 using homology but defined by Brouwer directly in geometric terms. Hatcher also flags that all these proofs are by contradiction and "show just the existence of fixed points without giving any clue as to how to find one in explicit cases", the same being true of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra proof; he points to Sperner's more constructive 1928 argument.

Theorem 1.10 (Borsuk–Ulam, dimension 2). For every continuous map f : S² → ℝ² there exists a pair of antipodal points x and −x in S² with f(x) = f(−x). — Hatcher §1.1, p. 32

Hatcher's gloss: it may be that there is only one such pair, for example if f is orthogonal projection of the standard S² ⊂ ℝ³ onto a plane. The theorem holds more generally for maps Sn → ℝn, shown as Corollary 2B.7. The case n = 1 is easy, since f(x) − f(−x) changes sign as x goes halfway round the circle, so the difference must vanish somewhere; for n ≥ 2 it is "certainly less obvious". His illustration: at every instant there must be a pair of antipodal points on the earth's surface with the same temperature and the same barometric pressure.

Corollary 1.11. Whenever S² is expressed as the union of three closed sets, at least one of them must contain a pair of antipodal points. — Hatcher §1.1, p. 33

The ceiling

Notice where the Brouwer argument stops. Run it in dimension n and it reduces to "no retraction Dn → Sn−1", which reduces in turn to π₁(Sn−1) ≠ 0. For n = 2 that is Theorem 1.7. For n ≥ 3 the sphere Sn−1 is simply-connected — van Kampen gives this in at1-05 — so the final contradiction evaporates and the proof simply fails. The failure is not a defect in the argument; it is π₁ reaching its ceiling. π₁ sees one-dimensional holes and is blind to the higher ones, which is exactly why Hatcher's generalisations are cited to homology rather than to a cleverer π₁ argument, and why at1-07 onwards exist.

Guided proof. Write it out before revealing the model answer.

Prove that there is no retraction r : D² → S¹, and then state carefully what this does and does not tell you about retractions Dn → Sn−1 for n ≥ 3.

Reveal the model answer

Claim. There is no continuous r : D² → S¹ with r|S¹ = 𝟙.

Proof. Suppose such an r exists. Fix a basepoint x0 ∈ S¹ and let f0 be any loop in S¹ at x0. Regarding f0 as a loop in D², the linear homotopy ft(s) = (1 − t)f0(s) + tx0 is a homotopy of loops in D²: each ft(s) is a convex combination of two points of the convex set D² and so lies in D², the endpoints are fixed at x0 for every t, and joint continuity holds because the operations are continuous. This is at1-03's Examples 1.1 and 1.4.

Compose with r. Since r is continuous, rft is a homotopy of loops in S¹. Since r|S¹ = 𝟙 and f0 has image in S¹, we get rf0 = f0; and rf1 is the constant loop at r(x0) = x0. So [f0] = 0 in π₁(S¹, x0).

As f0 was an arbitrary loop, π₁(S¹, x0) = 0, contradicting Theorem 1.7, which gives π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ. Hence no such r exists. ∎

What this says about n ≥ 3, and what it does not. The argument used exactly one fact about the boundary sphere: that its fundamental group is non-zero. Repeating it for Dn → Sn−1 would require π₁(Sn−1) ≠ 0, and for n − 1 ≥ 2 the sphere is simply-connected (at1-05), so the argument yields no contradiction. This does not show a retraction Dn → Sn−1 exists — none does — only that π₁ cannot rule one out. The correct invariant is Hn−1, and the resulting theorem is Hatcher's Corollary 2.15.

Self-check 4. The Brouwer proof and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra proof use Theorem 1.7 differently. How?

Brouwer's contradiction is that "every loop in S¹ is nullhomotopic", which any non-trivial group refutes — π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ/2ℤ would have served. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra instead derives n·[ω] = 0 for the degree n and needs to conclude n = 0, which requires [ω] to have infinite order. Reading a proof for which part of a cited theorem it actually consumes is a habit worth forming; it is what tells you which generalisations will survive.

Self-check 5. Hatcher calls these proofs non-constructive. Which step in the Brouwer proof is responsible?

Everything after the supposition is explicit: r is given by a formula in x and h(x), the homotopy is linear, the composition is concrete. What is not explicit is the conclusion. Having shown that h with no fixed point leads to a contradiction, one learns that a fixed point exists and nothing whatever about where. Hatcher makes the same remark about the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra proof and points to Sperner's 1928 argument as "somewhat more constructive". Note the contrast with Theorem 1.7 itself, which is constructive: given a loop, lift it and read off the endpoint.

Reveal the skeleton

1. A covering space converts loops into paths, and paths remember more. p : ℝ → S¹ with every open arc evenly covered. Facts (a) and (b) — unique lifting of paths and of homotopies of paths — both follow from the single statement (c) about F : Y × I → X, proved by compactness of I to get a finite partition, then induction sheet by sheet, then gluing by uniqueness on segments. The clause that matters is that the vertical edges lift to constant paths, so the lifted homotopy is a homotopy of paths.

2. That forces π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ. A loop lifts to a path from 0 to an integer n; ℝ is convex, so that lift is homotopic to ω̃n and pushing down gives [f] = [ωn] — surjectivity. If ωm ≃ ωn, lift the homotopy: uniqueness in (a) identifies its ends as ω̃m and ω̃n, and the fixed endpoint of a homotopy of paths gives m = n — injectivity. The first non-trivial fundamental group of the course.

3. The applications follow immediately, and then stop. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra collides two homotopies of the same loop, one giving class 0 and one giving class n, and needs the generator to have infinite order. Brouwer builds a retraction D² → S¹ from a fixed-point-free map and needs only that π₁(S¹) ≠ 0 — settling at1-01's deferred claim in the stronger form, since no retraction at all exists. Borsuk–Ulam and Corollary 1.11 come from the same lifting technique. All of it stops at dimension 2, because π₁(Sn−1) = 0 for n ≥ 3: the invariant sees one-dimensional holes only, which is the reason homology is coming.
