Simplicial and singular homology (at1-07)

Count the holes with linear algebra. That is the whole idea, and it works.

π₁ took four units and gave you one number per space, computed by hand, in a group that might be any group at all. Homology gives you a number in every dimension at once, and computes them by row-reducing integer matrices. That is not a small trade. The reason it is possible is a single algebraic identity — the boundary of a boundary is zero — and a single construction that turns an identity into a group: the quotient of what has no boundary by what is a boundary.

Dexter puts the appeal plainly at the top of his §7.1: these invariants are analogous to π₀ and π₁ but "only use linear algebra in the definitions, and are thus much simpler", the drawback being that they are "slightly less intuitive at first sight". Both halves of that are true, and this unit is the price and the payoff.

Prediction gate — commit before reading on. The boundary of an edge [v0, v1] is defined to be [v1] − [v0], with a minus sign, rather than [v0] + [v1]. Why the signs?

Hatcher is explicit on p. 105: one might wish to define the boundary as the plain sum of the faces, "however, it turns out to be better to insert certain signs", and heuristically the signs are there "to take orientations into account, so that all the faces of a simplex are coherently oriented". The payoff is Lemma 2.1. Drop the signs and it fails immediately: the unsigned double boundary of a triangle counts each vertex twice rather than cancelling, so Im ∂ would not sit inside Ker ∂ and the quotient defining Hn would not exist. The third option is therefore the tempting wrong one — the signs are a convention in the sense that one chooses where to put them, but not in the sense that they could be omitted. The chain groups are free whatever the boundary map does, so the last option confuses two independent parts of the construction.

Why this matters for the mission: This is homology proper: H_n = ker d_n / im d_(n+1), the object every persistence computation tracks.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Its first clause is this unit exactly — and the formula in it is Dexter's, written in his notation, from Defn (Simplicial homology group) in §7.1. The second clause is a forward pointer that neither source makes, and it is not quite right as stated.

Persistence is not in either source. Hatcher §2.1 and Dexter §7.1 contain no filtrations, no barcodes and no stability. Persistence is tda1's, and this lesson establishes nothing about it.

And "the object every persistence computation tracks" is a simplification worth naming now rather than unlearning later. Persistence does not track one homology group. It tracks the whole family of them attached to a growing filtration of complexes, together with the maps between them induced by the inclusions — and that family, not any single Hn, is what a barcode summarises. Two further differences will matter: persistence works over a field, usually 𝔽₂, where this unit works over ℤ, and the decomposition theorem that produces barcodes has no analogue over ℤ. That is exactly why the torsion you will compute for ℝP2 in Example 2.4 — the first interesting answer in the whole subject — is invisible to a standard 𝔽₂ barcode. The functoriality that makes the family a coherent object is at1-10; the coefficient question is at1-09.

So: this lesson builds Hn = Ker ∂n / Im ∂n+1 twice, proves the two constructions agree, and stops there.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Chains, the boundary map, and the identity everything rests on

Start with a Δ-complex X. Let Δn(X) be the free abelian group with basis the n-simplices of X. Its elements, the n-chains, are finite formal sums Σ nασα with integer coefficients — a finite collection of n-simplices with multiplicities. The multiplicities are allowed to be negative, and that is the first place orientation enters.

Boundary homomorphism. ∂n : Δn(X) → Δn−1(X) is defined on basis elements by

∂n(σα) = Σi (−1)i σα|[v0, ···, v̂i, ···, vn],

where the hat means the vertex is deleted. Each restriction is the characteristic map of an (n−1)-simplex of X, so the right side does lie in Δn−1(X). — Hatcher §2.1, p. 105

Write out the first three and check the pictures:

∂[v0, v1] = [v1] − [v0]
∂[v0, v1, v2] = [v1, v2] − [v0, v2] + [v0, v1]
∂[v0, v1, v2, v3] = [v1, v2, v3] − [v0, v2, v3] + [v0, v1, v3] − [v0, v1, v2]

Lemma 2.1. The composition Δn(X) → Δn−1(X) → Δn−2(X) given by ∂n−1∂n is zero. — Hatcher §2.1, p. 105

Proof. Expanding twice,

∂n−1∂n(σ) = Σj<i (−1)i(−1)j σ|[···, v̂j, ···, v̂i, ···] + Σj>i (−1)i(−1)j−1 σ|[···, v̂i, ···, v̂j, ···].

The two summations cancel: after switching the names i and j in the second sum it becomes the negative of the first. ∎

The exponent j − 1 in the second sum is the whole content, and it is worth pausing on. If you delete vi first, then every later vertex has shifted down one position, so vj for j > i now sits at index j − 1. That single index shift is what flips the sign and makes the cancellation work. Problem 1(b) asks you to write it out; do write it out, because the same computation is reused verbatim for singular simplices later in the section and never proved again.

Chain complex; homology. A sequence of homomorphisms of abelian groups ··· → Cn+1 → Cn → Cn−1 → ··· → C0 → 0 with ∂n∂n+1 = 0 for each n is a chain complex. The condition is equivalent to Im ∂n+1 ⊂ Ker ∂n, so one may define the nth homology group Hn = Ker ∂n / Im ∂n+1. Elements of Ker ∂n are cycles, of Im ∂n+1 are boundaries, of Hn are homology classes; two cycles are homologous when their difference is a boundary. With Cn = Δn(X) this is the simplicial homology group HnΔ(X). — Hatcher §2.1, p. 106

Dexter builds the same object from a slightly different starting point, and the difference is instructive. He works with simplicial complexes, where a simplex is determined by its vertex set, so he has to add orientation by hand: an oriented n-simplex is an (n+1)-tuple (a0, ···, an), with two tuples counted as the same oriented simplex when they differ by an even permutation. He then chooses an orientation for each simplex to get a basis of Cn(K), notes the choice does not matter but must be made, and identifies −σ with σ̄, the same simplex reversed. Hatcher's Δ-complexes carry an ordering of the vertices of each simplex as part of the structure, so the orientation is already there and no choice is needed. Same theory, one bookkeeping step moved.

Dexter's boundary map is written dn rather than ∂n, with the identical formula, and his homology group is Hn(K) = ker dn / im dn+1. That is the formula in this unit's mission strip, in his notation.

Modality note. Dexter states the lemma dn−1 ∘ dn = 0; his proof reads, in full, "This just involves expanding the definition and working through the mess." Hatcher proves it, and his proof is the one given above and the one you should learn. Where the two sources overlap, the numbered, proved version is Hatcher's.

Self-check 1. Which statement about the identity ∂∂ = 0 is correct?

∂n∂n+1 = 0 means exactly Im ∂n+1 ⊂ Ker ∂n — boundaries are cycles — and a quotient group needs the numerator to contain the denominator. The first option has it backwards, and inverting it is the whole subject: Hn measures the extent to which cycles fail to be boundaries, which is a failure of the inclusion to be an equality, not a failure of surjectivity. As for the last option, the proof above touches no space at all; it is an identity about a signed sum of index deletions, and that is precisely why the same proof works for singular simplices, where there is no geometry to appeal to.

Segment 2 · ~30 min

Computing, and the limits of the simplicial definition

Worked example: the torus and the projective plane

Both use the two-triangle Δ-complex structures from at1-02, and the contrast between them is the first genuinely interesting output of the subject.

The torus T (Example 2.3): one vertex, three edges a, b, c, two 2-simplices U, L.

Since there is one vertex, ∂1 = 0, so H0Δ(T) ≅ ℤ. Both triangles have the same boundary, ∂2U = a + b − c = ∂2L. Since {a, b, a + b − c} is a basis for Δ1(T), the image of ∂2 is the single summand generated by a + b − c, so

H1Δ(T) = Ker ∂1 / Im ∂2 ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ, on [a] and [b].

There are no 3-simplices, so H2Δ(T) = Ker ∂2. Now ∂(pU + qL) = (p + q)(a + b − c), which vanishes only when p = −q, so the kernel is infinite cyclic on U − L and H2Δ(T) ≅ ℤ.

The projective plane ℝP2 (Example 2.4): two vertices v, w, three edges a, b, c, two 2-simplices U, L.

Im ∂1 is generated by w − v, so H0Δ ≅ ℤ on either vertex. Here ∂2U = −a + b + c and ∂2L = a − b + c, which are different, and independent: ∂2 is injective, so H2Δ(ℝP2) = 0. That is the first difference from the torus, and it is orientability showing up as a computation.

For H1: Ker ∂1 ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ with basis a − b and c. Choose instead the basis c and a − b + c. Then Im ∂2 has basis a − b + c and 2c = (a − b + c) + (−a + b + c). So Im ∂2 is an index-two subgroup of Ker ∂1, and

H1Δ(ℝP2) ≅ ℤ2.

Where the torsion came from. One line: the coefficient 2 in 2c. Nothing about a hole is being counted here in the naive sense — the group is finite. A ℤ2 in H1 records a loop that is not a boundary but whose double is, which is exactly the behaviour of the generator of π₁(ℝP2) that at1-06 found by lifting to S2. Two entirely different methods, same 2.

Two more computations, both quick. S1 with one vertex and one edge has ∂1e = v − v = 0, so every boundary map is zero and the homology equals the chain groups: ℤ in degrees 0 and 1, zero above. Hatcher draws the general principle on p. 106 — if all boundary maps vanish, homology is the chain complex itself. And Sn, built from two copies of Δn glued along their boundaries by the identity, has Ker ∂n infinite cyclic on U − L, so HnΔ(Sn) ≅ ℤ. Hatcher adds, honestly, that the other groups "would be more difficult" for that structure — which is a hint that the simplicial definition is about to run out of road.

It runs out in two places. First, the groups are defined only for Δ-complexes, and the definition depends on the chosen structure. Hatcher raises the question directly on p. 107: are the HnΔ independent of the choice, and do homotopy equivalent complexes have isomorphic groups? Nothing so far answers either. Second, a structure has to exist and be small enough to compute with — Hatcher notes on p. 107 that a genuine simplicial complex structure on the torus needs at least 14 triangles, 21 edges and 7 vertices against the Δ-complex's 2, 3 and 1.

You try. Compute the Euler characteristic V − E + F of the torus from both structures: the Δ-complex with 1, 3, 2 and the simplicial complex with 7, 21, 14. Do they agree?

Then ask the harder question. You now have two structures giving the same three homology groups and the same alternating count. Is that a coincidence, a theorem, or a theorem you have not been given yet? Say precisely what you would need in order to settle it — and notice that the thing you need relates homology to a count of cells, which is at1-08's business, not this unit's.

Self-check 2. H2Δ(T) ≅ ℤ but H2Δ(ℝP2) = 0. Which computational fact is responsible?

∂2U = a + b − c = ∂2L on the torus, so their difference is killed; ∂2U = −a + b + c and ∂2L = a − b + c on ℝP2 are independent, so nothing is killed. H2 in top degree is just Ker ∂2, there being no 3-simplices, and a top-degree cycle is what orientability buys — the torus is orientable and ℝP2 is not, and this computation is that fact in arithmetic form. The vertex count is a red herring; it affects H0's computation and not H2. And there is no implication between torsion in one degree and vanishing in another: at1-08 will produce spaces with torsion in H1 and a non-zero H2.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Singular homology, and why it is worth the size

Singular homology. A singular n-simplex in X is just a map σ : Δn → X — "singular" because σ need not be a nice embedding and may have singularities where its image looks nothing like a simplex; all that is required is continuity. Let Cn(X) be the free abelian group on the singular n-simplices, with the boundary map given by the same formula as before. The proof of Lemma 2.1 applies unchanged, so ∂2 = 0 and one defines Hn(X) = Ker ∂n / Im ∂n+1. — Hatcher §2.1, p. 108

Two things follow at once, one good and one alarming. The good one: it is evident from the definition that homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic Hn, since nothing was chosen — in contrast to the situation for HnΔ. And it is defined for every space, not just for Δ-complexes.

The alarming one: Cn(X) is enormous. The number of singular n-simplices in a space is usually uncountable, and non-zero in every degree. So, as Hatcher puts it on p. 108, it is "not at all clear" that Hn(X) is finitely generated for a finite Δ-complex, or that it vanishes above the dimension — two properties trivial for HnΔ. The whole point of the rest of the section is to recover them.

The first results are nevertheless easy, and the ease is the lesson: one never manipulates the whole of Cn(X), only a finite formal sum.

Proposition 2.7. If X is non-empty and path-connected then H0(X) ≅ ℤ. Hence for any X, H0(X) is a direct sum of copies of ℤ, one per path-component. — Hatcher §2.1, p. 109

The proof introduces the map that recurs for the rest of the chapter: ε(Σ niσi) = Σ ni, the sum of the coefficients. One inclusion is immediate — ε∂1σ = 1 − 1 = 0. The other is the content: given Σ niσi with Σ ni = 0, join each point σi to a basepoint by a path τi, regard it as a singular 1-simplex, and compute ∂(Σ niτi) = Σ niσi − (Σ ni)σ0 = Σ niσi. Path-connectedness is used exactly once, to choose the τi. Problem 3(a) has you write it out.

A point has Hn = 0 for n > 0 and H0 ≅ ℤ, by Proposition 2.8: there is one singular simplex in each degree and the boundary maps alternate between isomorphisms and zero. That leftover ℤ in degree 0 is a nuisance often enough that Hatcher defines reduced homology H̃n(X) as the homology of the augmented complex ··· → C1(X) → C0(X) → ℤ → 0, with ε as the last map. Since ε∂1 = 0, this gives H0(X) ≅ H̃0(X) ⊕ ℤ and Hn ≅ H̃n for n > 0. X must be non-empty, or there would be a spurious group in degree −1.

Functoriality, and the theorem that costs something

A map f : X → Y sends a singular simplex σ to fσ, giving f♯ : Cn(X) → Cn(Y), and a one-line computation shows f♯∂ = ∂f♯ — a chain map. Chain maps take cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, so:

Proposition 2.9. A chain map between chain complexes induces homomorphisms between the homology groups of the two complexes. — Hatcher §2.1, p. 111

With the two formal properties (fg)∗ = f∗g∗ and 𝟙∗ = 𝟙, both immediate from associativity of composition.

Theorem 2.10. If two maps f, g : X → Y are homotopic, then they induce the same homomorphism f∗ = g∗ : Hn(X) → Hn(Y). — Hatcher §2.1, p. 111

Corollary 2.11. The maps f∗ : Hn(X) → Hn(Y) induced by a homotopy equivalence f : X → Y are isomorphisms for all n. — Hatcher §2.1, p. 111

Notice the difference in cost between the last two named results and the one before them. Proposition 2.9 is a formality — cycles go to cycles because f♯ commutes with ∂, and that is the entire argument. Theorem 2.10 has to convert a continuous homotopy into an algebraic one, a chain homotopy, and that construction — the prism operator, and Proposition 2.12, that chain-homotopic chain maps induce the same map on homology — is where the topology actually enters. Corollary 2.11 then follows formally from Theorem 2.10 and the two properties above, and in particular a contractible space has H̃n = 0 for all n.

Guided proof. Write it out before revealing the model answer.

Deduce Corollary 2.11 from Theorem 2.10 and Proposition 2.9. Then explain carefully why Corollary 2.11 does not, on its own, answer Hatcher's question from p. 107 about whether HnΔ(X) depends on the chosen Δ-complex structure — and name the extra theorem that closes the gap.

Reveal the model answer

Corollary 2.11. Let f : X → Y be a homotopy equivalence with homotopy inverse g, so gf ≃ 𝟙X and fg ≃ 𝟙Y. Applying Theorem 2.10 to the first homotopy, (gf)∗ = (𝟙X)∗. By the two formal properties this reads g∗f∗ = 𝟙 on Hn(X). Symmetrically f∗g∗ = 𝟙 on Hn(Y). So f∗ has a two-sided inverse and is an isomorphism, for every n. ∎

Why that is not yet an answer. Corollary 2.11 is a statement about singular homology, and about maps between spaces. The question on p. 107 is about simplicial homology, and about two Δ-complex structures on one space. These are different functors of different inputs: HnΔ is not even defined for a general space, only for a space equipped with a structure, so there is nothing for Corollary 2.11 to be applied to. Nothing proved so far connects the two.

What closes it. Theorem 2.27, that the natural map HnΔ(X, A) → Hn(X, A) is an isomorphism for all Δ-complex pairs. With that bridge the argument runs in three steps: each structure gives HnΔ ≅ Hn by Theorem 2.27; Hn is defined without reference to any structure; therefore any two structures give groups isomorphic to a common third group, hence to each other. Homotopy invariance then transfers along the same bridge, which answers the second and stronger form of the question too. The order matters: the bridge does the work, and Corollary 2.11 is what the bridge makes useful.

Theorem 2.27. The homomorphisms HnΔ(X, A) → Hn(X, A) are isomorphisms for all n and all Δ-complex pairs (X, A). — Hatcher §2.1, p. 128

Stated, not proved here. Hatcher's proof runs by induction over skeleta and uses two pieces of machinery this unit does not develop: the long exact sequence of a pair, and excision. Both are in §2.1, and neither is in at1-07's scope; the exact-sequence toolkit is at1-09's. What matters now is what the theorem buys, which is exactly the two-way trade the section has been setting up. Simplicial homology is computable — finite matrices, finitely generated answers, zero above the dimension — but depends on a structure and only exists for complexes. Singular homology is defined for every space and is manifestly a topological invariant, but is built from an uncountable basis and offers no way to compute anything. Theorem 2.27 says you never have to choose: compute simplicially, conclude singularly.

One tempting shortcut is worth explicitly closing off. Hatcher points out on p. 108 that singular homology can be regarded as a special case of simplicial homology, via the singular complex S(X) — the Δ-complex with one n-simplex per singular n-simplex of X — for which HnΔ(S(X)) is identical to Hn(X). True, and useless for computing: S(X) is, in his words, "usually an extremely large object compared to X". It is a change of description, not a bridge. Theorem 2.27 is the bridge, and it is a theorem rather than an observation.

Self-check 3. Which property does singular homology have that simplicial homology does not?

The other three are simplicial advantages, and Hatcher says so directly on p. 108: finite generation and vanishing above the dimension are "trivial" for HnΔ and "not at all clear" for Hn. Row reduction needs a finite basis, which singular chains do not have. What singular homology has instead is that nothing was chosen — the definition uses only the space — so invariance is free rather than earned. Theorem 2.27 is what stops this from being a dilemma, and the reason it is the last theorem of the section rather than the first is that its proof needs most of the section's machinery.

Self-check 4. Hatcher gives a geometric reading of cycles on p. 109: an n-cycle ξ builds a Δ-complex Kξ with a map to X. How far does the manifold picture extend?

Hatcher is careful about exactly this. Pairing off the cancelling faces of a cycle gives a Kξ that is a manifold near everything outside the (n−2)-skeleton, and closer inspection extends this across the interiors of (n−2)-simplices, so the non-manifold points lie in the (n−3)-skeleton — but "near points in the interiors of (n−3)-simplices it can very well happen that Kξ is not a manifold." So the picture is exact in low degrees: H1 classes are collections of oriented loops and H2 classes are maps of closed oriented surfaces, both of which he states outright. It is the extension to all higher dimensions that fails, and he says why he mentions it: in the early days of the subject it "may have been believed, or at least hoped" that the connection continued, and the theory built from manifolds instead — bordism — turns out to be considerably more complicated than homology. The first option is the hope, not the fact.

Self-check 5. You have π₁ from at1-05 and H1 from this unit. For the figure eight, π₁ ≅ ℤ ∗ ℤ. What should you expect of H1, and on what authority?

H1 is an abelian group by construction — it is a subquotient of a free abelian group — so it cannot be the non-abelian ℤ ∗ ℤ, and the expected answer is the abelianisation ℤ ⊕ ℤ. But expected is the operative word: the theorem that H1 is the abelianisation of π₁ is Hatcher's Theorem 2A.1, printed p. 166, and §2.A is not among the resources of at1-07 or of any other at1 unit. This is the same gap at1-03's mission strip ran into. What you can do without it is compute H1 of the figure eight directly from a Δ-complex structure with one vertex and two edges, which takes about a line, and observe that it agrees. The figure eight is a perfectly good Δ-complex, so the last option is simply false.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Signed faces make ∂∂ = 0, and ∂∂ = 0 makes homology exist. ∂nσ = Σi(−1)iσ|[v0,···,v̂i,···,vn]. Lemma 2.1: expanding twice gives a j < i sum and a j > i sum, the latter carrying an extra sign because deleting vi first shifts vj down one index; swapping names makes them cancel. That gives Im ∂n+1 ⊂ Ker ∂n, hence Hn = Ker ∂n/Im ∂n+1. Dexter's dn is the same map with orientation supplied by hand rather than by the Δ-structure, and his lemma is stated rather than proved.

2. Simplicial homology computes, and its two best examples disagree in a way that matters. The torus: ∂2U = ∂2L, so U − L is a 2-cycle and H2 ≅ ℤ, with H1 ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ. ℝP2: the two boundaries are independent, ∂2 is injective, H2 = 0, and Im ∂2 sits with index two inside Ker ∂1, giving H1 ≅ ℤ2. Torsion, from a single coefficient 2. But the groups are defined only for Δ-complexes and only relative to a chosen structure.

3. Singular homology fixes the definition and breaks the computation; Theorem 2.27 repairs it. Cn(X) is free on all maps Δn → X: defined for every space, invariant with nothing chosen, and uncountable. Proposition 2.7 gives H0; reduced homology removes the leftover ℤ; Proposition 2.9 gives functoriality for free, and Theorem 2.10 gives homotopy invariance at real cost, with Corollary 2.11 following formally. Theorem 2.27 identifies the two theories, so one computes simplicially and concludes singularly — and only then is the p. 107 question about dependence on the structure actually answered.
