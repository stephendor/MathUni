Cellular homology and degree (at1-08)

at1-07 left you with a definition that computes nothing. Singular homology is defined for every space and depends on no choices, and its chain groups are free on all maps Δn → X — usually uncountably many, in every degree at once. Theorem 2.27 rescued the situation for Δ-complexes. This unit does much better. For a CW complex the chain group in degree n is free abelian on the n-cells, of which there are typically a handful, and the boundary maps are integers you get by counting how many times one sphere wraps around another. ℝP4 has five cells. Its entire homology falls out of a chain of five copies of ℤ.

Predict before we start. ℝP4 will turn out to have H∗ = (ℤ, ℤ2, 0, ℤ2, 0). A one-point space has H∗ = (ℤ, 0, 0, 0, 0). Predict: is there any degree in which the rank of Hn(ℝP4) differs from the rank of Hn(point)?

No. ℤ2 is finite, so it contributes no ℤ summand and has rank 0. The ranks are 1, 0, 0, 0, 0 on both sides, and the Euler characteristics are both 1. Everything that distinguishes ℝP4 from a point lives in the torsion, which the ranks do not see. Hold on to that; it is the last thing this unit does, and it is the reason the mission strip below needs qualifying.

Why this matters for the mission: Cellular homology is the hand-computable engine; Betti numbers are free ranks, exactly aa-22's invariants.

What this unit's sources do and do not establish. The mission strip is quoted verbatim, as the rubric requires. Its first clause is this unit exactly, and is Hatcher's own claim: cellular homology is, in his words, "a very efficient tool for computing the homology groups of CW complexes, based on degree calculations" (p. 137). The second clause is true and sourced, but not from §2.2 — Hatcher defines the nth Betti number as the number of ℤ summands of Hn(X), on p. 130, at the tail of §2.1, which is at1-07's range and not this unit's. Theorem 2.44 uses the same notion under the name rank. Both are cited where used.

"Exactly aa-22's invariants" is a claim neither source makes, and it is a simplification in a direction this unit can correct on the spot. Betti numbers are half of an invariant, not all of one. In the same sentence in which he defines them, Hatcher defines the other half: the torsion coefficients, the orders of the finite cyclic summands. This matters here because the unit's headline computation is entirely torsion. ℝP4 has Betti numbers 1, 0, 0, 0, 0 and Euler characteristic 1 — those of a one-point space — while its homology is (ℤ, ℤ2, 0, ℤ2, 0). Reporting free ranks would report nothing whatever about ℝP4. Whether the free ranks are "exactly" the invariants of aa-22 is a cross-semester identification the syllabus makes and Hatcher does not; take it as a pointer, not a result.

One hypothesis in the strip that is silent and should not be. "Rank" is defined for finitely generated abelian groups; Hatcher says so explicitly when stating Theorem 2.44, and the theorem is stated for finite CW complexes. Finite generation is not free in general — it is bought here by Lemma 2.34(a), which makes each cellular chain group free abelian on finitely many cells.

Machinery borrowed from §2.1. This unit's resources name Hatcher §2.2 only, but Lemma 2.34 cannot be proved without relative homology, the long exact sequence of a pair (Theorem 2.16), excision (Theorem 2.20), good pairs (Proposition 2.22) and Corollary 2.25 on wedge sums — all §2.1, all listed in at1-07's range and none in this unit's. They are used, cited by number at each use, and itemised in the footer. The syllabus gap is recorded in the branch plan rather than hidden by pretending the proofs are self-contained.

Segment 1 · ~25 min

Degree: reading an induced map as an integer

Degree. For n > 0 and a map f : Sn → Sn, the induced map f∗ : Hn(Sn) → Hn(Sn) is an endomorphism of an infinite cyclic group, so it must be f∗(α) = dα for a single integer d depending only on f. That integer is the degree of f, written deg f. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 134

Notice what has and has not happened. No new construction has been made. Degree is a reading of f∗, and it is available only because the target group happens to be ℤ. Two facts are doing the work: Hn(Sn) is infinite cyclic, and f∗ is a homomorphism from that group to itself. Drop either and the definition collapses — at n = 0, for instance, H0(S0) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ, and "multiplication by d" is not forced.

Hatcher's seven properties are all one line each, and it is worth seeing how little they cost.

Properties of degree. — Hatcher §2.2, pp. 134–135

(a) deg 𝟙 = 1, since 𝟙∗ = 𝟙.
(b) deg f = 0 if f is not surjective: choose x0 outside the image and factor f through Sn − {x0}, which is contractible, so Hn of it is 0 and f∗ = 0.
(c) f ≃ g ⇒ deg f = deg g, since f∗ = g∗. The converse is Hopf's theorem, proved much later, and is not formal.
(d) deg fg = deg f · deg g, since (fg)∗ = f∗g∗. Hence a homotopy equivalence has degree ±1.
(e) A reflection of Sn, fixing an Sn−1 and swapping the complementary hemispheres, has degree −1: give Sn a Δ-structure with the two hemispheres as its n-simplices, so that Δn1 − Δn2 generates Hn(Sn), and the reflection swaps them.
(f) The antipodal map has degree (−1)n+1, being a composite of n+1 reflections.
(g) If f has no fixed points then deg f = (−1)n+1, because f is then homotopic to the antipodal map along the straight line from f(x) to −x, which misses the origin.

Property (c) is the one to be careful with, and property (d) is the one that does the most work later. (c) runs one way only: equal degrees do not by themselves give a homotopy, and the fact that they do is a theorem of Hopf from around 1925 that Hatcher does not reach until Corollary 4.25. When you find in Segment 3 that two attaching maps have the same degree, you have learnt something about the boundary map and nothing yet about the maps.

Theorem 2.28. Sn has a continuous field of nonzero tangent vectors if and only if n is odd. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 135

Proof. Given such a field v, normalise so |v(x)| = 1. Tangency means x and v(x) are orthogonal in ℝn+1, so ft(x) = (cos t)x + (sin t)v(x) has |ft(x)|2 = cos2t + sin2t = 1 — the cross term 2 cos t sin t ⟨x, v(x)⟩ vanishing is exactly where orthogonality is spent. Letting t run from 0 to π gives a homotopy from 𝟙 to the antipodal map, so (−1)n+1 = 1 and n is odd. Conversely for n = 2k−1, v(x1, …, x2k) = (−x2, x1, …, −x2k, x2k−1) is orthogonal to x and of unit length. ∎

That is the hairy ball theorem, and the whole proof is one homotopy and one parity. Proposition 2.29 is the same trick with a homomorphism instead of a homotopy: an action of a group G on Sn gives a degree function d : G → {±1}, which is a homomorphism by property (d); if the action is free, property (g) sends every non-trivial element to (−1)n+1. For n even that is −1, so d has trivial kernel and G embeds in ℤ2. Note carefully what happens for n odd: the same computation sends everything to +1 and tells you nothing at all. That is not a gap in the proof — the lens spaces of Example 2.43 are quotients of odd-dimensional spheres by free ℤm actions for every m, so the conclusion is genuinely false there.

Self-check 1. Property (b) says a non-surjective map has degree 0. Does it follow that a degree-0 map is non-surjective?

No. Example 2.31 builds a map of any prescribed degree by collapsing the complement of k disjoint balls and choosing signs on the local degrees; choosing them to cancel gives a surjective map of degree 0. Property (b) is a sufficient condition for degree 0, not a characterisation of it — and the habit of asking "does this implication reverse?" is the same habit property (c) needs.

You try. Property (g) says a fixed-point-free map has degree (−1)n+1. Use it to prove the following: every map f : S2 → S2 of degree 1 has a fixed point. Then decide whether the same argument works on S3, and if not, produce a fixed-point-free map of S3 of degree 1.

Then the harder half: Theorem 2.28's forward direction and property (g) both build a homotopy to the antipodal map. Compare the two constructions and say what each one needs that the other does not.

Segment 2 · ~30 min

Local degree, and the chain complex made of cells

Degree so far is defined but not computed. The tool that computes it is local, and it is the technical heart of the unit. Suppose f−1(y) is a finite set x1, …, xm. Choose disjoint neighbourhoods Ui of the xi, all mapped into a neighbourhood V of y. Hatcher's diagram on p. 136 has four isomorphisms in it: the upper two come from excision (Theorem 2.20, §2.1), cutting the pair (Sn, Sn − xi) down to (Ui, Ui − xi), and the lower two from the long exact sequence of a pair (Theorem 2.16, §2.1) together with the contractibility of a punctured sphere. Under all four, the two groups at the top become Hn(Sn) ≅ ℤ, and the top map f∗ becomes multiplication by an integer: the local degree deg f|xi.

Proposition 2.30. deg f = Σi deg f|xi. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 136

Proof sketch. Excision splits the central group Hn(Sn, Sn − f−1(y)) as the direct sum of the Hn(Ui, Ui − xi) ≅ ℤ, with ki the inclusion of the ith summand and pi the projection onto it — pikj = 0 for j ≠ i because that composite factors through Hn(Uj, Uj) = 0. The lower triangle gives pij(1) = 1 for every i, which pins j(1) = (1, …, 1) = Σiki(1). The upper square sends each ki(1) to deg f|xi, and the lower square reads the total off as deg f. ∎

Worked, then fading: deg(z ↦ zk) on S1 is k. — Example 2.32, Hatcher p. 137

Take k > 0 and any y ∈ S1. Then f−1(y) is k points x1, …, xk, and near each one f is a local homeomorphism that stretches a circular arc by a factor of k. Stretching is not degree: a deformation of f near xi removes it without changing the local degree, leaving a map that looks locally like a rotation. A rotation is a homeomorphism, so its local degree at any point equals its global degree; and a rotation is homotopic to the identity, so that global degree is +1. Hence deg f|xi = 1 for each i, and Proposition 2.30 gives deg f = k.

Now k = 0: f is constant, so it is not surjective, so property ⟨ … ⟩ gives deg f = 0 immediately.

And k < 0: write z ↦ zk as the composite of z ↦ z|k| with z ↦ z−1. The second map is a reflection of S1 — it fixes ⟨ how many points? name them ⟩ and swaps the two open arcs between them — so by property (e) its degree is ⟨ … ⟩. Property (d) then multiplies, giving deg f = ⟨ … ⟩.

Proposition 2.33 completes the picture: deg Sf = deg f for the suspension Sf : Sn+1 → Sn+1, by naturality of the boundary map in the long exact sequence of the pair (CSn, Sn). Suspending z ↦ zk repeatedly therefore produces a map Sn → Sn of degree k for every n ≥ 1. Hatcher then observes something easy to read past: Sf sends exactly one point to each pole of Sn+1, so its local degree at a pole equals its global degree. Since the global degree can be any integer, so can a local degree, once n ≥ 2.

Cells instead of simplices

Lemma 2.34. If X is a CW complex, then — Hatcher §2.2, p. 137

(a) Hk(Xn, Xn−1) is 0 for k ≠ n, and for k = n is free abelian with a basis in one-to-one correspondence with the n-cells of X;
(b) Hk(Xn) = 0 for k > n — so Hk(X) = 0 above dim X when X is finite-dimensional;
(c) the inclusion Xn ↪ X induces an isomorphism on Hk for k < n and a surjection for k = n.

Part (a) is the one that changes everything, and it is where the §2.1 machinery is spent. The pair (Xn, Xn−1) is a good pair (Proposition 2.22), so its relative homology is the reduced homology of the quotient Xn/Xn−1; and that quotient is a wedge of n-spheres, one per n-cell, whose homology is given by Corollary 2.25. Parts (b) and (c) then follow by running along the long exact sequences of the pairs (Xn, Xn−1) and reading off where the maps Hk(Xn−1) → Hk(Xn) are forced to be surjective (k ≠ n) or injective (k ≠ n−1). The infinite-dimensional case of (c) is harder and Hatcher gives two proofs, the second via a mapping telescope; neither is reproduced here.

Compare part (a) with at1-07's singular chain groups. There Cn(X) was free on every map Δn → X. Here the group in degree n is free on the n-cells. For ℝP4 that is a single generator per degree, and there are five degrees.

The cellular chain complex. Take the groups Hn(Xn, Xn−1), free abelian on the n-cells, and the maps dn = jn−1∂n, where ∂n : Hn(Xn, Xn−1) → Hn−1(Xn−1) is the boundary map of the pair and jn−1 : Hn−1(Xn−1) → Hn−1(Xn−1, Xn−2). Then dndn+1 = jn−1(∂njn)∂n+1 = 0, because ∂njn is two consecutive maps of a long exact sequence. The homology of this complex is the cellular homology HnCW(X). — Hatcher §2.2, pp. 139–140

Theorem 2.35. HnCW(X) ≅ Hn(X). — Hatcher §2.2, p. 140; see the footer on how this statement was read

Proof. From the diagram, Hn(X) is identified with Hn(Xn)/Im ∂n+1. Since jn is injective it carries Im ∂n+1 isomorphically onto Im dn+1, and Hn(Xn) isomorphically onto Im jn = Ker ∂n. Since jn−1 is injective, Ker ∂n = Ker dn. So jn induces an isomorphism from Hn(Xn)/Im ∂n+1 onto Ker dn/Im dn+1. ∎

Three consequences are immediate, and Hatcher lists them as (i)–(iii) on p. 140. (i) No n-cells means Hn(X) = 0. (ii) With k n-cells, Hn(X) is generated by at most k elements — a bound on generators, not on the group: one 1-cell gives a cyclic H1, and ℝP2's is ℤ2, not ℤ. (iii) If no two cells sit in adjacent dimensions, every dn vanishes and Hn(X) is free abelian on the n-cells. That last one settles ℂPn in a line — one cell in each even dimension up to 2n, so Hi(ℂPn) is ℤ for i = 0, 2, …, 2n and 0 otherwise — and settles Sn × Sn for n > 1 just as fast.

Cellular Boundary Formula. dn(enα) = Σβ dαβ en−1β, where dαβ is the degree of the composite Sn−1α → Xn−1 → Sn−1β: the attaching map of enα, followed by the quotient map collapsing Xn−1 minus the cell en−1β to a point. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 140

Hatcher states this one without a number, and it is the payoff of the whole segment: the boundary maps of the cellular complex are degrees, and degrees are computed by Proposition 2.30. The sum is finite because an attaching map has compact image and so meets only finitely many cells. For n = 1 no degree theory is needed — d1 is the simplicial boundary map Δ1(X) → Δ0(X), and if X is connected with a single 0-cell then d1 = 0, since otherwise H0(X) would not be ℤ.

Self-check 2. Application (ii) says a complex with one n-cell has Hn generated by at most one element. What does that tell you about H1(ℝP2), which at1-07 computed to be ℤ2?

Cyclic. The bound is on the number of generators, because Ker dn sits inside a free abelian group of rank k and Hn is a quotient of that kernel — and a quotient of a cyclic group is cyclic. Freeness is lost in the quotient, which is exactly how torsion arises. Application (iii) is the special case where no quotienting happens at all.

Self-check 3. Why is dndn+1 = 0?

Exactness. Writing dndn+1 = jn−1(∂njn)∂n+1, the bracketed composite runs Hn(Xn) → Hn(Xn, Xn−1) → Hn−1(Xn−1), two consecutive maps of the long exact sequence of the pair, so it is zero. Nothing is inherited from the singular complex: the cellular groups are not subgroups of the singular ones, which is why Theorem 2.35 has content.

Segment 3 · ~25 min

Computing, and what the computation throws away

The surfaces first, because they are quick and they show both outcomes. Mg, the closed orientable surface of genus g, has one 0-cell, 2g 1-cells and one 2-cell attached along [a1, b1]···[ag, bg]. So the complex is 0 → ℤ → ℤ2g → ℤ → 0. Here d1 = 0 (one 0-cell) and d2 = 0 as well, because every generator occurs in the commutator word together with its inverse, so each Δαβ has total exponent zero and is homotopic to a constant map, of degree 0. Both boundary maps vanish, so the homology is the chain complex: ℤ, ℤ2g, ℤ.

Ng, the non-orientable surface, has one 0-cell, g 1-cells and one 2-cell along a12a22···ag2. Again d1 = 0. But now each ai appears with total exponent 2, so each Δαβ is homotopic to z ↦ z2 and has degree 2 by Example 2.32: d2(1) = (2, …, 2). That is injective, so H2(Ng) = 0; and replacing the last basis vector of ℤg by (1, …, 1) gives H1(Ng) ≅ ℤg−1 ⊕ ℤ2. At g = 1 that is ℝP2 and H1 = ℤ2, agreeing with at1-07's simplicial computation, as Theorem 2.27 and Theorem 2.35 between them require.

The contrast is not an accident of the two words. Hn of a closed connected n-manifold is ℤ if it is orientable and 0 if not — Hatcher notes this on p. 142 and proves it in Theorem 3.26, well outside this module.

Guided proof. Derive the cellular boundary map of ℝPn. Recall from at1-02 the CW structure with one cell ek in each dimension k ≤ n, the attaching map of ek being the double cover φ : Sk−1 → ℝPk−1. Show that dk is multiplication by 1 + (−1)k. Use the Cellular Boundary Formula, Proposition 2.30, and property (f) of degree. Write your argument before revealing the model answer.

Reveal the model answer

By the Cellular Boundary Formula, dk is multiplication by the degree of the composite qφ : Sk−1 → ℝPk−1 → ℝPk−1/ℝPk−2 = Sk−1, where q is the quotient map. There is only one (k−1)-cell, so the sum over β has a single term.

Now compute deg qφ by local degrees. The map qφ restricts to a homeomorphism from each of the two components of Sk−1 − Sk−2 onto ℝPk−1 − ℝPk−2. So a generic point of the target has exactly two preimages, one in each component, and the local degree at each is ±1 because qφ is a local homeomorphism there.

The two restrictions are not independent: each is obtained from the other by precomposing with the antipodal map of Sk−1, which by property (f) has degree (−1)k. So if one local degree is 1, the other is (−1)k. Proposition 2.30 adds them: deg qφ = deg 𝟙 + deg(−𝟙) = 1 + (−1)k.

Hence dk = 2 for k even and dk = 0 for k odd. ∎

What to notice. The whole computation is one application of Proposition 2.30 to a two-point fibre, and the entire content is the parity of the antipodal map's degree. The ℤ2's that appear in ℝPn's homology — the first torsion in at1-07, and now the general case — trace back to that single sign.

Assembling: the cellular chain complex of ℝPn is a copy of ℤ in each degree 0 through n, with boundary maps alternating 0 and 2, starting from d1 = 0. The picture below is the case n = 4.

Read the homology straight off. H0 = ℤ/Im d1 = ℤ. H1 = Ker d1/Im d2 = ℤ/2ℤ = ℤ2. H2 = Ker d2/Im d3 = 0/0 = 0, since d2 is injective. H3 = Ker d3/Im d4 = ℤ/2ℤ = ℤ2. H4 = Ker d4 = 0. In general Hk(ℝPn) is ℤ for k = 0 and for k = n odd, ℤ2 for k odd with 0 < k < n, and 0 otherwise.

You try. Before the last theorem, do two things with the picture. First, redraw it for ℝP5 — one more ℤ on the left — and check that the odd top dimension changes the answer in degree 5, and say why. Second, predict the Euler characteristic of ℝP4 from the cell counts, and separately from the homology, before reading on.

Theorem 2.44. For a finite CW complex X, χ(X) = Σn(−1)n rank Hn(X), where χ(X) = Σn(−1)ncn is the alternating sum of the numbers of cells. — Hatcher §2.2, p. 146

The rank of a finitely generated abelian group is its number of ℤ summands. The proof is purely algebraic and rests on the fact — Hatcher leaves it as an exercise — that rank is additive on short exact sequences of finitely generated abelian groups.

So χ is computable from a cell structure and yet depends only on the homology, hence only on the homotopy type: the alternating sum cannot tell which CW structure produced it. For ℝP4, the cells give 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + 1 = 1, and the homology gives 1 − 0 + 0 − 0 + 0 = 1, since ℤ2 has rank 0.

What the ranks do not see

Now put the two computations side by side. ℝP4 has homology (ℤ, ℤ2, 0, ℤ2, 0). Its Betti numbers — the ranks, in the sense Hatcher defines on p. 130 — are 1, 0, 0, 0, 0. A one-point space has homology (ℤ, 0, 0, 0, 0) and Betti numbers 1, 0, 0, 0, 0. The Betti numbers agree in every degree. So do the Euler characteristics, both 1. Every free-rank invariant this unit has built is blind to the difference between ℝP4 and a point.

The difference is entirely in the torsion, and Hatcher names the missing data in the same sentence in which he names the Betti numbers: the torsion coefficients, the orders of the finite cyclic summands. Two integers, 2 and 2, in degrees 1 and 3. That is the whole of what makes ℝP4 interesting, and it is the whole of what a report of free ranks discards.

Two cautions, since this is the kind of observation that is easy to overstate. First, this is a statement about one space and one pair of degrees, not a claim that Betti numbers are generally useless — for Mg the Betti numbers 1, 2g, 1 carry the entire homology, because there is no torsion to lose. Second, it does not follow that ℝP4 and a point cannot be distinguished by rank data of any kind: at1-09 will change the coefficient ring, and over 𝔽2 the picture is completely different. What is established here is narrower and sharper: integral Betti numbers, and the Euler characteristic built from them, do not distinguish these two spaces.

Self-check 4. Theorem 2.44 says χ(X) can be computed from homology. What does that buy you that the cell-count definition does not?

Invariance. The cell-count definition is manifestly computable and manifestly dependent on a choice; the homology formula is manifestly independent of the choice and much harder to compute. Having both is the point, and it is the same move Theorem 2.27 made in at1-07 for simplicial versus singular homology: one definition to compute with, one to reason with, and a theorem identifying them.

Self-check 5. Application (iii) says that if no two cells are in adjacent dimensions, Hn(X) is free abelian on the n-cells. Why does ℝP4 not qualify?

Adjacency. (iii) works by making every dn vanish for want of anywhere to go; ℝP4 has a cell in each of the five dimensions, so nothing is forced and the boundary maps have to be computed — which is what the guided proof did. ℂPn, with cells only in even dimensions, is the case where (iii) applies and the answer is free in every degree. That is exactly the difference between a space with torsion and a space without.

Self-check 6. Example 2.38 builds a complex X from S1 ∨ S1 with two 2-cells attached along a5b−3 and b3(ab)−2; the matrix of d2 has determinant −1, so all reduced homology vanishes. Does it follow that X is contractible?

Not contractible. Hatcher exhibits a surjection from π₁(X) = ⟨a, b | a5b−3, b3(ab)−2⟩ onto the rotation group of the dodecahedron, of order 60, with kernel ℤ2; so π₁(X) has order 120. Such an X is called acyclic. The lesson is the converse direction of Corollary 2.11: homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms on homology, but isomorphic homology does not give a homotopy equivalence — not even when the homology is that of a point.

Reveal the skeleton

1. Degree turns an induced map into an integer, and local degree computes it. f∗ on Hn(Sn) ≅ ℤ is multiplication by deg f. The properties are one line each from functoriality, except (e) and (f), which need a Δ-structure and a count of reflections. Proposition 2.30 breaks the degree into a sum of local degrees over a finite fibre, using excision (Theorem 2.20) and the long exact sequence of a pair (Theorem 2.16) to identify the local groups with ℤ. Theorem 2.28 and Proposition 2.29 are what this alone already buys.

2. Cells make the chain groups small, and the Cellular Boundary Formula makes the maps computable. Lemma 2.34(a) — via good pairs and Corollary 2.25 — says Hn(Xn, Xn−1) is free abelian on the n-cells. With dn = jn−1∂n this is a chain complex, because ∂njn is two consecutive maps of an exact sequence; Theorem 2.35 identifies its homology with singular homology. The boundary entries dαβ are degrees of attaching maps composed with collapses, so Segment 1 computes Segment 2.

3. The computations are cheap, and the cheapest summary of them is the one that loses the most. Mg has both boundary maps zero; Ng has d2(1) = (2, …, 2) and picks up a ℤ2; ℝPn has dk = 1 + (−1)k, straight from the antipodal map's degree. Theorem 2.44 makes χ a homotopy invariant. But χ and the Betti numbers are built from ranks alone, and ℝP4 has the Betti numbers and the Euler characteristic of a point while having ℤ2 in degrees 1 and 3. The torsion coefficients are the other half of the invariant, and this unit's best example is entirely torsion.
