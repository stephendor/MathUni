## Problem 1 — if $n$ is odd, then $n^2+n$ is even

**Solution.** Assume $n$ is odd. By Definition 2.2, $n=2a+1$ for some integer $a$. Then
$$n^2+n = (2a+1)^2+(2a+1) = 4a^2+4a+1+2a+1 = 4a^2+6a+2 = 2(2a^2+3a+1).$$
Since $2a^2+3a+1$ is an integer, setting $k=2a^2+3a+1$ gives $n^2+n=2k$, so by Definition 2.2, $n^2+n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- Correctly writes $n=2a+1$ from Definition 2.2 — 0.2
- Correct algebraic expansion of $(2a+1)^2+(2a+1)$ — 0.4
- Factors out 2 and names the resulting integer explicitly (e.g. $k=2a^2+3a+1$) — 0.2
- States the conclusion invoking Definition 2.2 ("therefore $n^2+n$ is even") — 0.2

## Problem 2 — conjecture and prove: when is $mn$ even?

**Solution.** Conjecture: $mn$ is even if and only if $m$ is even or $n$ is even (equivalently, $mn$ is odd iff both $m,n$ are odd).

*("If" direction, by cases.)* Case 1: $m$ is even, so $m=2a$. Then $mn=2(an)$, and since $an$ is an integer, $mn$ is even. Case 2: $m$ is odd and $n$ is even, so $n=2b$. Then $mn=2(mb)$, even. These two cases exhaust "$m$ even or $n$ even" (by symmetry the case "$n$ even, $m$ odd" is covered; if both even, Case 1 applies). So in every case, $mn$ is even.

*("Only if" direction, contrapositive.)* Suppose neither $m$ nor $n$ is even, i.e. both are odd: $m=2a+1$, $n=2b+1$. Then
$$mn = (2a+1)(2b+1) = 4ab+2a+2b+1 = 2(2ab+a+b)+1,$$
which is odd. This proves the contrapositive of "$mn$ even $\Rightarrow$ ($m$ even or $n$ even)", establishing the "only if" direction. $\blacksquare$

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- States a correct conjecture (biconditional or equivalent odd/odd formulation) — 0.15
- "If" direction: correct case split and algebra for both cases — 0.35
- "Only if" direction: correct contrapositive setup (assume both odd) — 0.15
- "Only if" direction: correct algebra showing $mn$ odd, and explicit closing inference — 0.35

## Problem 3 — if $3n+5$ is even, then $n$ is odd

**Solution.** We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is not odd (i.e. $n$ is even), then $3n+5$ is not even (i.e. odd).

Assume $n$ is even, so $n=2a$ for some integer $a$. Then
$$3n+5 = 6a+5 = 2(3a+2)+1.$$
Since $3a+2$ is an integer, $3n+5$ is odd by Definition 2.2 — that is, not even. We have shown $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$; therefore, by the contrapositive (Theorem 5.14), $P\Rightarrow Q$: if $3n+5$ is even, then $n$ is odd. $\blacksquare$

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- States the intent to use the contrapositive and correctly identifies $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$ to prove — 0.3
- Correct algebra: $n=2a \Rightarrow 3n+5=2(3a+2)+1$ — 0.4
- Explicit final inference invoking the contrapositive (Theorem 5.14) to conclude $P\Rightarrow Q$ — 0.3

## Problem 4 — if $n^2$ odd then $n$ odd; and the converse fallacy

**Solution.** *(Main proof, contrapositive.)* Assume $n$ is not odd, i.e. $n$ is even, so $n=2a$. Then $n^2=(2a)^2=4a^2=2(2a^2)$. Since $2a^2$ is an integer, $n^2$ is even, i.e. not odd. This shows $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$; by the contrapositive, $P\Rightarrow Q$: if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.

*(Converse-fallacy discussion.)* Proving $P\Rightarrow Q$ never automatically proves $Q\Rightarrow P$, because these are logically independent statements — knowing an implication holds tells you nothing about whether its converse does. A concrete instance: "$n=4\Rightarrow n$ is even" is true, but its converse "$n$ is even $\Rightarrow n=4$" is false ($n=6$ is even but not 4). This is distinct from the contrapositive $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$, which *is* always logically equivalent to $P\Rightarrow Q$ — the converse and the contrapositive must not be confused. $\blacksquare$

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- Correct contrapositive setup for the main proof ($n$ even $\Rightarrow n^2$ even) — 0.25
- Correct algebra and explicit final inference for the main proof — 0.25
- Correctly identifies that $P\Rightarrow Q$ does not imply $Q\Rightarrow P$ (the converse fallacy), with a clear statement of the logical independence — 0.25
- Gives a concrete counterexample distinguishing converse from contrapositive — 0.25

## Problem 5 — $\sqrt2$ is irrational (proof problem)

**Solution.** Assume, for contradiction, that $\sqrt2$ is rational. Then $\sqrt2=p/q$ for some non-zero integers $p,q$ in lowest terms (no common divisor). Squaring: $2=p^2/q^2$, so
$$2q^2=p^2.$$
Since $q^2$ is an integer, $2\mid p^2$. By the lemma "if $2\mid k^2$ then $2\mid k$" (itself provable by the contrapositive argument of Problem 4's twin, or directly: if $k$ were odd, $k^2$ would be odd), $2\mid p$. Write $p=2k$ for some integer $k$. Substituting:
$$2q^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2, \quad\text{so}\quad q^2=2k^2.$$
Hence $2\mid q^2$, and by the same lemma, $2\mid q$. But now both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting the assumption that $p/q$ was in lowest terms (no common divisor). This contradiction shows $\sqrt2$ cannot be rational; therefore $\sqrt2$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

**Any logically valid alternative proof earns full marks** (e.g. via unique factorisation: if $\sqrt2=p/q$ then $2q^2=p^2$, and comparing the exponent of 2 in the prime factorisations of both sides gives an even number on the right and an odd number on the left — contradiction).

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- Correct contradiction setup: assumes $\sqrt2=p/q$ in lowest terms — 0.15
- Correct algebra to $2q^2=p^2$ and correctly deduces $2\mid p$ — 0.25
- Correctly substitutes $p=2k$ and deduces $2\mid q$ — 0.25
- Explicit final contradiction named (both $p,q$ even, violating lowest terms) and concluding sentence — 0.35

## Problem 6 — no smallest positive rational

**Solution.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $q$ is the smallest positive rational number. Consider $q/2$. Since $q>0$, we have $q/2>0$, and $q/2$ is rational (the ratio of the integer numerator of $q$ to twice its integer denominator, hence itself a ratio of integers). Also $q/2 < q$ since $q>0$. So $q/2$ is a positive rational number strictly smaller than $q$, contradicting the assumption that $q$ was the *smallest* positive rational. Therefore no smallest positive rational number exists. $\blacksquare$

**Rubric (total 1.0):**
- Correct contradiction setup: assumes a smallest positive rational $q$ exists — 0.2
- Constructs $q/2$ (or an equivalent valid smaller positive rational) explicitly — 0.3
- Verifies $q/2$ is positive and rational — 0.2
- Verifies $q/2<q$ and states the explicit contradiction with minimality, plus concluding sentence — 0.3
