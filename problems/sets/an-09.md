## Problem 1 (easy — finishing the lesson's proof)

Complete the proof of Theorem 3.4.2 that the Cantor set contains no isolated points.

(a) Given $x \in C$, argue that there exists $x_1 \in C \cap C_1$ with $x_1 \ne x$ and $|x - x_1| \le 1/3$.
(b) Show that for each $n \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists $x_n \in C \cap C_n$, different from $x$, with $|x - x_n| \le 1/3^n$.

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.3, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$x$ lies in one of the $2^n$ closed intervals making up $C_n$. That interval has two endpoints, and both of them belong to $C$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $x_n$ to be an endpoint of the component interval of $C_n$ containing $x$, choosing the endpoint that differs from $x$. The interval has length $1/3^n$, which bounds the distance. Then check Definition 3.2.4 directly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Endpoints of the components of $C_n$ survive every later stage, since removal only ever takes an open middle third and never an endpoint. So each such endpoint is in $C_m$ for all $m$, hence in $C$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Given $x \in C$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, let $I$ be the component of $C_n$ containing $x$; $I$ is closed of length $1/3^n$. At least one endpoint of $I$ differs from $x$ (they are distinct from each other), so choose such an endpoint and call it $x_n$. Then $x_n \in C$, $x_n \ne x$, and $|x - x_n| \le 1/3^n$ because both lie in $I$.

Hence $(x_n) \to x$ with every $x_n \in C$ and $x_n \ne x$, so $x$ is a limit point of $C$ by an-07's Theorem 3.2.5 and is not isolated. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $C$ has no isolated points, and with closedness this makes $C$ perfect.

State explicitly why endpoints are never removed — it is the one fact the construction gives you for free and the whole argument leans on it.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the same construction with a different fraction)

Repeat the Cantor construction starting from $[0,1]$, but at each stage remove the open middle *fourth* of each component.

(a) Is the resulting set compact? Is it perfect?
(b) Compute its length and its dimension, using the methods of Abbott's Section 3.1.

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.4, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Removing the middle fourth of an interval of length $\ell$ leaves two intervals, each of length $3\ell/8$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), nothing in the lesson's arguments used the fraction $1/3$ — reread them and check. For (b), the length is a geometric sequence; the dimension comes from asking by what factor you must magnify one copy to recover the whole, and how many copies appear.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Each stage $D_n$ is a union of $2^n$ closed intervals of length $(3/8)^n$, so the total length is $(3/4)^n \to 0$ by an-04's Example 2.5.3. Each $D_n$ is closed by an-07's Theorem 3.2.14, so $D = \bigcap D_n$ is closed and, being inside $[0,1]$, bounded — hence compact by an-08's Theorem 3.3.4.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Perfection: the argument of Problem 1 transfers verbatim with $1/3^n$ replaced by $(3/8)^n$, since it used only that endpoints survive and that component lengths tend to $0$. So $D$ is perfect, and by Theorem 3.4.3 uncountable.

For the dimension: magnifying $D$ by a factor of $8/3$ produces exactly $2$ copies of $D$, so writing $2 = (8/3)^x$ gives
$$x = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(8/3)} \approx 0.7067,$$
compared with $\ln 2 / \ln 3 \approx 0.6309$ for the standard Cantor set. Removing less leaves a larger dimension, which is the sanity check to state.

Say which properties depended on the fraction and which did not. Compact, perfect, uncountable, zero length and totally disconnected hold for every such construction; the *value* of the dimension is the only thing the fraction changes. That separation is the point of the exercise, and it is the reason fractal dimension is a useful invariant rather than a curiosity.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — a perfect set of rationals?)

Does there exist a perfect set consisting only of rational numbers?

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.2, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A nonempty perfect set is uncountable. How large is any set of rationals?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Compare Theorem 3.4.3 with an-02's countability of $\mathbb{Q}$. Then check whether the empty set slips through the statement.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Any subset of $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, since a subset of a countable set is countable. A nonempty perfect set is uncountable by Theorem 3.4.3. So no nonempty perfect set can consist only of rationals.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The complete answer must handle the degenerate case: $\emptyset$ is closed and has no isolated points, so it is perfect by Definition 3.4.1 and consists (vacuously) only of rational numbers. Whether you count it depends on how the question is read, and the honest answer says so: the only perfect subset of $\mathbb{Q}$ is the empty one.

Notice how much work Theorem 3.4.3 does here. Without it you would be trying to build a counterexample and failing, with no way to know whether the failure was yours. A theorem that forbids a construction is worth several that permit one.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — a sufficient condition for separation)

Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of $\mathbb{R}$. Show that if there exist disjoint open sets $U$ and $V$ with $A \subseteq U$ and $B \subseteq V$, then $A$ and $B$ are separated.

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.5, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You must show $\overline{A} \cap B = \emptyset$. Take a point of $B$ and show it is neither in $A$ nor a limit point of $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Let $x \in B \subseteq V$. Since $V$ is open, some $V_\epsilon(x) \subseteq V$, and $V$ is disjoint from $U \supseteq A$. So that neighbourhood misses $A$ entirely, which kills both ways $x$ could be in $\overline{A}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$V_\epsilon(x) \cap A \subseteq V \cap U = \emptyset$. Hence $x \notin A$ — since $x \in V_\epsilon(x)$ — and $x$ is not a limit point of $A$, since Definition 3.2.4 would require this very neighbourhood to meet $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Therefore $x \notin \overline{A} = A \cup L(A)$, and since $x \in B$ was arbitrary, $\overline{A} \cap B = \emptyset$. The argument with $A$ and $B$ interchanged gives $A \cap \overline{B} = \emptyset$, so $A$ and $B$ are separated by Definition 3.4.4.

Add a remark on the converse, which is where the interest lies: separated sets need not sit inside disjoint open sets in every topological setting, and in $\mathbb{R}$ they do. The condition in this exercise is the one Semester 2 will take as the definition of a disconnected space, precisely because it mentions only open sets — the same trade an-08 made when it preferred open covers to sequences.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — what closure preserves)

(a) Find an example of a disconnected set whose closure is connected.
(b) If $A$ is connected, must $\overline{A}$ be connected? If $A$ is perfect, must $\overline{A}$ be perfect?

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.7, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), remove a single point from an interval. For the perfect half of (b), remember that a perfect set is already closed.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use Theorem 3.4.7 as the working test for connectedness in $\mathbb{R}$: a set is connected exactly when it contains every point between any two of its points.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Take $A = (0,1) \cup (1,2)$. It is disconnected — it contains $1/2$ and $3/2$ but not $1$, so Theorem 3.4.7 fails — while $\overline{A} = [0,2]$ is an interval and hence connected.

Check whatever you reach for first, because the most obvious candidate does not work. $A = [0,1] \cup [2,3]$ is certainly disconnected, but it is also *closed*, so $\overline{A} = A$ and the closure is exactly as disconnected as the set. It answers nothing.

The difference between the two is the whole point of the problem. Closure adds limit points and nothing else, so it can only repair a gap the set already comes arbitrarily close to filling. The single missing point $1$ is a limit point of both $(0,1)$ and $(1,2)$, so closure restores it. The interval $(1,2)$ separating $[0,1]$ from $[2,3]$ contains no limit point of either piece, so closure leaves it precisely as it found it. Removing *one point* is the construction; removing an interval is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Yes for connectedness. Suppose $A$ is connected and let $a < c < b$ with $a, b \in \overline{A}$. If $c \notin \overline{A}$ then $A \subseteq \overline{A}$ misses $c$ too, and one can split $\overline{A}$ at $c$ exactly as in the lesson's faded example; the two pieces are separated and both nonempty, so $\overline{A}$ would be disconnected. Working instead through Theorem 3.4.7 directly: points of $\overline{A}$ are limits of points of $A$, so choose sequences in $A$ converging to $a$ and to $b$, note that beyond some index their terms straddle $c$, and connectedness of $A$ puts $c \in A \subseteq \overline{A}$.

The perfect half is a one-liner and worth spotting rather than computing: a perfect set is closed by Definition 3.4.1, so $\overline{A} = A$ and the question answers itself.

Say which direction of (b) is trivial and why. Recognising that a question about $\overline{A}$ is vacuous when $A$ is already closed saves the whole computation, and missing it is a common way to spend twenty minutes proving $A = A$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the Cantor set has no interval in it)

Follow these steps to show the Cantor set is totally disconnected, in the sense that for any two points $x, y \in C$ there are separated sets $A$, $B$ with $x \in A$, $y \in B$ and $C = A \cup B$.

(a) Given $x < y$ in $C$, set $\epsilon = y - x$. Explain why there is an $N$ for which $x$ and $y$ cannot both lie in the same component interval of $C_N$.
(b) Argue that there is a point $z \notin C$ with $x < z < y$, and explain how this shows no interval $(a,b)$ with $a < b$ lies inside $C$.
(c) Conclude that $C$ is totally disconnected.

*(Abbott §3.4, Exercise 3.4.9, p. 93)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The components of $C_N$ have length $1/3^N$, and that number can be made smaller than any given $\epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Once $x$ and $y$ are in different components of $C_N$, the gap between those components is a removed open interval, and any point of it serves as $z$. Then split $C$ at $z$ using the open sets $(-\infty, z)$ and $(z, \infty)$ and quote Problem 4.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Choose $N$ with $1/3^N < \epsilon$, which is possible by the Archimedean property from an-01. Two points in a common component of $C_N$ are at distance at most $1/3^N < \epsilon = y - x$, so $x$ and $y$ lie in different components.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Between two distinct components of $C_N$ lies at least one interval removed at some stage $n \le N$; that interval is disjoint from $C$, and every point $z$ in it satisfies $x < z < y$ and $z \notin C$. Now suppose some $(a,b) \subseteq C$ with $a < b$. Applying the above to $x, y \in (a,b) \cap C$ chosen with $x < y$ — for instance $x = a + (b-a)/3$ and $y = a + 2(b-a)/3$, both in $(a,b)$ and so in $C$ — produces a point $z$ strictly between them that is not in $C$, yet $z \in (a,b) \subseteq C$. Contradiction.

(c) Put $A = C \cap (-\infty, z)$ and $B = C \cap (z, \infty)$. These are nonempty, since $x \in A$ and $y \in B$; their union is $C$, since $z \notin C$; and they lie inside the disjoint open sets $(-\infty, z)$ and $(z, \infty)$, so Problem 4 makes them separated. Hence $C$ is totally disconnected.

Two closing remarks. Part (b) is the precise sense in which $C$ contains no interval, and it is the fact that made the prediction gate's second half false. And notice the shape of the whole unit in this one exercise: $C$ is compact, uncountable and perfect, and at the same time so thoroughly broken up that its only connected subsets are single points. Every one of the words in this chapter's vocabulary is independent of the others, and $C$ is the set that proves it.
</details>
