## Problem 1 (easy — the hypotheses are the theorem) [interleaves at2-06]

State Theorem 3.30 in full, naming every hypothesis. Then show that each of the two
hypotheses is doing real work, by giving a space that satisfies the other one and
for which the conclusion fails:

(a) a manifold that is $R$-orientable but not closed, and
(b) a closed manifold that is not $\mathbb{Z}$-orientable,

and in each case exhibit a specific $k$ for which $H^k(M;R) \not\cong H_{n-k}(M;R)$.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Theorem 3.30, p. 241)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a) the cheapest example is $\mathbb{R}^n$ itself. For (b) take a surface you already know is non-orientable.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is contractible, so $H^k$ and $H_{n-k}$ are both trivial except in one degree each — and those degrees are $0$ and $n$, which differ once $n>0$. (b) Use $\mathbb{RP}^2$ with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients and compare $H^0$ with $H_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For $\mathbb{R}^2$: $H^0 = \mathbb{Z}$ but $H_{2-0} = H_2(\mathbb{R}^2) = 0$. (b) For $\mathbb{RP}^2$: $H^0(\mathbb{RP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ while $H_2(\mathbb{RP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ by Theorem 3.26(b).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Theorem 3.30: if $M$ is a closed $R$-orientable $n$-manifold with fundamental class $[M] \in H_n(M;R)$, then $D(\alpha) = [M] \frown \alpha$ is an isomorphism $H^k(M;R) \to H_{n-k}(M;R)$ for all $k$. Dropping *closed*: $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ is orientable, $H^0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_2 = 0$. Dropping *orientable*: $M = \mathbb{RP}^2$ is closed, and Theorem 3.26(b) gives $H_2(\mathbb{RP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ whereas $H^0(\mathbb{RP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$. Note that duality *is* restored for $\mathbb{RP}^2$ over $R = \mathbb{Z}_2$, since every manifold is $\mathbb{Z}_2$-orientable — the hypothesis is about the pair $(M, R)$, not about $M$ alone.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — duality on a surface, checked degree by degree)

Let $\Sigma_g$ be the closed orientable surface of genus $g$. Write out
$H_k(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Z})$ and $H^k(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Z})$ for $k = 0,1,2$, and verify
explicitly that the duality map of Theorem 3.30 is an isomorphism in each degree.
Say which degree pairs with which.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Example 3.31, p. 241)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\Sigma_g$ has a CW structure with one 0-cell, $2g$ 1-cells and one 2-cell.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Homology is free in every degree here, so the universal coefficient theorem makes $H^k \cong \mathrm{Hom}(H_k,\mathbb{Z})$ with no Ext term. Then match $k$ against $n-k = 2-k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^{2g}$, $H_2 = \mathbb{Z}$; cohomology is the same in each degree.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
All homology is free, so $H^k \cong \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}^{2g}, \mathbb{Z}$ for $k=0,1,2$. Duality pairs $k$ with $2-k$: $H^0 = \mathbb{Z} \to H_2 = \mathbb{Z}$; $H^1 = \mathbb{Z}^{2g} \to H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^{2g}$; $H^2 = \mathbb{Z} \to H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$. Each is an isomorphism of free abelian groups of equal rank, as Theorem 3.30 requires. Note the middle degree is self-paired — this is what makes the cup product pairing of Problem 5 a form on a single space.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — an odd-dimensional consequence)

Prove Corollary 3.37 in the orientable case: a closed orientable manifold of odd
dimension has Euler characteristic zero.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Corollary 3.37, p. 249)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\chi(M) = \sum_i (-1)^i \operatorname{rank} H_i(M;\mathbb{Z})$. You want the terms to cancel in pairs.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use Theorem 3.30 to relate $\operatorname{rank} H^{n-i}$ to $\operatorname{rank} H_i$, then the universal coefficient theorem to replace $\operatorname{rank} H^{n-i}$ by $\operatorname{rank} H_{n-i}$. Then compare the signs attached to $i$ and to $n-i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\operatorname{rank} H_i = \operatorname{rank} H^{n-i} = \operatorname{rank} H_{n-i}$. With $n$ odd, $(-1)^i$ and $(-1)^{n-i}$ have opposite signs.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Let $M$ be closed, orientable, of odd dimension $n$. Theorem 3.30 gives $H^{i}(M;\mathbb{Z}) \cong H_{n-i}(M;\mathbb{Z})$, so $\operatorname{rank} H_i = \operatorname{rank} H^{n-i}$; the universal coefficient theorem gives $\operatorname{rank} H^{n-i} = \operatorname{rank} H_{n-i}$ (the Ext term is finite and contributes no rank). Hence $\operatorname{rank} H_i = \operatorname{rank} H_{n-i}$ for every $i$. Now $\chi(M) = \sum_i (-1)^i \operatorname{rank} H_i$. Since $n$ is odd, $(-1)^{n-i} = -(-1)^i$, so the terms for $i$ and $n-i$ are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. As $n$ odd forces $i \neq n-i$ for every $i$, the sum cancels in pairs and $\chi(M) = 0$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — reading orientability off homology)

Let $K$ be the Klein bottle, with $H_0(K;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$,
$H_1(K;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$, $H_2(K;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$.

(a) Use Theorem 3.26 to conclude that $K$ is non-orientable, and say precisely which
clause of the theorem you are invoking.
(b) Check that the torsion you see in $H_1(K;\mathbb{Z})$ is exactly what Corollary 3.28
predicts.
(c) Explain why (b) is *not* an instance of Poincaré duality.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Theorem 3.26 and Corollary 3.28, pp. 236–238)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Theorem 3.26(a) and (b) differ in what $H_n(M;\mathbb{Z})$ comes out to be. Corollary 3.28 is a statement about degree $n-1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), notice that Theorem 3.30 requires $R$-orientability, which is precisely what (a) has just ruled out over $\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$K$ is a closed connected 2-manifold with $H_2(K;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$; Corollary 3.28 concerns the torsion subgroup of $H_{n-1} = H_1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $K$ is a closed connected $2$-manifold. Theorem 3.26 says $H_n(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ if $M$ is orientable and $0$ if not; here $H_2(K;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$, so by clause (b) of the theorem $K$ is non-orientable and has no fundamental class over $\mathbb{Z}$.
(b) Corollary 3.28 states that for a closed connected $n$-manifold the torsion subgroup of $H_{n-1}(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is trivial when $M$ is orientable and $\mathbb{Z}_2$ when it is not. With $n=2$ this predicts torsion $\mathbb{Z}_2$ in $H_1(K;\mathbb{Z})$, which is exactly the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ summand given.
(c) Poincaré duality as stated in Theorem 3.30 assumes $R$-orientability. Over $R = \mathbb{Z}$ the Klein bottle fails that hypothesis, so Theorem 3.30 does not apply and Corollary 3.28 must be — and is — proved by other means. Duality does return over $\mathbb{Z}_2$.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the cup product pairing)

Let $\Sigma_g$ be the closed orientable genus-$g$ surface and take field coefficients
$R = \mathbb{Q}$.

(a) State what Proposition 3.38 asserts about the pairing
$H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q}) \times H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q}) \to H^2(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Q}$,
$(\alpha,\beta) \mapsto \langle \alpha \smile \beta, [\Sigma_g]\rangle$.
(b) Deduce that for every non-zero $\alpha \in H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q})$ there exists
$\beta$ with $\alpha \smile \beta \neq 0$.
(c) The pairing is skew-symmetric on $H^1$. Explain why skew-symmetry plus
non-singularity forces $\dim H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q})$ to be even, and reconcile this
with your answer to Problem 2.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Proposition 3.38 and Corollary 3.39, pp. 249–250)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
"Non-singular" means the induced map $H^1 \to \mathrm{Hom}(H^1, \mathbb{Q})$, $\alpha \mapsto \langle \alpha \smile -\,, [\Sigma_g]\rangle$, is an isomorphism.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) is a restatement of injectivity of that induced map. For (c), recall that a non-degenerate skew-symmetric bilinear form on a finite-dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic $\neq 2$ admits a symplectic basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Non-singularity says $\alpha \mapsto (\beta \mapsto \langle \alpha \smile \beta, [\Sigma_g]\rangle)$ is injective, so a non-zero $\alpha$ cannot annihilate all of $H^1$. A non-degenerate alternating form pairs basis vectors off in hyperbolic planes.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Proposition 3.38 states that for a closed $R$-orientable manifold with $R$ a field, the cup product pairing is non-singular: the map $H^{k} \to \operatorname{Hom}_R(H^{n-k}, R)$ sending $\alpha$ to $\beta \mapsto \langle \alpha \smile \beta, [M]\rangle$ is an isomorphism. Here $n=2$, $k=1$, so $H^1$ pairs with itself into $H^2 \cong \mathbb{Q}$.
(b) If $\alpha \neq 0$ satisfied $\alpha \smile \beta = 0$ for all $\beta$, then $\alpha$ would lie in the kernel of an isomorphism — impossible. So some $\beta$ has $\alpha \smile \beta \neq 0$. (Corollary 3.39 is the integral refinement of this statement.)
(c) A non-degenerate alternating form on a finite-dimensional space over $\mathbb{Q}$ decomposes the space into an orthogonal direct sum of hyperbolic planes, so its dimension is even. Problem 2 gave $\dim H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Q}) = 2g$, which is even, and the symplectic basis is the familiar $a_1,b_1,\dots,a_g,b_g$ with $a_i \smile b_i$ generating $H^2$ and all other products vanishing.
</details>
