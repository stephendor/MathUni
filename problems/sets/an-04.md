## Problem 1 (easy — monotone convergence, then the value) [interleaves an-03]

Let $x_1 = 3$ and define $x_{n+1} = \dfrac{1}{4 - x_n}$.

(a) Prove that $(x_n)$ converges.
(b) Explain why $\lim x_{n+1}$ exists and equals $\lim x_n$.
(c) Take limits on both sides of the recursion and compute $\lim x_n$ exactly.

*(Abbott §2.4, Exercise 2.4.2, p. 54)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Compute the first four terms before proving anything: $3, 1, 1/3, 3/11, \dots$. That tells you which of the two hypotheses of Theorem 2.4.2 to aim at, and in which direction.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Prove by induction that $0 < x_{n+1} \le 1$ for all $n \ge 1$; this both bounds the sequence and keeps $4 - x_n \ge 3 > 0$, so the recursion never divides by zero. Then show $x_{n+1} \le x_n$. For (b), $(x_{n+1})$ is the subsequence of $(x_n)$ with indices $2, 3, 4, \dots$, so Theorem 2.5.2 applies. For (c), set $L = \lim x_n$ and solve $L = 1/(4 - L)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Monotonicity is cleanest as a consequence of the bound. If $0 < x_n \le 1$ then $3 \le 4 - x_n < 4$, so $x_{n+1} = 1/(4 - x_n) \in (1/4, 1/3]$, which is again inside $(0, 1]$ — and the induction closes. For the decrease, $x_n - x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{1}{4-x_n} = \frac{x_n(4 - x_n) - 1}{4 - x_n} = \frac{-(x_n^2 - 4x_n + 1)}{4 - x_n}$, and the numerator is non-negative exactly when $x_n \ge 2 - \sqrt{3}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$L = 1/(4-L)$ gives $L(4 - L) = 1$, so $L^2 - 4L + 1 = 0$ and $L = 2 \pm \sqrt{3}$. The terms lie in $(0, 1]$ and limits respect non-strict inequalities (an-03's Theorem 2.3.4), so $L \le 1$ and the root $2 + \sqrt{3} \approx 3.73$ is impossible. Hence $L = 2 - \sqrt{3} \approx 0.2679$, which is where the numerical terms were heading.

Two things to get right in the write-up. Part (b) is not a formality: without it you would be taking the limit of $x_{n+1}$ as though it were obviously $L$, and the reason it is obviously $L$ is Theorem 2.5.2 applied to a shift. And the quadratic has two roots — you must rule one out by an argument, not by inspection of decimals.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — a nested radical) [interleaves an-01]

Show that the sequence
$$\sqrt{2}, \quad \sqrt{2\sqrt{2}}, \quad \sqrt{2\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}}}, \quad \dots$$
converges, and find its limit.

*(Abbott §2.4, Exercise 2.4.4, p. 54)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Write the sequence recursively before doing anything else: $x_1 = \sqrt{2}$ and $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 x_n}$. A nested radical is unmanageable; a recursion is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show by induction that $x_n < 2$, then that $(x_n)$ is increasing, then apply Theorem 2.4.2. Only after existence is settled may you compute the value.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $x_n < 2$ then $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2x_n} < \sqrt{4} = 2$, and $x_1 = \sqrt{2} < 2$, so the bound holds for every $n$. For monotonicity, $x_{n+1} \ge x_n \iff 2x_n \ge x_n^2 \iff x_n \le 2$, which is exactly the bound just proved.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By Theorem 2.4.2 the limit $L$ exists, and $L \ge x_1 = \sqrt{2} > 0$. Squaring the recursion gives $x_{n+1}^2 = 2x_n$; both sides converge, so $L^2 = 2L$ by the Algebraic Limit Theorem, and $L(L - 2) = 0$. Since $L > 0$ we get $L = 2$.

Note the shape, which is the same as Problem 1 and will be the same every time: monotone plus bounded buys *existence*, and only then does the recursion pin down the *value*. Writing "let $L = \lim x_n$" before proving the limit exists is the single most common way to get full marks for a wrong proof.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — Newton's method in disguise)

Let $x_1 = 2$ and define
$$x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{2}{x_n}\right).$$

(a) Show that $x_n^2 > 2$ for every $n$, use this to prove $x_n - x_{n+1} \ge 0$, and conclude that $\lim x_n = \sqrt{2}$.
(b) Modify the sequence so that it converges to $\sqrt{c}$ for an arbitrary $c > 0$.

*(Abbott §2.4, Exercise 2.4.5, p. 54)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For $x_n^2 > 2$, compute $x_{n+1}^2 - 2$ in terms of $x_n$ and look for a square.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$x_{n+1}^2 - 2 = \frac{(x_n^2 - 2)^2}{4x_n^2}$, which is non-negative for free and strictly positive as long as $x_n^2 \ne 2$. Then $x_n - x_{n+1} = \frac{x_n^2 - 2}{2x_n}$, so the decrease follows from part one. Bounded below by $0$ and decreasing gives convergence by Theorem 2.4.2; the value comes from the recursion.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{2}{x_n}\right) = \frac{x_n^2 + 2}{2x_n}$, so
$$x_{n+1}^2 - 2 = \frac{(x_n^2+2)^2 - 8x_n^2}{4x_n^2} = \frac{(x_n^2 - 2)^2}{4x_n^2} \ge 0.$$
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
With $L = \lim x_n$, the recursion gives $L = \frac{1}{2}(L + 2/L)$, hence $2L^2 = L^2 + 2$ and $L^2 = 2$. Since every $x_n > 0$, the Order Limit Theorem gives $L \ge 0$, so $L = \sqrt{2}$.

One step needs care and is where marks are lost: dividing by $L$ is only legal once you know $L \ne 0$. The bound $x_n^2 > 2$ gives $x_n > \sqrt{2}$ for all $n$, so $L \ge \sqrt{2} > 0$ by the Order Limit Theorem, and the division is justified before it is used.

For (b), replace the $2$ inside the bracket by $c$: $x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}(x_n + c/x_n)$ with $x_1$ any positive starting value. The identity $x_{n+1}^2 - c = (x_n^2 - c)^2/(4x_n^2)$ is the same computation with $2$ replaced by $c$ throughout.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — finishing a proof from the lesson)

Complete the proof of Theorem 2.4.6, the Cauchy Condensation Test, by showing that if $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^n b_{2^n}$ diverges, then so does $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$. As in the theorem, $(b_n)$ is decreasing and satisfies $b_n \ge 0$.

*(Abbott §2.4, Exercise 2.4.1, p. 54)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Example 2.4.5, the harmonic series argument in the lesson, is the whole method. Reread it with $1/n$ replaced by $b_n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Group the partial sum $s_{2^k}$ into blocks running from index $2^{j-1}+1$ to $2^j$, and bound each block *below* by its smallest term — which, because $(b_n)$ is decreasing, is the last one. The bracket with $2^{j-1}$ terms is then at least $2^{j-1} b_{2^j}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$$s_{2^k} \;\ge\; b_1 + b_2 + 2b_4 + 4b_8 + \cdots + 2^{k-1}b_{2^k} \;=\; b_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(2b_2 + 4b_4 + \cdots + 2^k b_{2^k}\right) \;\ge\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, t_k,$$
where $t_k$ is the $k$-th partial sum of the condensed series.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If $\sum 2^n b_{2^n}$ diverges then $(t_k)$ is unbounded, since its terms are non-negative and so $(t_k)$ is increasing — an increasing sequence that converged would be handled by Theorem 2.4.2, and an increasing sequence that diverges must be unbounded. The inequality above then makes $(s_{2^k})$ unbounded, so $(s_m)$ is unbounded, so $(s_m)$ diverges by an-03's Theorem 2.3.2. Hence $\sum b_n$ diverges.

Say explicitly where "decreasing" is used — it is the step that lets the smallest term of a block be identified as its last, and the whole estimate collapses without it. Note also that $(s_{2^k})$ is a subsequence of $(s_m)$: unbounded on a subsequence is enough, because a bounded sequence has bounded subsequences.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — turning subsequences back into the sequence)

Assume $(a_n)$ is a bounded sequence with the property that every convergent subsequence of $(a_n)$ converges to the same limit $a \in \mathbb{R}$. Show that $(a_n)$ itself must converge to $a$.

*(Abbott §2.5, Exercise 2.5.4, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Negate the conclusion. If $(a_n)$ does not converge to $a$, what exactly does the failed $\epsilon$-$N$ statement give you — and how many terms does it give you?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The negation supplies an $\epsilon_0 > 0$ such that $|a_n - a| \ge \epsilon_0$ for infinitely many $n$. Collect those indices into a subsequence. It is bounded, so Theorem 2.5.5 applies to *it*, producing a convergent subsequence of a subsequence — which is still a subsequence of $(a_n)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Failure of convergence reads: there exists $\epsilon_0 > 0$ such that for every $N \in \mathbb{N}$ there is some $n \ge N$ with $|a_n - a| \ge \epsilon_0$. Applying this with $N = 1$, then with $N = n_1 + 1$, then with $N = n_2 + 1$, and so on, produces indices $n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \cdots$ with $|a_{n_k} - a| \ge \epsilon_0$ for every $k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
That construction is the load-bearing step and it deserves a sentence of its own: the indices are chosen recursively, each strictly larger than the last, which is what makes the result a subsequence in the sense of Definition 2.5.1 rather than merely a set of terms.

Now $(a_{n_k})$ is bounded, being part of a bounded sequence, so Theorem 2.5.5 gives a convergent subsequence $(a_{n_{k_j}})$, say with limit $b$. A subsequence of a subsequence of $(a_n)$ is a subsequence of $(a_n)$ — the composed index sequence is still strictly increasing — so the hypothesis forces $b = a$. But $|a_{n_{k_j}} - a| \ge \epsilon_0$ for every $j$, and the Order Limit Theorem then gives $|b - a| \ge \epsilon_0 > 0$, contradicting $b = a$.

Notice which hypothesis each half consumed: boundedness was spent on Theorem 2.5.5, and "every convergent subsequence has limit $a$" was spent on identifying $b$. Drop boundedness and the statement is false — $a_n = n$ has no convergent subsequence at all, so the hypothesis holds vacuously for any $a$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — Bolzano-Weierstrass without bisection)

Let $(a_n)$ be a bounded sequence and define
$$S = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < a_n \text{ for infinitely many terms } a_n\}.$$
Show that there exists a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ converging to $s = \sup S$. This is a second proof of Theorem 2.5.5, going directly through the Axiom of Completeness instead of through nested intervals.

*(Abbott §2.5, Exercise 2.5.6, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
First check $S$ is nonempty and bounded above, so that $\sup S$ exists at all. A lower bound for the sequence belongs to $S$; an upper bound for the sequence is an upper bound for $S$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Fix $\epsilon > 0$. Show that $s - \epsilon \in S$ and $s + \epsilon \notin S$, and read off what each of those says about how many terms exceed the respective number. Together they trap infinitely many terms inside $(s - \epsilon, s + \epsilon)$, which is exactly the raw material for a recursive choice of indices.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $(a_n)$ is bounded by $M$, then $-M - 1 < a_n$ for every $n$, so $-M-1 \in S$ and $S \ne \emptyset$; and no $x > M$ can satisfy $x < a_n$ for even one $n$, so $M$ is an upper bound for $S$. By the Axiom of Completeness, $s = \sup S$ exists.

Given $\epsilon > 0$: by an-01's Lemma 1.3.7, $s - \epsilon$ is not an upper bound for $S$, so some $x \in S$ has $x > s - \epsilon$; since $x < a_n$ for infinitely many $n$, so is $s - \epsilon < a_n$ for infinitely many $n$. And $s + \epsilon \notin S$ because $s + \epsilon > s$, so $s + \epsilon < a_n$ for only finitely many $n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Combining the two: infinitely many $n$ satisfy $a_n > s - \epsilon$, and only finitely many satisfy $a_n > s + \epsilon$. Subtracting the finite set from the infinite one leaves infinitely many $n$ with $s - \epsilon < a_n \le s + \epsilon$, hence $|a_n - s| \le \epsilon$.

Now build the subsequence. Apply the above with $\epsilon = 1$ and pick any such index $n_1$. Apply it with $\epsilon = 1/2$: infinitely many indices qualify, so one of them exceeds $n_1$ — pick it and call it $n_2$. Continue with $\epsilon = 1/k$, at each stage choosing an index larger than the last, which is possible precisely because "infinitely many" cannot be exhausted by the finitely many indices already used. Then $|a_{n_k} - s| \le 1/k \to 0$, so $(a_{n_k}) \to s$.

Two remarks worth making at the end. First, $s$ is the largest subsequential limit — that is what $\limsup$ means, and Exercise 2.4.6 defines it by a different route. Second, compare the two proofs of Theorem 2.5.5: the bisection in the lesson and this one both cash in completeness, once as nested intervals and once as a supremum. That is not a coincidence, and an-05's characterisation of completeness by Cauchy sequences will be a third face of the same fact.
</details>
