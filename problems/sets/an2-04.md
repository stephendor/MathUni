# an2-04 — The contraction mapping theorem and applications

**Module:** Analysis II · **Unit:** an2-04
**Sources:** Lindström, *Spaces: An Introduction to Real Analysis* — §3.4
"Complete spaces" (pp. 61–63, the contraction half), §4.7 "Applications to
differential equations" (pp. 103–107), §4.9 "Differential equations revisited"
(pp. 112–116).

Numbered results used: Theorem 3.4.5 and the estimate (3.4.1), Lemma 4.7.1,
Theorem 4.7.2, Lemma 4.9.1, Theorem 4.9.2. Carried from an2-01 to an2-03:
Definition 3.1.1, Proposition 3.2.3, Proposition 3.2.5, Definition 3.4.1,
Definition 3.4.3, Proposition 3.4.4, Theorem 3.5.10, Proposition 4.8.6,
Theorem 4.8.9. Named from sections belonging to other units: Definition 4.1.3
(an2-05's §4.1) and Theorem 4.6.4 (an2-08's §4.6).

**Two existence theorems for one problem, and the trade is the subject.**
Theorem 4.7.2 solves y′ = f(t, y), y(0) = y₀ by making the integral operator a
contraction and applying Banach's Theorem 3.4.5. Theorem 4.9.2 solves the same
problem by running Euler's method and extracting a convergent subsequence with
Arzelà–Ascoli. The first needs f uniformly Lipschitz and returns a *unique*
solution on *all* of [0, ∞) with a computable error bound. The second needs only
f *continuous* and returns *some* solution on *some* interval, with no bound and
no uniqueness. Lindström states the trade himself at p. 116: compactness methods
"are strong in the sense that they can often prove local existence of solutions
under very general conditions, but they are weak in the sense that they give
very little information about the nature of the solution."

**What Lindström proves and what he sets.** He *proves* Theorem 3.4.5 with the
estimate (3.4.1), Lemma 4.7.1, Theorem 4.7.2, Lemma 4.9.1 and Theorem 4.9.2. He
*sets as exercises* the two negative controls on Theorem 4.7.2's hypotheses —
Exercises 1 and 2 to §4.7 — and the generalisation of Theorem 4.9.2 to a solution
through a point rather than from one (Exercise 3 to §4.9). Neither of the two
negative controls is worked in the text, and both are load-bearing: Problem 4
below is built on them.

## Problem 1 (easy — the theorem, and where each hypothesis is spent)

(a) Define a *contraction* with contraction factor $s$, following Lindström at
p. 61: $d(f(x), f(y)) \leq s\, d(x,y)$ for all $x, y$, with the *same* $s < 1$
throughout. Prove the two things he asserts in a parenthesis and an aside —
that every contraction is continuous, and that
$d(f^{\circ n}(x), f^{\circ n}(y)) \leq s^n d(x,y)$ by induction. For the first,
say what $\delta$ you take and note that it does not depend on the point, so you
have proved something stronger than continuity; name it, and cite
**Definition 4.1.3** for the version of that idea applied to a whole family.

(b) State and prove **Theorem 3.4.5**, Banach's Fixed Point Theorem. Then answer
the question the proof is designed to make answerable: **completeness is used on
exactly one line.** Identify it, and say what would have to be supplied by hand
if the space were not complete.

(c) Uniqueness costs one line and does not use completeness at all. Extract it:
if $a$ and $b$ are both fixed, then $d(a,b) = d(f(a), f(b)) \leq s\,d(a,b)$.
Finish, and name the condition of **Definition 3.1.1** that turns
$d(a,b) = 0$ into $a = b$.

(d) Derive the estimate **(3.4.1)**, $d(x_n, a) \leq \frac{s^n}{1-s} d(x_0, x_1)$,
by letting $m \to \infty$ in the Cauchy bound. Then compute: for $s = 0.55$ and
$d(x_0, x_1) = 0.2775$, how many iterations guarantee an error below $10^{-3}$?
Note what you have just done that no theorem in an2-03 could do — you have
answered a question about *how long*, not merely *whether*.

(e) Lindström's Exercise 5 to §3.4: let $f : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ be differentiable
with $|f'(x)| < s$ for some $s < 1$ and all $x \in (0,1)$. Show $f$ has exactly
one fixed point. Then Exercise 6, which is the same statement in a coat: you are
standing inside the area depicted on a map you are holding; explain why exactly
one point of the map lies vertically above the point it depicts. Say which
hypothesis of Theorem 3.4.5 the physical setup is quietly supplying.

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — a differential equation is a fixed-point problem)

(a) Lindström's first move is to replace the initial value problem
$$\mathbf{y}'(t) = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y}(t)), \qquad \mathbf{y}(0) = \mathbf{y}_0$$
by the integral equation
$$\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{y}_0 + \int_0^t \mathbf{f}(s, \mathbf{y}(s))\, ds.$$
Show the two are equivalent, in both directions, and say what the integral form
has that the differential form does not — specifically, which of the two lets you
*apply an operator to a function that is not yet known to be differentiable*.

(b) Define $F(\mathbf{z})(t) = \mathbf{y}_0 + \int_0^t \mathbf{f}(s, \mathbf{z}(s))\,ds$
and say, in one sentence each, what the domain of $F$ is, what its codomain is,
and what a fixed point of $F$ is. Lindström adds a Remark to Lemma 4.7.1 about
the notation being "rather messy" — write out what $F(\mathbf{z})(t)$ means with
every layer named.

(c) Define *uniformly Lipschitz with constant K* as Lindström does at p. 105:
$\|\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y}) - \mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{z})\| \leq K\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{z}\|$
for all $t$ and all $\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z}$. Note carefully which variable the
condition is about and which it is uniform over. Then give a continuous
$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is not Lipschitz, and a Lipschitz $f$ that is
not differentiable, to confirm the two notions are independent.

(d) Prove **Lemma 4.7.1**: if $a < 1/K$ then
$F : C([0,a], \mathbb{R}^n) \to C([0,a],\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a contraction with
factor $Ka$. Lindström's chain of four inequalities is the whole proof — write it
out and label each step: pulling the norm inside the integral, applying the
Lipschitz condition, replacing $\|\mathbf{v}(s) - \mathbf{w}(s)\|$ by the sup
metric $\rho(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})$, and extending the range of integration from
$t$ to $a$. Then say why taking the supremum over $t$ at the end is legitimate,
in the words of an2-01's worked example.

(e) The step that is easy to skip: $F$ must map $C([0,a],\mathbb{R}^n)$
*into itself*. Say why $F(\mathbf{z})$ is continuous when $\mathbf{z}$ is, and
name the hypothesis on $\mathbf{f}$ that makes the integral exist at all.

*(Lindström p. 103 for the opening of §4.7; p. 104 for the vector notation and
the passage from (4.7.1) to (4.7.2); pp. 104–105 for the operator $F$ and the
"don't worry if you feel a little dizzy" aside; p. 105 for the uniform Lipschitz
condition, Lemma 4.7.1 with its Remark and proof, and the statement of
Theorem 4.7.2.)*

Calculus is doing the work in both directions, and it needs the integrand
continuous.
For (c), $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous and not Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}$;
$f(x) = |x|$ is Lipschitz and not differentiable at 0.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The chain is
$$\|F(\mathbf{v})(t) - F(\mathbf{w})(t)\| \leq \int_0^t \|\mathbf{f}(s,\mathbf{v}(s)) - \mathbf{f}(s,\mathbf{w}(s))\|\,ds \leq \int_0^t K\|\mathbf{v}(s)-\mathbf{w}(s)\|\,ds \leq K\int_0^a \rho(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\,ds = Ka\,\rho(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}).$$

does not depend on $t$: a fixed number bounding every member of a set bounds its
supremum. That is the move from an2-01's worked example on the sup metric, used
for the third time in this module.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The integral form is what makes the operator well defined. $F$ has to accept
*any* continuous $\mathbf{z}$, and a continuous function need not be
differentiable — so there is no operator "apply $\mathbf{y} \mapsto \mathbf{y}'$"
on $C([0,a],\mathbb{R}^n)$ at all. Integration, by contrast, accepts every
continuous function and returns a differentiable one, so it maps the space into
itself with room to spare. The reformulation is not a convenience; it is what
makes the fixed-point framing possible.

(c) The condition is Lipschitz in the *second* variable $\mathbf{y}$, and
uniform in the *first* variable $t$ — the same $K$ must work at every time. That
asymmetry is what "uniformly" names, and it is why Theorem 4.7.2 can extend its
solution interval by interval without the constant degrading.

(e) $F(\mathbf{z})$ is continuous — in fact differentiable — because it is an
integral with a continuous integrand, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
The hypothesis that makes the integrand continuous is that $\mathbf{f}$ is
continuous and $\mathbf{z}$ is continuous, so $s \mapsto \mathbf{f}(s,\mathbf{z}(s))$
is a composition of continuous functions — **Proposition 3.2.5**'s territory.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $F$ takes an element of $C([0,a],\mathbb{R}^n)$ — a whole *function*
$\mathbf{z} : [0,a] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ — and returns another such function. So
$F(\mathbf{z})$ is a function, and $F(\mathbf{z})(t)$ is the value of that
function at the number $t \in [0,a]$, which is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Three
layers: a point $t$, a function $\mathbf{z}$, an operator $F$. A fixed point of
$F$ is a function $\mathbf{y}$ with $F(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{y}$, meaning
$F(\mathbf{y})(t) = \mathbf{y}(t)$ for every $t$ — and that equation, written
out, *is* the integral equation.

(d) Lindström's Exercise 7 to §3.4 generalises Theorem 3.4.5: if

(e) Exercise 3 to §4.7, which is Lemma 4.7.1 in a different costume. For a

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the two negative controls, which Lindström sets and does not work)

Lindström writes, immediately after Theorem 4.7.2: "In the exercises you will see
that the conditions in the theorem are important. If they fail, the equation may
have more than one solution, or a solution defined only on a bounded interval."
The exercises are the watched failures for the theorem's two conclusions, and
this problem is both of them.

(a) **Exercise 1 to §4.7 — global existence fails.** Solve
$y' = 1 + y^2$, $y(0) = 0$, and show the solution is defined only on
$[0, \pi/2)$. Then check the hypothesis: is $f(t,y) = 1 + y^2$ continuous? Is it
uniformly Lipschitz in $y$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$? Compute
$\partial f/\partial y$ and say exactly which clause of the definition in
Problem 2(c) fails.

(b) Lindström returns to this equation at p. 115 with a sharper observation: with
initial condition $y(0) = y_0$ the solution is $y(t) = \tan(t + \arctan y_0)$,
"which shows that the life span of the solution depends on the initial
condition". Compute the life span as a function of $y_0$, and say why that
dependence is exactly what makes the interval-by-interval extension of
Theorem 4.7.2 unavailable here.

(c) **Exercise 2 to §4.7 — uniqueness fails.** Consider the initial value problem
$y' = \tfrac{3}{2}\, y^{1/3}$, $y(0) = 0$, and the family

for each $a \geq 0$. Show every member of the family is a solution — and, as
Lindström says, "Remember to check that the differential equation is satisfied at
$t = a$", which is the only interesting point. Then identify the failing

neither survives it.

(e) Both right-hand sides are continuous, so Theorem 4.9.2 applies to both and

infinitely many solutions, so a theorem promising uniqueness would be false. The
comment is that Theorem 4.9.2's weaker conclusion is not a weaker proof of the
same fact; it is the strongest true statement.
