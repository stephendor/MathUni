## Problem 1 (easy–medium — roots decide, up to degree three and no further)

(a) Prove Example 7.13 (p. 136): every degree-1 polynomial over a field is irreducible.

(b) Prove Proposition 7.15 (p. 137): over a field $k$, a polynomial of degree $2$ or $3$ is irreducible if and only if it has no root in $k$.

(c) Show the criterion fails at degree $4$: exhibit a polynomial in $\mathbb{R}[x]$ with no real root that is reducible. Compare Example 7.21 (p. 139).

(d) Remark 7.16 (p. 137) points out that one direction of (b)'s proof takes an inverse in $k$. Which direction, and what is the statement over a ring that is not a field?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) A factorisation of a degree-2 or degree-3 polynomial into non-units must have a factor of degree exactly 1, and a degree-1 factor produces a root.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $x^4+4$ is the standard one; factor it as a product of two quadratics.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $f$ has degree 1 and $f = gh$ with neither a unit, then $\deg g + \deg h = 1$, so one factor has degree $0$ — a nonzero constant, which in a field is a unit. Contradiction.

(b) ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $f$ of degree $2$ or $3$ factors as $gh$ with both non-units. Degrees add, and neither factor can have degree $0$ (that would be a unit), so one of them has degree $1$, say $g = ax+b$ with $a\ne0$. Then $-b/a$ is a root of $g$ and hence of $f$. So an irreducible $f$ has no root.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) ($\Rightarrow$) Conversely if $f(a)=0$ then $(x-a)\mid f$ by the factor theorem (aa-13 Problem 3(b)), and the cofactor has degree $1$ or $2$, so neither factor is a unit and $f$ is reducible.

(c) $x^4+4$ has no real root, since $x^4+4\ge4$. But
$$x^4+4 = (x^2-2x+2)(x^2+2x+2),$$
as multiplying out confirms: the $x^3$ and $x$ terms cancel and the $x^2$ terms give $2+2-4=0$. So it is reducible with no roots — the two quadratic factors have no real roots either, which is exactly why the root test cannot see the factorisation. Example 7.21 (p. 139) gives the slicker argument. By Proposition 7.20 (p. 138) the irreducibles of $\mathbb{R}[x]$ have degree at most $2$, so a degree-4 polynomial is reducible whatever its roots do.

(d) The direction ($\Leftarrow$), where $-b/a$ is formed. Over a general commutative ring a degree-1 factor $ax+b$ need not produce a root: in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $2x+1$ divides $f(x) = 4x^2-1$ and $f$ has no integer root. So the correct statement over a ring is about degree-1 *factors*, not roots, and the two coincide only when leading coefficients can be inverted.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — what the irreducibles are over ℂ and over ℝ)

(a) Assume Theorem 7.17 (p. 137), the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Prove Corollary 7.18 (p. 137): the irreducibles of $\mathbb{C}[x]$ are exactly the degree-1 polynomials.

(b) Prove Corollary 7.19 (p. 138): every $f\in\mathbb{C}[x]$ of degree $d$ is a product of $d$ linear factors, unique up to units.

(c) Prove Proposition 7.20 (p. 138): $f\in\mathbb{R}[x]$ is irreducible if and only if it has degree 1, or degree 2 with no real root.

(d) Why is there no analogous list for $\mathbb{Q}[x]$? Answer with Corollary 7.38 (p. 147).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) Factor $f$ over $\mathbb{C}$ and pair each non-real root with its conjugate.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) If $z$ is a non-real root of a real polynomial then so is $\bar z$, and $(x-z)(x-\bar z)$ has real coefficients.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Degree-1 polynomials are irreducible by Problem 1(a). Conversely if $\deg f\ge2$ then $f$ has a root $z$ by Theorem 7.17, so $(x-z)\mid f$ with a cofactor of degree $\ge1$; neither factor is a unit, so $f$ is reducible.

(b) $\mathbb{C}[x]$ is a UFD by Corollary 7.8 (p. 134), and by (a) its irreducibles are the linear polynomials. So the factorisation guaranteed by unique factorisation consists of linear factors, and degrees add to $d$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Degree-1 polynomials are irreducible, and a degree-2 real polynomial with no real root is irreducible by Problem 1(b). Conversely, let $f\in\mathbb{R}[x]$ have degree $d\ge2$ and suppose it has no real root — otherwise it visibly factors. Take a root $z\in\mathbb{C}$; since $f$ has real coefficients, applying complex conjugation (a ring homomorphism, aa-08 Problem 1(d)) to $f(z)=0$ gives $f(\bar z)=0$. As $z$ is not real, $z\ne\bar z$, so $(x-z)(x-\bar z)$ divides $f$ in $\mathbb{C}[x]$; and that product is $x^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(z)x + |z|^2$, which lies in $\mathbb{R}[x]$. Dividing in $\mathbb{R}[x]$ — legitimate, the divisor being monic — shows $f$ is a real multiple of a real quadratic, so $f$ is reducible unless $d=2$.

(d) Over $\mathbb{Q}$ there is no such list because the irreducibles are unbounded in degree: Corollary 7.38 says $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ has irreducible polynomials of arbitrarily high degree, and Example 7.39 (p. 147) produces one for each prime. So "which polynomials are irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$" cannot be answered by a degree bound, and §7.5 exists to supply tests instead of a classification.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — Gauss's Lemma)

(a) Prove Lemma 7.23 (p. 140): if an irreducible integer $q$ divides every coefficient of a product $\alpha(x)\beta(x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, then it divides every coefficient of $\alpha$ or every coefficient of $\beta$.

(b) State Definition 7.25 (p. 141) — content and primitivity — and show a nonzero $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is uniquely $c\cdot f_0$ with $c$ its content and $f_0$ primitive, up to sign.

(c) Deduce Corollary 7.26 (p. 141): a primitive $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ if and only if it is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

(d) Why does Corollary 7.26 need the primitivity hypothesis? Give a polynomial irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Reduce mod $q$: the statement becomes that $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})[x]$ has no zero-divisors.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) A constant factor is a unit in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and not in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Reducing coefficients mod $q$ is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}[x]\to(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})[x]$. The hypothesis says $\bar\alpha\bar\beta = 0$ there. Since $q$ is irreducible, $\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ is a field (Theorem 2.15, p. 32), so $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})[x]$ is an integral domain by aa-13 Problem 1(c). Hence $\bar\alpha=0$ or $\bar\beta=0$, which is the conclusion.

(b) The content is the gcd of the coefficients, and $f$ is primitive when its content is $1$. Dividing every coefficient by the content leaves a polynomial whose coefficients have gcd $1$; uniqueness up to sign is uniqueness of the gcd up to sign.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) If $f$ is reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ into two factors of positive degree, that is also a factorisation in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$; and a factorisation of a primitive $f$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ cannot have a constant non-unit factor, since that constant would divide the content. Conversely, if $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ with both of positive degree, Theorem 7.22 (p. 140) supplies rationals $a,b$ with $ag, bh \in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $f = (ag)(bh)$ — a factorisation in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ into factors of the same positive degrees. So $f$ is reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

(d) $2x+2$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, being of degree 1 (Problem 1(a)), and reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: $2x+2 = 2(x+1)$, where $2$ is not a unit of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ by aa-13 Problem 1(c). Its content is $2$, so it is not primitive, and the corollary does not apply. This is not a defect in the corollary — the two rings genuinely have different unit groups, and primitivity is precisely the condition that removes the difference.

Corollary 7.27 (p. 142) then upgrades this to the statement that $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD, which with aa-11 Problem 6(c) — $(2,x)$ is not principal — gives the UFD-not-a-PID witness aa-12 Problem 4(c) needed.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the rational root test)

(a) Prove Proposition 7.29 (p. 143): if $c = p/q$ in lowest terms is a root of $a_0 + a_1x+\cdots+a_dx^d\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$, then $p \mid a_0$ and $q\mid a_d$.

(b) Use it to prove $f(x) = 4x^3+2x^2-5x+3$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, and in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ — Example 7.30 (p. 144).

(c) Show $g(x) = 4x^3+2x^2-4x+3$ is reducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ — Example 7.31 (p. 144).

(d) Show $h(x) = x^4-3x^2+1$ has no rational root, and explain why that does **not** settle its irreducibility — Example 7.32 (p. 144).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Substitute and clear denominators: $a_0q^d + a_1pq^{d-1}+\cdots+a_dp^d = 0$. Then read the equation mod $p$ and mod $q$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) A cubic is reducible over a field exactly when it has a root, by Problem 1(b). List the candidates and test them.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) From $f(p/q)=0$, multiplying by $q^d$ gives
$$a_0q^d + a_1pq^{d-1} + \cdots + a_{d-1}p^{d-1}q + a_dp^d = 0.$$
Every term but the first has a factor $p$, so $p \mid a_0q^d$; and $\gcd(p,q)=1$ gives $\gcd(p,q^d)=1$ by aa-02 Problem 4's method, so $p\mid a_0$. Symmetrically every term but the last has a factor $q$, so $q\mid a_dp^d$ and hence $q\mid a_d$.

(b) Candidates $p/q$ with $p\mid3$ and $q\mid4$: $\pm1,\pm3,\pm\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac32,\pm\tfrac14,\pm\tfrac34$. None is a root — for instance $f(1)=4$, $f(-1)=6$, $f(\tfrac12)=1.5$, $f(-\tfrac32)=0$? No: $f(-\tfrac32) = 4(-\tfrac{27}8)+2(\tfrac94)+\tfrac{15}2+3 = -13.5+4.5+7.5+3 = 1.5$. So $f$ has no rational root, and being a cubic it is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ by Problem 1(b). Its content is $\gcd(4,2,5,3)=1$, so it is primitive and Corollary 7.26 (p. 141) carries irreducibility to $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The same candidate list, and $g(-\tfrac32) = 4(-\tfrac{27}{8}) + 2(\tfrac94) + 6 + 3 = -13.5 + 4.5 + 6 + 3 = 0$. So $x+\tfrac32$ divides $g$, i.e. $2x+3$ does, and $g$ is reducible. (Dividing: $g = (2x+3)(2x^2-2x+1)$, which multiplies out to $4x^3-4x^2+2x+6x^2-6x+3 = 4x^3+2x^2-4x+3$ ✓.)

(d) $h(x)=x^4-3x^2+1$ is monic with constant term $1$, so the only candidates are $\pm1$, and $h(\pm1) = 1-3+1 = -1\ne0$. So there is no rational root. But $h$ has degree $4$, and Problem 1(c) already showed that a degree-4 polynomial can be reducible with no roots — it might factor into two quadratics. The rational root test is a test for degree-1 factors only, and above degree 3 the absence of roots is not the absence of factors. (In fact $h = (x^2-x-1)(x^2+x-1)$, so $h$ really is reducible: expanding gives $x^4 + x^3 - x^2 - x^3 - x^2 + x - x^2 - x + 1 = x^4 - 3x^2 + 1$.)
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — Eisenstein, and what it proves that nothing else here does)

(a) Prove Theorem 7.35 (p. 145), Eisenstein's criterion: if $p$ is an irreducible integer with $p\nmid a_d$, $p\mid a_i$ for all $i<d$, and $p^2\nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

(b) Apply it to $x^2-p$ and deduce that $\sqrt p$ is irrational for every prime $p$ — Example 7.37 (p. 147).

(c) Apply it, after the substitution $x\mapsto x+1$, to the cyclotomic polynomial $\gamma(x) = 1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}$ — Example 7.39 (p. 147). You will need that $p \mid\binom{p}{k}$ for $0<k<p$.

(d) Deduce Corollary 7.38 (p. 147).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Suppose $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (legitimate by Gauss) and reduce mod $p$: the reduction is $\bar a_dx^d$. Then use Lemma 7.36 (p. 146).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $\gamma(x) = (x^p-1)/(x-1)$, so $\gamma(x+1) = ((x+1)^p-1)/x$. Expand by the binomial theorem.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By Theorem 7.22 (p. 140) it is enough to rule out a factorisation $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with both factors of positive degree. Reducing mod $p$ gives $\bar g\bar h = \bar a_dx^d$, a monomial, and $\bar a_d\ne0$. By Lemma 7.36 (p. 146), applied in the domain $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$, both $\bar g$ and $\bar h$ are monomials. Since $\deg g,\deg h\ge1$, both have zero constant term, so $p$ divides the constant terms of $g$ and of $h$ — whence $p^2 \mid a_0$, contradicting the hypothesis.

(b) $x^2-p$ has $a_0=-p$, $a_1=0$, $a_2=1$: $p\nmid1$, $p\mid p$, $p^2\nmid p$. So $x^2-p$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. If $\sqrt p$ were rational it would be a root, and Problem 1(b) would make $x^2-p$ reducible. So it is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\gamma(x)(x-1) = x^p-1$, so $\gamma(x+1)\cdot x = (x+1)^p - 1$, giving
$$\gamma(x+1) = \frac{(x+1)^p-1}{x} = x^{p-1} + \binom p1 x^{p-2} + \cdots + \binom p{p-2}x + \binom p{p-1}.$$
Every coefficient except the leading one is a binomial coefficient $\binom pk$ with $0<k<p$, hence divisible by $p$ — which is aa-04 Problem 4(a). The constant term is $\binom p{p-1} = p$, not divisible by $p^2$. And the leading coefficient is $1$. So Eisenstein applies to $\gamma(x+1)$, making it irreducible; and $x\mapsto x+1$ is a ring automorphism of $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ — with inverse $x\mapsto x-1$ — so irreducibility transfers back to $\gamma(x)$ by aa-08 Problem 5.

(d) $\gamma$ has degree $p-1$, and there are primes of every size, so $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ has irreducible polynomials of arbitrarily high degree. Nothing earlier in the chapter could produce them: the rational root test only detects degree-1 factors, and Proposition 7.15 only settles degrees 2 and 3. Eisenstein is the first tool that certifies irreducibility at unbounded degree, and it does it by throwing the question into a ring where degrees collapse.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — reduction mod p, and the limits of every test here)

(a) Prove Proposition 7.33 (p. 145): if $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ has leading coefficient not divisible by the irreducible integer $p$, and the reduction $\bar f$ is irreducible in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$, then $f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

(b) Show the leading-coefficient hypothesis cannot be dropped, using $p=2$ and $f(x) = 2x^2+x$.

(c) Show the converse fails badly. Verify that $x^4+1$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, and then factor it modulo $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$.

(d) In fact $x^4+1$ is reducible modulo **every** prime. Say why (c) does not prove that, and what kind of argument would.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Reduction is a ring homomorphism; a factorisation of $f$ reduces to a factorisation of $\bar f$, and the hypothesis on the leading coefficient keeps the degrees from dropping.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) For irreducibility over $\mathbb{Q}$, substitute $x\mapsto x+1$ and use Eisenstein at $p=2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Suppose $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ with both of positive degree; by Theorem 7.22 (p. 140) we may take $g,h\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ of the same degrees. Reducing mod $p$ gives $\bar f = \bar g\bar h$. Since $p$ does not divide the leading coefficient of $f$, $\deg\bar f = \deg f = \deg g+\deg h$; and $\deg\bar g\le\deg g$, $\deg\bar h\le\deg h$, so both must be equalities. Hence $\bar f$ factors into two polynomials of positive degree, contradicting its irreducibility.

(b) $f = 2x^2+x$ reduces mod $2$ to $\bar f = x$, which is irreducible in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$. But $f = x(2x+1)$ is reducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. The degree dropped from $2$ to $1$ under reduction, which is precisely what the hypothesis forbids.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) *Irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$:* $(x+1)^4+1 = x^4+4x^3+6x^2+4x+2$. Eisenstein at $p=2$: $2\nmid1$, $2$ divides $4, 6, 4, 2$, and $4\nmid2$. So the substituted polynomial is irreducible, hence so is $x^4+1$.

*Reducible mod every small prime:*
- $p=2$: $x^4+1 = (x+1)^4$, since squaring is additive in characteristic $2$.
- $p=3$: $x^4+1 = (x^2+x+2)(x^2+2x+2)$.
- $p=5$: $x^4+1 = (x^2+2)(x^2+3)$, since $(x^2+2)(x^2+3) = x^4+5x^2+6 \equiv x^4+1$.
- $p=7$: $x^4+1 = (x^2+3x+1)(x^2+4x+1)$, since the cross terms give $(3+4)x^3 = 0$ and $x^2(1+1+12) = 14x^2 = 0$.

So a polynomial with no factorisation over $\mathbb{Q}$ at all can factor modulo every prime one tries. Proposition 7.33 is a one-way test: irreducible mod some $p$ proves irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, and reducible mod every $p$ proves nothing.

(d) Four primes are four primes. Checking $2, 3, 5, 7$ — or four thousand primes — establishes nothing about all of them, and the gap is not one of diligence: it is the difference between a finite verification and a proof over an infinite family. The argument that does it goes through the structure of the multiplicative group of $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$: for odd $p$, one of $-1$, $2$, $-2$ is always a square mod $p$, and each case supplies a factorisation into quadratics. That is a genuine theorem, it is true, and it is not proved here — recorded as stated-not-proved, in the same spirit as aa-12 Problem 5(d).
</details>
