## Problem 1 (easy–medium — three-cycles generate)

(a) Prove Lemma 12.45 (p. 282): $\mathcal{A}_n$ is generated by $3$-cycles, for $n\ge2$.

(b) Show a product of two transpositions is a $3$-cycle or a product of two $3$-cycles, according to whether the transpositions share a point.

(c) Write $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ as a product of $3$-cycles.

(d) Why does the statement need $\mathcal{A}_n$ rather than $S_n$?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) An even permutation is a product of an even number of transpositions — pair them up.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $(a\,b)(a\,c) = (a\,c\,b)$; and $(a\,b)(c\,d) = (a\,b)(b\,c)\cdot(b\,c)(c\,d)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By aa-25 Problem 4(d), every permutation is a product of transpositions, and an even one is a product of an even number of them. Group them in consecutive pairs; by (b) each pair is a product of $3$-cycles. So the $3$-cycles generate $\mathcal{A}_n$, and every $3$-cycle is even by aa-25 Problem 4(c), so they generate no more.

(b) If the transpositions share a point: $(a\,b)(a\,c) = (a\,c\,b)$, a $3$-cycle. If they are disjoint: insert $(b\,c)(b\,c) = e$ to get
$$(a\,b)(c\,d) = \big[(a\,b)(b\,c)\big]\big[(b\,c)(c\,d)\big] = (a\,c\,b)\,(b\,d\,c)\ \text{-- up to the convention for composing},$$
a product of two $3$-cycles.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $(1\,2)(3\,4) = (1\,3\,2)(1\,3\,4)$. Check by tracking, composing right to left: $1\mapsto3\mapsto2$; $2\mapsto2\mapsto1$; $3\mapsto4\mapsto4$; $4\mapsto1\mapsto3$. That is $1\mapsto2$, $2\mapsto1$, $3\mapsto4$, $4\mapsto3$, which is $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ ✓.

(d) A $3$-cycle is even, so any product of $3$-cycles is even and lies in $\mathcal{A}_n$. The $3$-cycles therefore cannot generate $S_n$ for $n\ge2$ — the odd permutations are out of reach. Generating $S_n$ needs transpositions, which are odd, and $\mathcal{A}_n$ is exactly the part of $S_n$ the $3$-cycles can reach.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — conjugacy in Sₙ is cycle type)

(a) State Definition 12.47 (p. 283) and prove Lemma 12.46 (p. 283): conjugating a permutation by $\tau$ relabels its entries by $\tau$.

(b) Prove Corollary 12.48 (p. 283): two permutations are conjugate in $S_n$ if and only if they have the same type.

(c) List the types in $S_4$, compute each class size, and check they sum to $24$.

(d) Compute the number of permutations of each type directly and confirm your answer to (c).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $\tau(a_1\ \dots\ a_r)\tau^{-1} = (\tau(a_1)\ \dots\ \tau(a_r))$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Count arrangements: an $r$-cycle on a chosen $r$-subset can be written $r!/r$ ways.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For any $b$, $\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}$ sends $\tau(b)$ to $\tau(\sigma(b))$. So if $\sigma$ is the cycle $(a_1\dots a_r)$ then $\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}$ sends $\tau(a_i)$ to $\tau(a_{i+1})$, i.e. it is the cycle $(\tau(a_1)\dots\tau(a_r))$. Applying this to each cycle of a disjoint decomposition gives the general statement.

(b) ($\Rightarrow$) By (a), conjugation replaces each cycle by a cycle of the same length, so the type is unchanged. ($\Leftarrow$) Given two permutations of the same type, write both in disjoint-cycle form with the cycles in matching order and define $\tau$ by sending each entry of the first to the corresponding entry of the second; $\tau$ is a bijection, and by (a) it conjugates one to the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The types are the partitions of $4$: $1{+}1{+}1{+}1$, $2{+}1{+}1$, $2{+}2$, $3{+}1$, $4$. Class sizes $1, 6, 3, 8, 6$, summing to $1+6+3+8+6 = 24 = |S_4|$ ✓, as the class equation requires.

(d) Directly: the identity, $1$. Transpositions, $\binom42 = 6$. Double transpositions, $\tfrac12\binom42 = 3$ — choosing the pair for the first cycle counts each element twice. $3$-cycles, $\binom43\cdot2 = 8$, there being two $3$-cycles on each triple. $4$-cycles, $3! = 6$, fixing the first entry and permuting the rest. The counts agree.

Note the contrast with $\mathcal{A}_4$, whose class sizes are $1,3,4,4$ (aa-28 Problem 1(d)): the $8$ three-cycles form **one** $S_4$-class and split into **two** $\mathcal{A}_4$-classes of size $4$. Conjugacy is a property of the pair (element, group), exactly as normality was in aa-26 Problem 6.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — when three-cycles fuse)

(a) Prove Lemma 12.50 (p. 284): for $n\ge5$, all $3$-cycles are conjugate **in $\mathcal{A}_n$**.

(b) Show this fails for $n = 4$.

(c) Explain the mechanism: given $\sigma,\sigma'$ of the same type, when can the conjugating $\tau$ be chosen even?

(d) Why is $n\ge5$ the right threshold?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) If the $\tau$ from Problem 2(b) is odd, fix it by multiplying by a transposition that commutes with $\sigma$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The repair in (a) needs two points outside the $3$-cycle.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $\sigma,\sigma'$ be $3$-cycles. By Corollary 12.48 there is $\tau\in S_n$ with $\tau\sigma\tau^{-1} = \sigma'$. If $\tau$ is even we are done. If $\tau$ is odd, pick two points $c,d$ outside the support of $\sigma$ — possible when $n\ge5$, since a $3$-cycle moves only three points — and replace $\tau$ by $\tau(c\,d)$. That is even, and
$$\tau(c\,d)\,\sigma\,(c\,d)^{-1}\tau^{-1} = \tau\sigma\tau^{-1} = \sigma',$$
because $(c\,d)$ commutes with $\sigma$, their supports being disjoint.

(b) In $\mathcal{A}_4$ the eight $3$-cycles split into two classes of four — aa-28 Problem 1(d). Concretely $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2)$ are not conjugate in $\mathcal{A}_4$, though they are in $S_4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The conjugating $\tau$ is never unique: it may be replaced by $\tau\rho$ for any $\rho$ in the centraliser of $\sigma$. So $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ are conjugate in $\mathcal{A}_n$ exactly when *some* element of the coset $\tau\cdot Z_{S_n}(\sigma)$ is even — equivalently, when the centraliser of $\sigma$ in $S_n$ contains an odd permutation. For a $3$-cycle with $n\ge5$, the transposition $(c\,d)$ on two untouched points is such an element.

(d) At $n=4$ a $3$-cycle moves three of the four points, leaving only one fixed, so there is no transposition disjoint from it, and the centraliser of $(1\,2\,3)$ in $S_4$ is $\langle(1\,2\,3)\rangle$ — all even. The repair has nothing to use. At $n\ge5$ there are always two spare points. So the threshold is not an artefact of the proof: it is where the second spare point appears, and $\mathcal{A}_4$ is genuinely different, being the one alternating group above $\mathcal{A}_3$ that is not simple.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — Aₙ is simple for n ≥ 5)

(a) State Lemma 12.51 (p. 285).

(b) Prove Theorem 12.52 (p. 286): $\mathcal{A}_n$ is simple for $n\ge5$, given Lemmas 12.45, 12.50 and 12.51.

(c) Where does the proof use each of the three lemmas?

(d) Check the theorem's sharpness: is $\mathcal{A}_3$ simple? $\mathcal{A}_4$?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Let $N\ne\{e\}$ be normal; show $N$ contains a $3$-cycle, then all of them, then everything.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) One lemma gets you into the $3$-cycles, one spreads you across them, one gets you out to all of $\mathcal{A}_n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Lemma 12.51: for $n\ge5$ and $\sigma\in\mathcal{A}_n$ not the identity and not a $3$-cycle, there is an even permutation $\tau$ such that the commutator-type element built from $\sigma$ and $\tau$ is a non-identity element moving strictly fewer points — the technical step that lets an induction reach a $3$-cycle.

(b) Let $N\trianglelefteq\mathcal{A}_n$ with $N\ne\{e\}$, and take $\sigma\in N$, $\sigma\ne e$, moving as few points as possible. If $\sigma$ is not a $3$-cycle, Lemma 12.51 produces a non-identity element of $N$ — normality keeps it inside $N$ — moving fewer points, contradicting minimality. So $\sigma$ is a $3$-cycle.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) By Lemma 12.50 (p. 284) every $3$-cycle is conjugate to $\sigma$ in $\mathcal{A}_n$, and $N$ is normal, so $N$ contains **every** $3$-cycle. By Lemma 12.45 (p. 282) the $3$-cycles generate $\mathcal{A}_n$, so $N = \mathcal{A}_n$. Hence the only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $\mathcal{A}_n$, and $\mathcal{A}_n$ is simple.

(c) **Lemma 12.51** gets you *into* the $3$-cycles: it drives an arbitrary non-identity element of $N$ down to one. **Lemma 12.50** spreads you *across* them: one $3$-cycle in $N$ forces all of them, using normality. **Lemma 12.45** gets you *out* to the whole group: all $3$-cycles force everything. Three lemmas, three distinct jobs, and each is where the hypothesis $n\ge5$ or the evenness matters.

(d) $\mathcal{A}_3\cong C_3$ has prime order, so it is simple — but abelian, so it is not a *noncommutative* simple group. $\mathcal{A}_4$ is **not** simple: $V$ is a normal subgroup of order $4$ (aa-26 Problem 6(a)), and Problem 3(b) shows why the proof above breaks there. So $\mathcal{A}_5$, of order $60$, is the smallest noncommutative simple group — which is the sharpness claimed by Proposition 12.42 (p. 280) and quoted in aa-29 Problem 6(e).
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — solvable groups)

(a) State Definition 12.53 (p. 287).

(b) Prove Example 12.54 (p. 287): abelian groups are solvable.

(c) Prove Example 12.55 (p. 287): dihedral groups are solvable; and Example 12.56 (p. 287): $S_3$ and $S_4$ are solvable.

(d) Prove Example 12.57 (p. 287): $p$-groups are solvable.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) $D_{2n}\supseteq\langle y\rangle\supseteq\{e\}$, with $\langle y\rangle$ normal by aa-26 Problem 6(e).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Induct using the nontrivial centre from aa-28 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $G$ is solvable if there is a chain $G = G_0\supseteq G_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq G_k = \{e\}$ with each $G_{i+1}$ normal in $G_i$ and each quotient $G_i/G_{i+1}$ abelian.

(b) For abelian $G$ the chain $G\supseteq\{e\}$ works: $\{e\}$ is normal and $G/\{e\}\cong G$ is abelian.

(c) $D_{2n}\supseteq\langle y\rangle\supseteq\{e\}$. The rotation subgroup is normal (aa-26 Problem 6(e)) with quotient of order $2$, hence abelian; and $\langle y\rangle\cong C_n$ is abelian. So $D_{2n}$ is solvable, and $S_3\cong D_6$ with it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c, cont.) For $S_4$, take
$$S_4\ \supseteq\ \mathcal{A}_4\ \supseteq\ V\ \supseteq\ \{e\}.$$
$\mathcal{A}_4\trianglelefteq S_4$ with quotient $C_2$; $V\trianglelefteq\mathcal{A}_4$ with quotient of order $3$, hence $C_3$ (aa-27 Problem 5(b)); and $V\cong C_2\times C_2$ is abelian. All three quotients are abelian, so $S_4$ is solvable.

(d) Induct on $|G| = p^r$. If $r=0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $Z(G)$ is nontrivial by aa-28 Problem 6(a), and $G/Z(G)$ is a $p$-group of smaller order, so solvable by induction; splice its chain, pulled back to $G$, onto $G\supseteq Z(G)\supseteq\{e\}$, whose own quotient $Z(G)$ is abelian. Every quotient in the resulting chain is abelian, so $G$ is solvable.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — where solvability stops)

(a) Prove Example 12.59 (p. 288): a noncommutative simple group is not solvable.

(b) Prove Proposition 12.60 (p. 288): subgroups and homomorphic images of solvable groups are solvable.

(c) Deduce that $S_n$ is not solvable for $n\ge5$.

(d) Contrast with Problem 5(c): $S_4$ is solvable and $S_5$ is not. Identify the exact point in the chain for $S_4$ that has no analogue for $S_5$.

(e) Example 12.58 (p. 287) states that every group of odd order is solvable, and calls the proof not "fairly easy". Say what is being quoted here and what this set proves.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) A solvable chain for a simple group can only be $G\supseteq\{e\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $\mathcal{A}_n\le S_n$ is simple and noncommutative for $n\ge5$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $G$ be simple and noncommutative, and suppose $G = G_0\supseteq G_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\{e\}$ is a solvable chain. $G_1$ is normal in $G$, so it is $\{e\}$ or $G$. If $G_1 = G$ the chain never descends and can be truncated, so we may take $G_1 = \{e\}$ — whence $G/G_1\cong G$ must be abelian, contradicting noncommutativity.

(b) *Subgroups:* given a solvable chain $\{G_i\}$ for $G$ and $H\le G$, the chain $H_i = H\cap G_i$ has $H_{i+1}\trianglelefteq H_i$, and $H_i/H_{i+1}$ embeds in $G_i/G_{i+1}$ by the Second Isomorphism Theorem (aa-27 Problem 3(c)), hence is abelian — a subgroup of an abelian group.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) *Images:* for a surjection $\varphi : G\to K$, the chain $\varphi(G_i)$ has $\varphi(G_{i+1})\trianglelefteq\varphi(G_i)$, and $\varphi$ induces a surjection $G_i/G_{i+1}\to\varphi(G_i)/\varphi(G_{i+1})$; a quotient of an abelian group is abelian.

(c) For $n\ge5$, $\mathcal{A}_n$ is simple by Theorem 12.52 (p. 286) and noncommutative — $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,2\,4)$ do not commute — so it is not solvable by (a). It is a subgroup of $S_n$, so by (b) $S_n$ is not solvable either.

(d) The chain for $S_4$ was $S_4\supseteq\mathcal{A}_4\supseteq V\supseteq\{e\}$, and its second step is the one with no analogue: $\mathcal{A}_4$ has the proper nontrivial normal subgroup $V$, while $\mathcal{A}_5$ has none at all. So for $S_5$ the chain stalls at $\mathcal{A}_5$, whose only continuation is $\{e\}$, and $\mathcal{A}_5$ is not abelian. **Everything about the difference between $S_4$ and $S_5$ is the existence of $V$**, and $V$ exists because the three ways of splitting four points into two pairs are permuted among themselves — a coincidence available at $4$ and at no larger $n$.

This is the group-theoretic reason the general quintic has no solution by radicals and the quartic does, which is the theorem chapter 15 builds towards.

(e) Quoted: the **Feit–Thompson theorem**, that every finite group of odd order is solvable. It is true, its proof runs to hundreds of pages, and nothing here contributes to it. What this set proves is the opposite-direction statement it makes sharp: $\mathcal{A}_5$, of even order $60$, is simple and noncommutative, hence not solvable — so "odd order" in Feit–Thompson cannot be dropped, and $60$ is where the obstruction first appears.
</details>
