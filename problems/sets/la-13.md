## Problem 1 (easy — testing the axioms)

Show that the function taking $((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2))\in\mathbb{R}^2\times\mathbb{R}^2$ to
$$|x_1y_1| + |x_2y_2|$$
is **not** an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Name the axiom of Definition 6.3 that fails and give an explicit counterexample.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 1, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Absolute values destroy signs. Which of the five conditions in Definition 6.3 involves a scalar that could be negative?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Homogeneity in the first slot demands $\langle\lambda u,v\rangle=\lambda\langle u,v\rangle$ for *every* $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, negative ones included. The proposed function returns $|\lambda|$ times the original value instead. Choose $\lambda=-1$ and a pair on which the value is nonzero.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Take $u=v=(1,0)$ and $\lambda=-1$. The function gives $|(-1)(1)|+|0|=1$ at $(\lambda u,v)$, while $\lambda$ times its value at $(u,v)$ is $-1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $1\ne-1$, homogeneity in the first slot fails and the function is not an inner product. Two remarks worth making. Additivity in the first slot also fails — take $u=(1,0)$, $v=(-1,0)$, $w=(1,0)$, where the left side is $0$ and the right side $2$ — and exhibiting either failure is enough, though naming which one you are refuting is not optional. And note what the function *does* satisfy: positivity, definiteness and symmetry all hold, so the failure is not a matter of the function being obviously unreasonable. **Any correct counterexample, with the failing axiom named, earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — computing an orthogonal decomposition)

Find vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $u$ is a scalar multiple of $(1,3)$, $v$ is orthogonal to $(1,3)$, and
$$(1,2)=u+v.$$
Verify all three conditions.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 10, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
This is Theorem 6.14 with its letters renamed. Which vector plays the role of the one being decomposed, and which the direction being projected onto?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply Theorem 6.14 with the vector $(1,2)$ in the role of $u$ and $(1,3)$ in the role of $v$. Compute $c=\langle(1,2),(1,3)\rangle/\|(1,3)\|^2$ using the Euclidean inner product, then take $u=c(1,3)$ and $v=(1,2)-u$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\langle(1,2),(1,3)\rangle=1\cdot1+2\cdot3=7$ and $\|(1,3)\|^2=1+9=10$, so $c=\tfrac{7}{10}$ and $u=\left(\tfrac{7}{10},\tfrac{21}{10}\right)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Then $v=(1,2)-u=\left(\tfrac{3}{10},-\tfrac{1}{10}\right)$. Verify: $u$ is $\tfrac{7}{10}$ times $(1,3)$ by construction; $\langle v,(1,3)\rangle=\tfrac{3}{10}-\tfrac{3}{10}=0$; and $u+v=(1,2)$ by construction. Do all three checks explicitly rather than trusting the theorem — the arithmetic check on orthogonality is the one that catches a slip in $c$, and it costs one line.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, equality in Cauchy–Schwarz at the extreme)

Suppose $u,v\in V$ with $\|u\|=\|v\|=1$ and $\langle u,v\rangle=1$. Prove that $u=v$.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 8, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
To prove two vectors are equal in an inner product space, show their difference has norm $0$ and appeal to definiteness. Square first.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Expand $\|u-v\|^2=\langle u-v,u-v\rangle$ using additivity in both slots. You will get $\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2-\langle u,v\rangle-\langle v,u\rangle$, and conjugate symmetry evaluates the last term from the given one. Then use Theorem 6.10(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\langle v,u\rangle=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle}=\overline{1}=1$, so $\|u-v\|^2=1+1-1-1=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By Theorem 6.10(a), $\|u-v\|=0$ forces $u-v=0$, that is $u=v$. Two points. Say why $\langle v,u\rangle=1$ rather than assuming symmetry — over $\mathbb{C}$ the step is conjugate symmetry and the conjugate of $1$ happening to be $1$ is what makes it painless. And note the relationship to Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\langle u,v\rangle|=1=\|u\|\|v\|$ is the equality case, so Theorem 6.15 already tells you one vector is a multiple of the other; this problem pins down which multiple, and the direct computation is shorter than that route.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, an identity characterising equal norms)

Suppose $u,v\in V$. Prove that
$$\|au+bv\| = \|bu+av\|$$
for all $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ **if and only if** $\|u\|=\|v\|$.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 7, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction needs only a single well-chosen pair $(a,b)$. The other needs the general expansion — square both sides and compare.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) Put $a=1$, $b=0$. ($\Leftarrow$) Expand $\|au+bv\|^2$ with $a,b$ real: the cross terms give $ab(\langle u,v\rangle+\langle v,u\rangle)=2ab\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle$, which is *symmetric in $a$ and $b$* and so appears identically on both sides. Everything then reduces to comparing the two square terms.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\|au+bv\|^2=a^2\|u\|^2+b^2\|v\|^2+2ab\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\|bu+av\|^2=b^2\|u\|^2+a^2\|v\|^2+2ab\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle$. Subtracting, the difference is $(a^2-b^2)(\|u\|^2-\|v\|^2)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If $\|u\|=\|v\|$ that difference is $0$ for every $a,b$, so the two norms agree. Conversely, taking $a=1,b=0$ gives $\|u\|=\|v\|$ immediately.

Two things earn the marks. Say where $a,b$ being **real** is used: the cross terms would not combine into $2ab\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle$ for complex scalars, since $\langle au,bv\rangle=a\bar b\langle u,v\rangle$ picks up a conjugate. And notice how much the factorised form $(a^2-b^2)(\|u\|^2-\|v\|^2)$ tells you at a glance: it shows the identity can also hold for particular $a,b$ with $a^2=b^2$ without $\|u\|=\|v\|$, which is why the "for all $a,b$" in the statement is essential.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — orthogonality as a minimisation)

Suppose $u,v\in V$. Prove that $\langle u,v\rangle=0$ **if and only if**
$$\|u\| \le \|u+av\|$$
for all $a\in\mathbb{F}$.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 6, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The forward direction is Pythagoras. For the converse, the hypothesis holds for *every* $a$, so you may choose the one that makes $u+av$ as small as possible — and Theorem 6.14 tells you which $a$ that is.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) If $\langle u,v\rangle=0$ then $u$ is orthogonal to $av$ for every $a$, so Theorem 6.13 gives $\|u+av\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|av\|^2\ge\|u\|^2$. ($\Leftarrow$) Dispose of $v=0$ first, then set $c=\langle u,v\rangle/\|v\|^2$ and take $a=-c$, so that $u+av$ is exactly the orthogonal remainder $w$ of Theorem 6.14.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
With $u=cv+w$ and $w$ orthogonal to $v$, Pythagoras gives $\|u\|^2=|c|^2\|v\|^2+\|w\|^2$, while $u+(-c)v=w$ so $\|u+(-c)v\|^2=\|w\|^2$. The hypothesis at $a=-c$ then reads $|c|^2\|v\|^2+\|w\|^2\le\|w\|^2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Hence $|c|^2\|v\|^2\le0$, and since $\|v\|\ne0$ this forces $c=0$, i.e. $\langle u,v\rangle=0$.

Three points. Handle $v=0$ separately — the conclusion is then immediate from Theorem 6.7, and the division defining $c$ would be illegal. State plainly what the equivalence *means*: $\langle u,v\rangle=0$ exactly when $a=0$ minimises the distance from $u$ to the line of multiples of $v$, which is the reason orthogonal projection is the best approximation and is the idea la-14 builds on. And note that only one value of $a$ is needed in the converse, even though the hypothesis supplies all of them — choosing the right one is the entire proof.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a norm bound forces invertibility) [interleaves la-08]

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is such that $\|Tv\|\le\|v\|$ for every $v\in V$. Prove that $T-\sqrt2\,I$ is invertible.

*(Axler §6.A, Exercise 5, p. 175)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Do not try to construct an inverse. Show instead that nothing nonzero is sent to $0$, and let an earlier theorem convert that into invertibility.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Suppose $(T-\sqrt2\,I)v=0$ for some $v$. That says $Tv=\sqrt2\,v$, so compute $\|Tv\|$ and compare it with the hypothesis. Then use la-08's Theorem 3.69 to promote injectivity to invertibility — and be clear that this is where finite-dimensionality is spent.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\|Tv\|=\|\sqrt2\,v\|=\sqrt2\,\|v\|$ by Theorem 6.10(b). The hypothesis gives $\sqrt2\,\|v\|\le\|v\|$, so $(\sqrt2-1)\|v\|\le0$ and therefore $\|v\|\le0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since norms are nonnegative, $\|v\|=0$, so $v=0$ by Theorem 6.10(a). Hence $\operatorname{null}(T-\sqrt2\,I)=\{0\}$ and $T-\sqrt2\,I$ is injective (la-06, Theorem 3.16), so invertible by Theorem 3.69.

Two points of honesty for the write-up. Axler states this exercise without assuming $V$ finite-dimensional; the argument above establishes **injectivity** with no such hypothesis, and it is only the final step that needs it. Say so rather than passing over it. And note what makes $\sqrt2$ work: any scalar of modulus greater than $1$ would do, and the hypothesis $\|Tv\|\le\|v\|$ is exactly what rules such scalars out as eigenvalues.
</details>
