## Problem 1 (easy — quantifier order) [interleaves an-03]

Abbott reverses the two quantifiers of Definition 2.2.3 and names the result:

> A sequence $(x_n)$ **verconges** to $x$ if *there exists* an $\epsilon > 0$ such that *for all* $N \in \mathbf{N}$ it is true that $n \geq N$ implies $|x_n - x| < \epsilon$.

(a) Give a vercongent sequence. (b) Give a vercongent sequence that diverges. (c) State exactly what property of a sequence "verconges to some $x$" is, and prove your answer in both directions.

*(Abbott §2.2, Exercise 2.2.2, p. 43)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Who chooses first? In the real definition the adversary picks $\epsilon$; here you do, once, and it must then survive every $N$. An advantage that large usually means the condition has collapsed into something much weaker.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), try to prove the two implications separately: "vercongent ⟹ ___" and "___ ⟹ vercongent". For the first, ask what taking $N = 1$ buys you. For the second, you get to build $\epsilon$ out of the bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Taking $N = 1$, the condition says every term of the sequence lies in $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon)$. Conversely, if $|x_n| \le M$ for all $n$, choose $\epsilon = M + |x| + 1$ and check it works for every $N$ at once.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The answer to (c) is **boundedness** — vercongence is not a weak form of convergence, it is a different property with convergence's syntax. For (b), $1, 0, 1, 0, \ldots$ verconges to 0 (take $\epsilon = 2$) and converges to nothing. Say explicitly, in one sentence, which sequences verconge to *every* real number and why that is not paradoxical.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — a linear limit, both phases)

Use Definition 6.8 to prove that $\lim_{x \to -2} (4x + 3) = -5$.

Show Scratch Work and Proof separately. In the Proof, justify each step; in particular, say where $0 < |x - (-2)| < \delta$ is used.

*(Cummings, Real Analysis, §6 chapter exercises, Exercise 6.1(a), p. 270; the model is Example 6.9, p. 231)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$|(4x + 3) - (-5)|$ simplifies to a constant times $|x + 2|$. Find the constant; it is your whole problem.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Scratch Work: assume $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$ and unwind until only $|x + 2|$ is on the left. Whatever bound appears on the right is your $\delta$. Then write the Proof in the reverse direction, starting "Let $\epsilon > 0$. Let $\delta = \ldots$".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$|(4x+3) + 5| = |4x + 8| = 4|x + 2|$, and $4|x+2| < \epsilon$ exactly when $|x + 2| < \epsilon/4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Take $\delta = \epsilon/4$. Proof: let $\epsilon > 0$ and $\delta = \epsilon/4 > 0$; for any $x$ with $0 < |x + 2| < \delta$, $|(4x+3) - (-5)| = 4|x+2| < 4\delta = \epsilon$. Note that here every scratch-work step was reversible — an equality chain and one inequality — which is *why* the reversal is legitimate. Problem 3 is the case where it is not.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the min-trick)

Use the $\epsilon$–$\delta$ definition of continuity (Definition 6.16) to prove that $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2 + 2x + 1$, is continuous at $x = 1$.

Then answer: your $\delta$ has two constraints. State what each one buys, and explain in one sentence why dropping the first makes the proof false rather than merely weaker.

*(Cummings, Real Analysis, §6 chapter exercises, Exercise 6.5, p. 270)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$f(1) = 4$. Factor $x^2 + 2x + 1 - 4$ and look for a factor of $|x - 1|$; the other factor is the one you cannot control directly.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$x^2 + 2x + 1 = (x+1)^2$, so $|f(x) - 4| = |(x+1)^2 - 4| = |x - 1|\,|x + 3|$. Confine $x$ first — insist $\delta \le 1$ — to bound $|x+3|$, then choose the second constraint to convert that bound into $\epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $\delta \le 1$ then $0 < x < 2$, so $|x + 3| < 5$. Hence $|f(x) - 4| < 5|x - 1|$ on that window, and $5|x-1| < \epsilon$ once $|x - 1| < \epsilon/5$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Take $\delta = \min(1, \epsilon/5)$. In the Proof, the bound $|x+3| < 5$ must be *derived* from $\delta \le 1$ before it is used — it is not available otherwise, and this is exactly the step that makes the scratch work irreversible: it is a one-way $<$, not an $\iff$. Note also that continuity, unlike Definition 6.8, does not require $0 < |x - 1|$; say why that costs nothing here.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — a limit that does not exist) [interleaves pw-01]

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{|x-3|}{x-3} + 4$. Prove that $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$ does not exist.

State the negation of Definition 6.8 explicitly before you use it, and name the domain and codomain of $f$.

*(Cummings, Real Analysis, §6 chapter exercises, Exercise 6.2, p. 270)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
To refute "$\lim_{x\to 3} f(x) = L$" you must beat *every* candidate $L$, not just the obvious ones — so the proof begins "Suppose, for contradiction, that $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = L$ for some $L \in \mathbb{R}$."
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Negating $\forall \epsilon\, \exists \delta\, \forall x$ gives $\exists \epsilon\, \forall \delta\, \exists x$: you name one $\epsilon$, and then, for whatever $\delta$ you are handed, you produce a witness $x$ inside the punctured window that escapes the band. Note that $f$ takes only two values.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$f(x) = 5$ for $x > 3$ and $f(x) = 3$ for $x < 3$; the domain is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$. Take $\epsilon = 1$. Whatever $\delta > 0$ is given, both $3 + \delta/2$ and $3 - \delta/2$ lie in the punctured window, and their images are 5 and 3.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If both $|5 - L| < 1$ and $|3 - L| < 1$ then $2 = |5 - 3| \le |5 - L| + |L - 3| < 2$, a contradiction. So no $L$ works and the limit does not exist. This is pw-01's contradiction shape wrapped around a quantifier negation — write out both layers rather than eliding the outer one.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — a false statement, and a definition you design) [interleaves an-03]

(a) Cummings states this and says it is **false**:

> If $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$ for all $x$ for which $0 < |x - a| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon/2$ for all $x$ for which $0 < |x - a| < \delta/2$.

Refute it with an explicit counterexample: give $f$ with its domain and codomain, and give $a$, $L$, $\epsilon$ and $\delta$. Then say in one sentence what mistaken belief about $\delta$ the statement encodes.

(b) Imitate the logical structure of Definition 2.2.3 to write a rigorous definition of $\lim x_n = \infty$, and use your definition to prove $\lim \sqrt{n} = \infty$. Then say what your definition asserts about the sequence $1, 0, 2, 0, 3, 0, 4, 0, \ldots$.

*(a: Cummings, Real Analysis, §6 chapter exercises, Exercise 6.3, p. 270. b: Abbott §2.2, Exercise 2.2.7, p. 44)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The claim assumes shrinking the window shrinks the output spread proportionally. Nothing in the definition says that. What is the simplest function whose output spread does not shrink at all?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) A constant function makes $|f(x) - L|$ the same number for every $x$, so halving $\delta$ changes nothing on the left while the right-hand tolerance halves. Choose $L$ so that the original $\epsilon$ works and $\epsilon/2$ does not. (b) Replace "$|x_n - a| < \epsilon$" — a statement that the terms are eventually *close to* something — with a statement that they are eventually *beyond* something; your quantifier is then over bounds $M$ rather than tolerances $\epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Try $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(x) = 1$ for all $x$, $a = 0$, $L = 0$, $\epsilon = 2$, $\delta = 1$. The hypothesis holds since $|1 - 0| = 1 < 2$. What does the conclusion claim? (b) "For all $M \in \mathbb{R}$ there exists $N \in \mathbf{N}$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $x_n > M$."
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) The conclusion claims $|1 - 0| < 1$, which is false, so the statement is refuted with no analysis at all. The mistaken belief is that $\delta$ and $\epsilon$ are tied together proportionally — that the definition promises a *rate*. It promises only existence: for each $\epsilon$ some $\delta$ works, and a $\delta$ that works for $\epsilon$ keeps working for every larger tolerance and says nothing about smaller ones. (b) Given $M$, take any $N > M^2$; then $n \ge N$ gives $\sqrt{n} \ge \sqrt{N} > |M| \ge M$. For $1, 0, 2, 0, 3, 0, \ldots$ the definition fails — the zero terms return forever, so no $N$ works for $M = 1$ — even though the sequence is unbounded. State that consequence explicitly: "unbounded" and "diverges to infinity" are different properties, and the definition you wrote is what separates them.
</details>
