## Problem 1 (easy — proof, when a product stays self-adjoint)

Suppose $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ are self-adjoint. Prove that $ST$ is self-adjoint **if and only if** $ST=TS$.

*(Axler §7.A, Exercise 7, p. 215)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Compute $(ST)^*$ using Theorem 7.6(e) and the hypothesis on $S$ and $T$. The whole problem is that one computation followed by unwinding a definition.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$(ST)^*=T^*S^*=TS$, the second equality because both operators are self-adjoint. Then "$ST$ is self-adjoint" means $ST=(ST)^*$ by Definition 7.11, and you have just evaluated the right-hand side. Both directions come out of the same identity read in the two directions.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$ST$ self-adjoint $\iff ST=(ST)^* \iff ST=TS$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
That chain is the proof, but write it as two implications or make clear that every step is an equivalence — a chain of "$\iff$" is a legitimate proof of an "if and only if" only if each link really is reversible, and here each is.

Note the order-reversal in Theorem 7.6(e) is what creates the whole phenomenon: without it $(ST)^*$ would be $S^*T^*=ST$ and the product of self-adjoint operators would always be self-adjoint. Say so, and note that this is the same reversal as la-08's $(ST)^{-1}=T^{-1}S^{-1}$ and la-10's $(ST)'=T'S'$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — a subspace over $\mathbb{R}$ but not over $\mathbb{C}$) [interleaves la-02]

(a) Suppose $V$ is a real inner product space. Show that the set of self-adjoint operators on $V$ is a subspace of $\mathcal{L}(V)$.

(b) Suppose $V$ is a complex inner product space with $V\ne\{0\}$. Show that the set of self-adjoint operators on $V$ is **not** a subspace of $\mathcal{L}(V)$.

*(Axler §7.A, Exercises 8 and 9, p. 215)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Both parts run through la-02's three-condition subspace test. Only one of the three conditions behaves differently over the two fields — find it before writing anything.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Closure under addition is Theorem 7.6(a) and is field-independent, and the zero operator is self-adjoint. Closure under scalar multiplication is Theorem 7.6(b): $(\lambda T)^*=\overline{\lambda}T^*$, which equals $\lambda T$ only when $\overline{\lambda}=\lambda$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ that is automatic; over $\mathbb{C}$ pick $\lambda=i$ and a nonzero self-adjoint operator to break it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The identity operator $I$ is self-adjoint (Theorem 7.6(d)) and nonzero since $V\ne\{0\}$. But $(iI)^*=\overline{i}\,I=-iI\ne iI$, so $iI$ is not self-adjoint.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Hence the set is not closed under scalar multiplication over $\mathbb{C}$ and is not a subspace. For (a), write out all three checks: $0^*=0$; $(S+T)^*=S^*+T^*=S+T$; and $(\lambda T)^*=\overline{\lambda}T^*=\lambda T$ for real $\lambda$.

Say explicitly why $V\ne\{0\}$ is needed in (b) — on the zero space every operator is the zero operator, which is self-adjoint, and the set is trivially a subspace. And connect the result to the lesson's analogy: the self-adjoint operators behave like the reals inside $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{R}$ is a real subspace of $\mathbb{C}$ but not a complex one, for exactly the same reason.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, a polynomial that annihilates) [interleaves la-12]

Suppose $T$ is a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space and that $2$ and $3$ are the only eigenvalues of $T$. Prove that
$$T^2-5T+6I=0.$$

*(Axler §7.B, Exercise 2, p. 223)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$T^2-5T+6I$ is $p(T)$ for $p(x)=(x-2)(x-3)$. To prove an operator is zero it is enough to show it kills a basis — and self-adjointness hands you a very convenient one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The Real Spectral Theorem gives an orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ of eigenvectors (over $\mathbb{C}$ use instead that a self-adjoint operator is normal and apply the Complex Spectral Theorem — say which case you are in). Each $e_j$ has $Te_j=\lambda_je_j$ with $\lambda_j\in\{2,3\}$. Then use la-12's identity $p(T)v=p(\lambda)v$ for an eigenvector.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(T^2-5T+6I)e_j=(\lambda_j^2-5\lambda_j+6)e_j=(\lambda_j-2)(\lambda_j-3)e_j=0$, since $\lambda_j$ is $2$ or $3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So $T^2-5T+6I$ vanishes on a basis, hence on all of $V$ by linearity — write that last step out, since "agrees on a basis" implies "equal" is la-05's Theorem 3.5 and not an observation.

Two things worth stating. Self-adjointness is doing real work: without it the conclusion fails, and the next problem asks you to build the counterexample. And the polynomial $(x-2)(x-3)$ is the minimal polynomial of $T$, which la-12 flagged as Axler §8.C and out of scope — this problem is a special case you can prove with the tools in hand.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the same hypotheses without self-adjointness)

Give an example of an operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^3)$ such that $2$ and $3$ are the only eigenvalues of $T$ and
$$T^2-5T+6I\ne0.$$

*(Axler §7.B, Exercise 3, p. 223)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Problem 3 used a basis of eigenvectors. So build an operator whose eigenvalues are $2$ and $3$ and which has *too few* eigenvectors — la-12's non-diagonalisable example is the shape to copy.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $T(z_1,z_2,z_3)=(2z_1+z_2,\ 2z_2,\ 3z_3)$, whose matrix in the standard basis is upper triangular with diagonal $2,2,3$. Read the eigenvalues off that diagonal with la-12's Theorem 5.32, then evaluate $T^2-5T+6I$ at a well-chosen vector — one that is *not* an eigenvector.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
By Theorem 5.32 the eigenvalues are exactly $2$ and $3$. Take $v=(0,1,0)$: $Tv=(1,2,0)$ and $T^2v=T(1,2,0)=(4,4,0)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Then $(T^2-5T+6I)v=(4,4,0)-5(1,2,0)+6(0,1,0)=(-1,0,0)\ne0$, so the operator is not zero.

Two points. Verify the eigenvalue claim rather than asserting it — the diagonal of an upper-triangular matrix gives them by Theorem 5.32, and that citation is the whole justification. And say what this example shows against Problem 3: the operator is not self-adjoint (its matrix is not symmetric, and is not even normal), and by the Complex Spectral Theorem it cannot be, since it is not diagonalisable. **Any correct example, fully verified, earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the self-adjointness hypothesis in 7.26)

Give an example of a real inner product space $V$, an operator $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, and real numbers $b,c$ with $b^2<4c$, such that $T^2+bT+cI$ is **not** invertible. Explain what this shows about Theorem 7.26.

*(Axler §7.B, Exercise 10, p. 224)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You need $T$ not self-adjoint. The most familiar non-self-adjoint operator on a real inner product space is one this module has used repeatedly — and its square is remarkably simple.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $V=\mathbb{R}^2$ and $T(x,y)=(-y,x)$, la-11's rotation by $90^\circ$. Compute $T^2$ directly. Then choose $b$ and $c$ satisfying $b^2<4c$ that make $T^2+bT+cI$ collapse — with $T^2$ as simple as it is, the choice is forced and easy.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$T^2(x,y)=T(-y,x)=(-x,-y)$, so $T^2=-I$. Take $b=0$ and $c=1$: then $b^2=0<4=4c$, and $T^2+0\cdot T+1\cdot I=-I+I=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The zero operator on a nonzero space is not invertible, so the conclusion of Theorem 7.26 fails. Check the hypothesis that has been dropped: $T$ is not self-adjoint, since its matrix in the standard orthonormal basis has rows $(0,-1),(1,0)$ and its transpose has rows $(0,1),(-1,0)$, so $T^*=-T\ne T$.

What this shows is worth stating carefully. Theorem 7.26's hypothesis is not an artefact of the proof: it is needed for the *conclusion*, even over $\mathbb{R}$. And since Theorem 7.26 is what makes the irreducible quadratic factors invertible in the proof of Theorem 7.27, this example is also why la-11's rotation has no eigenvalue — the quadratic factor $x^2+1$ does not get neutralised, and there is nothing left to force a linear factor.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — every self-adjoint operator has a cube root)

Prove or give a counterexample: every self-adjoint operator on $V$ has a cube root. (An operator $S\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is a **cube root** of $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ if $S^3=T$.)

*(Axler §7.B, Exercise 11, p. 224)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Self-adjointness gives you an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. If you knew the eigenvalues of the cube root, could you simply *define* it on that basis?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The claim is true. Take an orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ of eigenvectors with $Te_j=\lambda_je_j$ — the Real Spectral Theorem over $\mathbb{R}$, or self-adjoint-hence-normal and the Complex Spectral Theorem over $\mathbb{C}$. Define $S$ by prescribing $Se_j=\mu_je_j$ for a suitable $\mu_j$, using la-05's Theorem 3.5, and check $S^3=T$ on the basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Each $\lambda_j$ is real (Theorem 7.13). Every real number has a real cube root, so set $\mu_j=\lambda_j^{1/3}$ and define $S\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ by $Se_j=\mu_je_j$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Then $S^3e_j=\mu_j^3e_j=\lambda_je_j=Te_j$ for every $j$, so $S^3$ and $T$ agree on a basis and are therefore equal (la-05, Theorem 3.5). Hence $S$ is a cube root of $T$.

Three points earn the marks. Say why $\lambda_j$ being **real** matters and where: every real number has a real cube root, including the negatives, which is exactly what makes the construction work — the same argument for *square* roots would fail on any negative eigenvalue. Say which spectral theorem you are using and why it applies in each field. And note the bonus: $S$ is itself self-adjoint, since it is diagonal with real entries in an orthonormal basis, so the cube root can be taken within the class.
</details>
