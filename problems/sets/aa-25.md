## Problem 1 (easy–medium — the dihedral relations)

(a) State Definition 11.32 (p. 242) and Proposition 11.35 (p. 245): $D_{2n} = \langle x,y\rangle$ with $x^2=e$, $y^n=e$, $xy = y^{-1}x$.

(b) Deduce that every element of $D_{2n}$ is $y^k$ or $xy^k$ with $0\le k<n$, and hence $|D_{2n}| = 2n$.

(c) Derive the rule $xy^k = y^{-k}x$ and use it to compute $(xy^3)(xy^5)$ in $D_{14}$ — Example 11.36 (p. 245).

(d) Identify $D_6$ and $D_8$ geometrically, per Example 11.33 (p. 242) and Example 11.34 (p. 243).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Use $xy = y^{-1}x$ to push every $x$ to the left of every $y$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Induct on $k$: $xy^{k+1} = (xy^k)y = y^{-k}(xy) = y^{-k}y^{-1}x$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $D_{2n}$ is the symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon; $x$ is the flip about the axis through the centre and vertex $1$, $y$ the rotation by $360/n$ degrees.

(b) Any word in $x,y$ and their inverses can be rewritten: $x^{-1}=x$ and $y^{-1}=y^{n-1}$ remove inverses, and $xy = y^{-1}x$ moves every $x$ leftwards past every $y$. Repeated application collapses the word to $x^\epsilon y^k$ with $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$ and $0\le k<n$. So there are at most $2n$ elements, and exactly $2n$ because the $n$ rotations and the $n$ flips are distinct symmetries of the $n$-gon.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) By induction: $xy^1 = y^{-1}x$ is the relation, and $xy^{k+1} = (xy^k)y = y^{-k}xy = y^{-k}y^{-1}x = y^{-(k+1)}x$. So $xy^k = y^{-k}x$, equivalently $y^kx = xy^{-k}$.

In $D_{14}$, where $n=7$:
$$(xy^3)(xy^5) = x(y^3x)y^5 = x(xy^{-3})y^5 = x^2y^2 = y^2 .$$
Two flips compose to a rotation, as they must — the product of two reflections is a rotation, and the arithmetic is doing the geometry.

(d) $D_6$ is the symmetry group of an equilateral triangle, of order $6$. $D_8$ is the symmetry group of a square, of order $8$. Aluffi's subscript is the **order** of the group, not the number of sides; Carter and many other texts use the other convention, and this module follows Aluffi throughout.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — cyclic groups, once more)

(a) Using Carter §5.1 (pp. 64–67) as the picture and aa-21 as the algebra, prove that every cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to $C_n = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n\ge1$.

(b) Prove that a group of prime order is cyclic, and that it has no subgroups but the two trivial ones.

(c) Prove that every subgroup and every quotient of a cyclic group is cyclic.

(d) Give a non-cyclic group all of whose proper subgroups are cyclic.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $\mathbb{Z}\to G$, $k\mapsto g^k$, is a surjective homomorphism when $g$ generates.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Use that the order of an element divides the order of the group — aa-24 Problem 5(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $G = \langle g\rangle$. The map $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}\to G$, $k\mapsto g^k$, is a homomorphism, surjective by definition of generation. Its kernel is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$, hence $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n\ge0$ (aa-09 Problem 6(a)). So $G\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, which is $\mathbb{Z}$ when $n=0$ and $C_n$ otherwise.

(b) If $|G| = p$ is prime, take $g\ne e$; then $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $p$ and exceeds $1$, so it is $p$ and $G = \langle g\rangle$. Any subgroup has order dividing $p$, so order $1$ or $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Both are aa-21 Problem 3, restated: subgroups of $C_n$ are the $\langle g^{n/m}\rangle$ for $m\mid n$, and an image of $\langle g\rangle$ is generated by the image of $g$.

(d) $D_6\cong S_3$. Its proper subgroups are the trivial one, three of order $2$ and one of order $3$ (aa-23 Problem 5(a)) — all cyclic, being of prime order or trivial. And $D_6$ itself is not cyclic: it is not abelian, and a cyclic group is abelian. So "all proper subgroups cyclic" is strictly weaker than "cyclic", and the gap is exactly where non-abelian groups start.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — permutations as products of cycles)

(a) State Definition 11.41 (p. 249) and Remark 11.43 (p. 250), and prove that disjoint cycles commute.

(b) Prove Proposition 11.44 (p. 250): every permutation other than the identity is a product of disjoint nontrivial cycles.

(c) Write $\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6\\3&5&1&6&2&4\end{pmatrix}$ in disjoint-cycle form, and compute its order.

(d) Prove that the order of a permutation is the least common multiple of its cycle lengths.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) The cycles are the orbits of $\langle\sigma\rangle$ acting on $\{1,\dots,n\}$ — aa-24 Problem 2.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Disjoint cycles commute, so a power of the product is the product of the powers; each vanishes exactly when its own length divides the exponent.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If two cycles have disjoint nontrivial orbits, then each fixes everything the other moves, so for any point at most one of them acts, and the two orders of composition agree there.

(b) Let $\sigma\in S_n$ and let $\langle\sigma\rangle$ act on $\{1,\dots,n\}$. The orbits partition the set (aa-24 Problem 2(b)). On each orbit $\{a,\sigma a,\dots,\sigma^{r-1}a\}$ of size $r$, $\sigma$ acts as the $r$-cycle $(a\ \sigma a\ \dots\ \sigma^{r-1}a)$. The product of these cycles over all orbits of size $>1$ agrees with $\sigma$ everywhere, and the cycles are disjoint because the orbits are.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Trace: $1\mapsto3\mapsto1$, so $(1\ 3)$. Then $2\mapsto5\mapsto2$, so $(2\ 5)$. Then $4\mapsto6\mapsto4$, so $(4\ 6)$. Hence
$$\sigma = (1\ 3)(2\ 5)(4\ 6),$$
a product of three disjoint transpositions. Its order is $\operatorname{lcm}(2,2,2) = 2$; indeed $\sigma^2 = e$.

(d) Write $\sigma = \tau_1\cdots\tau_k$ with the $\tau_i$ disjoint of lengths $r_i$. Since they commute, $\sigma^m = \tau_1^m\cdots\tau_k^m$, and because the $\tau_i$ move disjoint sets, $\sigma^m = e$ forces each $\tau_i^m = e$ separately. An $r$-cycle has order $r$, so this says $r_i\mid m$ for every $i$, i.e. $\operatorname{lcm}(r_i)\mid m$. Conversely that lcm works. So $|\sigma| = \operatorname{lcm}(r_1,\dots,r_k)$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — sign, and the alternating group)

(a) Prove Lemma 11.37 (p. 248): a permutation matrix has determinant $\pm1$; hence Definition 11.39 (p. 248) makes sense.

(b) Prove that $\sigma\mapsto(-1)^\sigma$ is a group homomorphism $S_n\to\{\pm1\}$.

(c) Prove Lemma 11.45 (p. 251) and Lemma 11.46 (p. 251): an $r$-cycle is a product of $r-1$ transpositions, and is even exactly when $r$ is odd.

(d) Prove Proposition 11.47 (p. 251): $S_n$ is generated by transpositions. Deduce that $\mathcal{A}_n$ (Definition 11.40, p. 249) has index $2$ in $S_n$ for $n\ge2$, so $|\mathcal{A}_n| = n!/2$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) A permutation matrix has one $1$ in each row and column, so its determinant is a single term $\pm1$ of the Leibniz expansion.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The sign homomorphism is onto $\{\pm1\}$ for $n\ge2$ because a transposition is odd, and $\mathcal{A}_n$ is its kernel.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The matrix of $\sigma$ has entries $\delta_{i,\sigma(j)}$. In the Leibniz expansion exactly one permutation contributes a nonzero product — namely $\sigma$ itself — and that product is $1$, so the determinant is the sign of $\sigma$ in the expansion, which is $\pm1$.

(b) The permutation matrix of a composite is the product of the matrices, and $\det$ is multiplicative, so $(-1)^{\sigma\tau} = (-1)^\sigma(-1)^\tau$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Lemma 11.45 is the identity $(a_1\dots a_r) = (a_1a_r)\cdots(a_1a_3)(a_1a_2)$, verified by tracking each $a_i$: applying the right side, $a_i$ is moved only by the factor $(a_1a_{i+1})$ and by $(a_1a_i)$, and the composite sends $a_i\mapsto a_{i+1}$; and $a_r\mapsto a_1$. That is $r-1$ transpositions, so by (b) an $r$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{r-1}$: even when $r$ is odd, odd when $r$ is even.

(d) Every non-identity permutation is a product of disjoint cycles (Problem 3(b)), and every cycle is a product of transpositions by (c); the identity is the empty product. So transpositions generate. For the index: the sign map is a surjective homomorphism $S_n\to\{\pm1\}$ for $n\ge2$, since a transposition has sign $-1$, and $\mathcal{A}_n$ is its kernel. The two cosets are $\mathcal{A}_n$ and its complement, of equal size by aa-24 Problem 5(c)'s coset argument, so $|\mathcal{A}_n| = n!/2$.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — free groups)

Aluffi describes free groups on p. 252 without a formal construction: $F(A)$ is the group of reduced words in the letters of $A$ and their inverses, and its defining feature is a universal property.

(a) State that universal property: a homomorphism $F(A)\to G$ is the same thing as a function $A\to G$.

(b) Prove $F(\{a\})\cong\mathbb{Z}$.

(c) Prove Example 11.48 (p. 253): every group is a quotient of a free group, so every group has a presentation by generators and relations.

(d) Give a presentation of $D_{2n}$, and say which two facts about $D_{2n}$ from Problem 1 are the two halves of showing the presentation is correct.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Compare aa-18 Problem 2 for free modules; this is the same sentence with "module" replaced by "group".
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Take $A = G$ as a set and use (a) on the identity function.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For every group $G$ and every function $f : A\to G$ there is exactly one group homomorphism $\tilde f : F(A)\to G$ with $\tilde f(a) = f(a)$ for $a\in A$. Nothing has to be checked on the $f$ side — no relations to respect — which is what "free" means, exactly as in aa-18 Problem 2(b).

(b) Reduced words in one letter $a$ and $a^{-1}$ are $a^k$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$, with $a^ja^k = a^{j+k}$. The map $a^k\mapsto k$ is an isomorphism to $\mathbb{Z}$. Alternatively use (a): a homomorphism $F(\{a\})\to G$ is a choice of one element of $G$, and so is a homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to G$ (aa-21 Problem 6(a) with $n$ unbounded), and an object with the same maps out of it is the same object.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $A$ be any generating set of $G$ — for instance $A=G$ itself. By (a) the inclusion $A\hookrightarrow G$ extends to a homomorphism $\pi : F(A)\to G$, and its image is a subgroup containing $A$, hence all of $G$: $\pi$ is surjective. So $G$ is a quotient of $F(A)$, and once aa-26 supplies quotient groups this reads $G\cong F(A)/\ker\pi$. A presentation is a choice of $A$ together with a set $R$ of words whose **normal closure** is $\ker\pi$ — the relations. The word "normal" is load-bearing: $R$ has to generate $\ker\pi$ as a normal subgroup, not as an ordinary one. For instance the single relator $[a,b]$ presents $\mathbb{Z}^2$ from $F(\{a,b\})$, because its normal closure is the whole commutator subgroup — which is not cyclic, so $[a,b]$ certainly does not generate the kernel as a plain subgroup. Read the weaker way, plenty of correct finite presentations would look insufficient.

(d) $D_{2n} = \langle x,y \mid x^2, y^n, xyxy\rangle$, the last relation being $xy = y^{-1}x$ rewritten as $xyxy^{-1}\cdot y^{2}$… more cleanly, $\langle x,y\mid x^2=e,\ y^n=e,\ xyx^{-1}=y^{-1}\rangle$.

The two halves of correctness are exactly Problem 1(a) and Problem 1(b). *That the relations hold* in the symmetry group is 1(a) — Proposition 11.35 — and gives a surjection from the presented group onto $D_{2n}$. *That the relations suffice* is 1(b): every word collapses to one of $2n$ normal forms, so the presented group has at most $2n$ elements. A surjection onto a group of order $2n$ from a group of order at most $2n$ is an isomorphism. Neither half alone would do, and the second is the one usually skipped.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — every group of order 6)

Let $|G| = 6$. Prove that $G\cong C_6$ or $G\cong D_6\cong S_3$, following these steps.

(a) Show every element has order $1, 2, 3$ or $6$, and that if some element has order $6$ then $G\cong C_6$.

(b) Show that not every non-identity element can have order $2$.

(c) So fix $y$ of order $3$ and put $H = \langle y\rangle$. Show $G = H\sqcup xH$ for any $x\notin H$, and that $x^2\in H$.

(d) Show that either $G$ is cyclic or $x$ may be chosen with $x^2 = e$; and in that case show $xyx^{-1}\in\{y,y^2\}$.

(e) Conclude, and check that $D_6\cong S_3$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) A group in which every element squares to the identity has order a power of $2$ — aa-23 Problem 6(b).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) If $xyx^{-1}=y$ then $x$ and $y$ commute and $xy$ has order $6$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The order of $g$ is $|\langle g\rangle|$, which divides $6$ by aa-24 Problem 5(c) — cosets of $\langle g\rangle$ partition $G$ into blocks of equal size. If $|g| = 6$ then $\langle g\rangle$ has six elements, so $G = \langle g\rangle\cong C_6$.

(b) If every non-identity element had order $2$ then $g^2 = e$ for all $g$, so by aa-23 Problem 6(b) $|G|$ would be a power of $2$. But $6$ is not. So some element has order $3$ or $6$; in the latter case (a) finishes, so assume some $y$ has order $3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $H = \{e,y,y^2\}$ has three elements, and its left cosets partition $G$ into blocks of three, so there are exactly two: $H$ and $xH$ for any $x\notin H$. Now $x^2$ lies in $H$ or in $xH$; if $x^2 = xh$ then $x = h\in H$, contrary to choice. So $x^2\in H$.

(d) If $x^2 = y$ or $x^2 = y^2$, then $x^2$ has order $3$, so $x$ has order $6$ — its order is a multiple of $3$ and not $3$ itself, since $x^3 = e$ with $x^2 = y$ would give $x = x^4 = y^2\in H$ — and (a) makes $G\cong C_6$. Otherwise $x^2 = e$.

With $x^2=e$: $xyx^{-1}$ has the same order as $y$, namely $3$, since conjugation is an automorphism (aa-24 Problem 6). To place it, use normality rather than a count. Part (c) already showed $H$ has exactly two cosets, so $H$ has index $2$ and is therefore **normal** — for $g\in H$ we get $gH = H = Hg$, and for $g\notin H$ both $gH$ and $Hg$ must be the one remaining block $G\setminus H$. Hence $xHx^{-1} = H$ and in particular $xyx^{-1}\in H$, so $xyx^{-1}\in\{y,y^2\}$ (it cannot be $e$, having order 3).

Resist the tempting shortcut "a second subgroup of order 3 would give more than six elements": two distinct subgroups of order 3 meet only at the identity, so together they hold $3+3-1 = 5$ elements, and nothing about that exceeds six. Uniqueness of $H$ is true here but needs its own argument; normality is the shorter road and is already paid for.

(e) If $xyx^{-1} = y$ then $x$ and $y$ commute, so $|xy| = \operatorname{lcm}(2,3) = 6$ and $G\cong C_6$. If $xyx^{-1} = y^2 = y^{-1}$, then $x,y$ satisfy exactly the relations $x^2=e$, $y^3=e$, $xyx^{-1}=y^{-1}$ of Problem 5(d) with $n=3$, and $G$ has six elements, so $G\cong D_6$ by the counting argument there.

Finally $D_6\cong S_3$: both act faithfully on the three vertices of the triangle, and $|S_3| = 6 = |D_6|$, so the injection $D_6\to S_3$ of Example 11.33 (p. 242) is onto. So there are exactly **two** groups of order $6$, one abelian and one not — and $6$ is the smallest order at which a non-abelian group exists, since orders $1,2,3,5$ are prime (Problem 2(b)) and order $4$ was settled in aa-23 Problem 6(c).
</details>
