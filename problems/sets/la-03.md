## Problem 1 (easy — computation, dependence by hand)

Find a number $t$ such that
$$(3,1,4),\quad (2,-3,5),\quad (5,9,t)$$
is **not** linearly independent in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then exhibit an explicit dependence relation for your value of $t$ — that is, produce scalars, not all zero, whose combination is $(0,0,0)$.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercise 3, p. 37)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
"Not linearly independent" means linearly dependent (Definition 2.19): some $a(3,1,4)+b(2,-3,5)+c(5,9,t)=(0,0,0)$ with $a,b,c$ not all zero. You are free to fix one of the three scalars — try $c=-1$, which turns the question into "is $(5,9,t)$ a linear combination of the other two?"
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
With $c=-1$ you need $a(3,1,4)+b(2,-3,5)=(5,9,t)$. The first two coordinates give two equations in the two unknowns $a,b$ and determine them completely; the third coordinate is then not a further constraint on $a,b$ but a formula for $t$. This is the same "the leftover equation is where the answer lives" move as the lesson's first worked example, run backwards.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$3a+2b=5$ and $a-3b=9$. Substituting $a=9+3b$ into the first gives $27+11b=5$, so $b=-2$ and $a=3$. Now read $t$ off the third coordinate: $t=4a+5b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$t = 4(3)+5(-2) = 12-10 = 2$, and the relation is $3(3,1,4)-2(2,-3,5)-1(5,9,2)=(0,0,0)$ — verify all three coordinates. Note that $t=2$ is the *only* value that works: for any other $t$ the first two coordinates still force $a=3, b=-2$, and the third then fails. Say so, and say why, rather than presenting $t=2$ as one lucky find.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — two verdicts with no computation) [interleaves la-01]

Let $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{F})$ be the space of polynomials of degree at most 4.

(a) Explain why there does not exist a list of six polynomials that is linearly independent in $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{F})$.

(b) Explain why no list of four polynomials spans $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{F})$.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercises 12 and 13, p. 38)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Both parts are Theorem 2.23 applied to one and the same list: $1, z, z^2, z^3, z^4$. What are the two different things you know about that list?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
That list *spans* $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{F})$ — this is what Definition 2.13 of $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbb{F})$ amounts to — and it is also *linearly independent* (Example 2.18(d)). Theorem 2.23 then bounds independent lists above by 5 and spanning lists below by 5. Each part uses one of those two bounds; make sure you say which property of the list you are invoking each time.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $1,z,z^2,z^3,z^4$ spans, and has length 5, so by Theorem 2.23 every linearly independent list in $\mathcal{P}_4(\mathbb{F})$ has length at most 5. (b) $1,z,z^2,z^3,z^4$ is linearly independent, and has length 5, so by Theorem 2.23 every spanning list has length at least 5.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Both conclusions are immediate once the right half of Theorem 2.23 is selected: 6 > 5 kills (a), and 4 < 5 kills (b). The marks are in the justification, not the arithmetic — state explicitly which list you are comparing against, which property of it you are using (spanning for (a), independence for (b)), and in which direction Theorem 2.23 runs. A solution that says "dimension is 5" without proving it has assumed la-04.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, spanning is preserved)

Suppose $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$ spans $V$. Prove that the list
$$v_1-v_2,\quad v_2-v_3,\quad v_3-v_4,\quad v_4$$
also spans $V$.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercise 1, p. 37)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You do not have to reach an arbitrary element of $V$ directly. It is enough to reach each of $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$ — think about why, and name the result that licenses the step.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Let $W$ be the span of the new list. $W$ is a subspace (Theorem 2.7). If you show each $v_j\in W$, then $W$ is a subspace containing every $v_j$, so $W\supseteq\operatorname{span}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)=V$ — using the *smallest containing subspace* half of Theorem 2.7, which is exactly the half that is easy to forget. Build the $v_j$ from the bottom up.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$v_4$ is in the list. $v_3=(v_3-v_4)+v_4$. $v_2=(v_2-v_3)+(v_3-v_4)+v_4$. Continue the telescoping pattern one more step for $v_1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$v_1=(v_1-v_2)+(v_2-v_3)+(v_3-v_4)+v_4$. So all four of $v_1,\dots,v_4$ lie in $W$, whence $V=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_4)\subseteq W\subseteq V$ and $W=V$. Write the chain of inclusions out; the final line should make clear that the containment $W\subseteq V$ is free (every $v_j\in V$ and $V$ is closed under the operations) while $V\subseteq W$ is what you worked for.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, independence is preserved) [pairs with Problem 3]

Suppose $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$ is linearly independent in $V$. Prove that the list
$$v_1-v_2,\quad v_2-v_3,\quad v_3-v_4,\quad v_4$$
is also linearly independent.

After proving it, write one or two sentences comparing this argument with Problem 3's: the two problems concern the same substitution, but the work goes in opposite directions.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercise 6, p. 37)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Start from a supposed dependence relation $a(v_1-v_2)+b(v_2-v_3)+c(v_3-v_4)+dv_4=0$ and regroup it as a combination of $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Collecting coefficients gives $av_1+(b-a)v_2+(c-b)v_3+(d-c)v_4=0$. Independence of the original list forces every one of those four coefficients to vanish, and you then solve the resulting system for $a,b,c,d$ — in a particular order.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$a=0$; then $b-a=0$ gives $b=0$; then $c-b=0$ gives $c=0$; then $d-c=0$ gives $d=0$. Note the cascade: each equation is useless until the previous one has been solved.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So the only dependence relation is the trivial one, and the list is linearly independent by Definition 2.17. For the comparison: Problem 3 argues *forwards*, expressing each old vector in terms of the new list; Problem 4 argues *backwards*, starting from a hypothetical relation among the new vectors and pushing it onto the old list where the hypothesis applies. Spanning proofs chase membership, independence proofs chase coefficients — and the same telescoping algebra serves both.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — prove or give a counterexample)

Prove or give a counterexample: if $v_1,\dots,v_m$ and $w_1,\dots,w_m$ are both linearly independent lists of vectors in $V$, then
$$v_1+w_1,\quad \dots,\quad v_m+w_m$$
is linearly independent.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercise 9, p. 37)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Before attempting a proof, test the claim at the smallest possible size. What does the statement say when $m=1$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For $m=1$ it reads: if $v_1\ne0$ and $w_1\ne0$ (Example 2.18(a)), then $v_1+w_1\ne0$. Ask whether two nonzero vectors can sum to zero. If they can, the claim is false and you have your counterexample — and it will be much smaller than the general shape of the problem suggests.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
In $\mathbb{R}^2$ take $m=1$, $v_1=(1,0)$, $w_1=(-1,0)$. Each single-vector list is linearly independent since each vector is nonzero. Their sum is $(0,0)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The claim is **false**. With the vectors above, $v_1+w_1=(0,0)$, and any list containing the zero vector is linearly dependent (Example 2.20's last bullet: $1\cdot 0=0$ is a dependence relation with a nonzero coefficient). Marks require three things: a statement that the claim is false, a fully checked counterexample, and a verification that *both* hypothesis lists really are independent — a "counterexample" that quietly violates the hypotheses proves nothing. **Any correct counterexample, fully verified, earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof, a dependence forces membership)

Suppose $v_1,\dots,v_m$ is linearly independent in $V$ and $w\in V$. Prove that if
$$v_1+w,\quad \dots,\quad v_m+w$$
is linearly dependent, then $w\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m)$.

*(Axler §2.A, Exercise 10, p. 37)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Write down what dependence gives you — scalars $a_1,\dots,a_m$, not all zero, with $\sum a_i(v_i+w)=0$ — and expand. The coefficient that appears in front of $w$ is not one of the $a_i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Expanding gives $a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m+(a_1+\dots+a_m)w=0$. Everything turns on whether the scalar $s=a_1+\dots+a_m$ is zero, so split into those two cases. One case lets you solve for $w$; the other must be ruled out, and the hypothesis you have not yet used is exactly what rules it out.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $s=0$, the equation collapses to $a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m=0$ with the $a_i$ not all zero — which contradicts the linear independence of $v_1,\dots,v_m$. Hence $s\ne0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $s\ne0$ you may divide: $w=-\frac{1}{s}(a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m)=\left(-\frac{a_1}{s}\right)v_1+\dots+\left(-\frac{a_m}{s}\right)v_m$, which exhibits $w$ as a linear combination of $v_1,\dots,v_m$, i.e. $w\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ by Definition 2.5. Two things earn the marks here. First, be explicit that dividing by $s$ is legal *only* because the previous case was eliminated — this is the same discipline as the Linear Dependence Lemma's choice of an index with $a_j\ne0$. Second, notice where the hypothesis of independence is spent: entirely on the case $s=0$, and nowhere else in the proof.
</details>
