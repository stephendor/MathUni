## Problem 1 (easy — computing images and preimages)

Let $A = \{-4,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,4\}$, $B = \{0,1,4,6,9,12,16,25\}$, and let $f : A \to B$ be $f(x) = x^2$. Compute each of the following, as an explicit list of elements:

(a) $f(\{-2,-1,0\})$ (b) $f^{-1}(\{0,1,4\})$ (c) $f^{-1}(\{6,12,25\})$ (d) $f(A)$ and $f^{-1}(B)$.

Then answer in one sentence each: is $f$ injective? Is it surjective?

*(Cummings §8 chapter exercises, Exercise 8.26, p. 280)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$f^{-1}(Y)$ is not "undo $f$" — it is $\{x \in A : f(x) \in Y\}$, a set-building operation that is defined whether or not $f$ has an inverse. Nothing in (c) requires $f$ to be invertible.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Work element by element from the definition. For (c), check each of $6, 12, 25$ in turn: which $x \in A$ satisfy $x^2 = 6$? Note that $B$ was chosen so that some of its elements are not squares of anything in $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\{4,1,0\}$. (b) every $x \in A$ with $x^2 \in \{0,1,4\}$, so $\{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$. (c) no element of $A$ squares to $6$, to $12$, or to $25$ — write down what that makes the preimage.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $f^{-1}(\{6,12,25\}) = \varnothing$: the empty set is a perfectly good answer, and self-check 4 in the lesson is the same phenomenon. (d) $f(A) = \{0,1,4,9,16\}$, a strict subset of $B$ — so $f$ is not surjective; and $f(-1) = f(1)$ with $-1 \ne 1$, so it is not injective either. State both conclusions against Definition 8.3 and Definition 8.5 by name.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, preimages and union)

Let $f : A \to B$ and $E, F \subseteq B$. Prove that

$$f^{-1}(E \cup F) = f^{-1}(E) \cup f^{-1}(F).$$

Write it as a chain of biconditionals, and state at each step which definition licenses it.

*(Cummings §8 chapter exercises, Exercise 8.28(d), p. 280 — this is the faded example in the pw-03 lesson's Segment 2, written out in full)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The lesson proved part (i) of Lemma 3 this way, for $\cap$. Copy the shape of that argument and change one connective throughout.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Fix an arbitrary $x \in A$ and unpack "$x \in f^{-1}(E \cup F)$" one definition at a time until you arrive at "$x \in f^{-1}(E) \cup f^{-1}(F)$". Because every step is an "if and only if", you get the equality directly rather than having to prove two inclusions.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$x \in f^{-1}(E \cup F) \iff f(x) \in E \cup F \iff f(x) \in E$ or $f(x) \in F \iff \ldots$ — the first step is the definition of preimage, the second the definition of union.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$\ldots \iff x \in f^{-1}(E)$ or $x \in f^{-1}(F) \iff x \in f^{-1}(E) \cup f^{-1}(F)$ — preimage again, then union again. Since $x$ was arbitrary and every step is a biconditional, the two sets have exactly the same elements. Say explicitly in your write-up why this argument does **not** work for images: where would the existential quantifier in the definition of image break the chain?
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — counterexample) [interleaves pw-01]

Give an explicit function $f : A \to B$ and explicit subsets $C, D \subseteq A$ for which

$$f(C \cap D) \ne f(C) \cap f(D),$$

and verify both sides by computing them. Then say, in one sentence, which hypothesis of Lemma 4 your example violates and where.

Your example may not be the lesson's ($f(x) = x^2$ on $[-2,0]$ and $[0,2]$) or the lesson's worked variant on $\mathbb{Z}$.

*(Cummings §8 chapter exercises, Exercise 8.29, p. 280)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A counterexample is a construction, not a search: decide first what you need to go wrong, then build the smallest object that does it.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
From the lesson's analysis, the failure needs two *different* points $x_1 \in C$ and $x_2 \in D$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ and $x_1, x_2 \notin C \cap D$. A three-element domain is enough. Finite sets are easier to verify than intervals, and verification is half the marks.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Try $A = \{1,2,3\}$, $B = \{a,b\}$, $C = \{1\}$, $D = \{2\}$, and choose $f$ so that $1$ and $2$ collide. Then $C \cap D = \varnothing$, so the left side is forced to be $\varnothing$ — now make the right side non-empty.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
With $f(1) = f(2) = a$ and $f(3) = b$: $f(C \cap D) = f(\varnothing) = \varnothing$, while $f(C) \cap f(D) = \{a\} \cap \{a\} = \{a\} \ne \varnothing$. The violated hypothesis is injectivity — $f(1) = f(2)$ with $1 \ne 2$ — which is exactly what Lemma 4 assumes and exactly what the two mismatched witnesses in the lesson's hook were. Write the verification of both sides out; asserting them is not proving them.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — prove or refute, preimage then image)

For each conjecture, either prove it for every function $f : A \to B$, or give a counterexample.

(a) **Conjecture.** If $Y \subseteq B$, then $Y \subseteq f(f^{-1}(Y))$.
(b) **Conjecture.** If $Y \subseteq B$, then $f(f^{-1}(Y)) = Y$.

If a conjecture is false, state and prove the exact hypothesis on $f$ that repairs it.

*(Cummings §8 chapter exercises, Exercise 8.30(c)–(d), pp. 280–281; cf. Lemma 5 in the pw-03 lesson, which states the repaired form)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One of the two inclusions in (b) is unconditional and one is not. Work out which before you commit to proving or refuting anything — and note that (a) asks about the direction the lesson's proof sketch called the harder one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For the unconditional inclusion, chase an arbitrary element through the two definitions. For the other direction, ask what could stop $y \in Y$ from being $f$ of something: if nothing in $A$ maps to $y$ at all, then $y$ contributes nothing to $f^{-1}(Y)$ and so cannot reappear in $f(f^{-1}(Y))$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$f(f^{-1}(Y)) \subseteq Y$ always: an element of the left side is $f(x)$ for some $x$ with $f(x) \in Y$. For the reverse, take $f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $f(n) = 2n$, and $Y = \{4,5,6\}$ — self-check 5 in the lesson computes both sides.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$f^{-1}(\{4,5,6\}) = \{2,3\}$ and $f(\{2,3\}) = \{4,6\} \ne \{4,5,6\}$, so (a) and (b) are both false in general, and the same example refutes both. The repair is surjectivity: if $f$ is surjective then every $y \in Y$ has some $a$ with $f(a) = y$, that $a$ lies in $f^{-1}(Y)$, and so $y \in f(f^{-1}(Y))$ — giving $Y \subseteq f(f^{-1}(Y))$ and hence equality. Prove that direction properly; Lemma 5 in the lesson only sketches it.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — an iff, and an induction) [interleaves pw-02]

(a) Prove that $f : A \to B$ is injective **if and only if** $f(C \cap D) = f(C) \cap f(D)$ for all $C, D \subseteq A$.

(b) Prove by induction on $n$ that for any $f : A \to B$ and any subsets $C_1, \ldots, C_n \subseteq A$ with $n \ge 1$,

$$f(C_1 \cup C_2 \cup \cdots \cup C_n) = f(C_1) \cup f(C_2) \cup \cdots \cup f(C_n).$$

*(Cummings §8 chapter exercises, Exercise 8.28(e), p. 280 gives the forward direction of (a); the converse and part (b) are composed. Part (b)'s base case is Lemma 2 in the pw-03 lesson, and the induction scaffold is the four-part one from pw-02.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is two implications and only one of them is Lemma 4. For the other, you get to *choose* the sets $C$ and $D$ — a hypothesis quantified over **all** $C, D$ is a hypothesis you may instantiate at whichever pair is convenient.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a, ⇐) Assume the equality holds for all $C, D$, and suppose $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$. Take $C = \{a_1\}$ and $D = \{a_2\}$ — two singletons — and compute both sides of the equality. (b) Induct on $n$ with the four-part scaffold; the induction step needs the two-set case, which is Lemma 2, applied to $C_1 \cup \cdots \cup C_k$ and $C_{k+1}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a, ⇐) $f(C) \cap f(D) = \{f(a_1)\} \cap \{f(a_2)\} = \{f(a_1)\}$, which is non-empty. So $f(C \cap D)$ is non-empty too, which forces $C \cap D = \{a_1\} \cap \{a_2\}$ to be non-empty. (b) Base case $n = 1$ is the trivial identity $f(C_1) = f(C_1)$; you may instead take $n = 2$ as the base if you say so and cite Lemma 2 for it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a, ⇐) A non-empty $\{a_1\} \cap \{a_2\}$ means $a_1 = a_2$, which is Definition 8.3 — so $f$ is injective. Note the shape of this argument: the contrapositive-flavoured move from pw-01, run inside an iff. (b) Induction Step: $f\big((C_1 \cup \cdots \cup C_k) \cup C_{k+1}\big) = f(C_1 \cup \cdots \cup C_k) \cup f(C_{k+1})$ by Lemma 2, and the first term is $f(C_1) \cup \cdots \cup f(C_k)$ by the inductive hypothesis. State the hypothesis with its quantifier ("for every choice of $k$ subsets") — the sets, not just the number, are what the induction ranges over.
</details>
