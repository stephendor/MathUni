## Problem 1 (easy — what a local orientation is)

(a) State the definition of an orientation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ at a point $x$, and
explain why one wants a definition with the property that rotations preserve it
and reflections reverse it.
(b) Using the isomorphisms
$H_n(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^n - \{x\}) \cong H_{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^n - \{x\}) \cong H_{n-1}(S^{n-1})$,
prove that a rotation $\rho$ fixing $x$ satisfies $\rho_*(\alpha) = \alpha$ and a
reflection sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$. Say where naturality is used and where
degree is used.
(c) Explain why an orientation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ at $x$ determines one at every
other point $y$, naming the two maps involved.
(d) Write down the definition of a local orientation of an $n$-manifold $M$ at
$x$, and state the notational convention $H_n(X|A)$ together with the reason
Hatcher calls it local homology.

*(Hatcher §3.3, pp. 233–234)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Rotations of $S^{n-1}$ are homotopic to the identity; reflections have degree $-1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c) go through a ball $B$ containing both points and use
$H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|x) \cong H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|B) \cong H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|y)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
By excision $H_n(X|A)$ depends only on a neighbourhood of the closure of $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) An orientation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ at $x$ is a choice of generator of the
infinite cyclic group $H_n(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^n - \{x\})$. The
characteristic property one is trying to capture is the one shared by
"clockwise/anticlockwise" in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and "right/left handed" in
$\mathbb{R}^3$: preserved by rotations, reversed by reflections. Hatcher's
viewpoint is that anything with this property may be regarded as an orientation.
(b) The displayed isomorphisms are natural, so a self-map of $\mathbb{R}^n$
fixing $x$ acts compatibly on all three groups; on $H_{n-1}(S^{n-1})$ the action
is multiplication by the degree. A rotation is homotopic to the identity, hence
has degree $1$ and fixes $\alpha$; a reflection has degree $-1$ and sends
$\alpha$ to $-\alpha$.
(c) Via $H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|x) \cong H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|B) \cong H_n(\mathbb{R}^n|y)$
for any ball $B$ containing both $x$ and $y$; the maps are induced by inclusions
of pairs.
(d) A local orientation of $M$ at $x$ is a choice of generator
$\mu_x$ of the infinite cyclic group $H_n(M, M - \{x\}) = H_n(M|x)$. The
convention writes $H_n(X, X - A)$ as $H_n(X|A)$; by excision this depends only on
a neighbourhood of the closure of $A$, which is why it is reasonable to call it
the local homology of $X$ at $A$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — the orientation double cover)

(a) Describe the set $\widetilde{M}$ and the basis of open sets $U(\mu_B)$ that
topologises it, and say why the projection $\widetilde{M} \to M$ is two-to-one.
(b) Explain why $\widetilde{M}$ is orientable.
(c) State Proposition 3.25 and use it to decide orientability of: $S^n$ for
$n \geq 2$; the Klein bottle; $\mathbb{RP}^2$. For each, say which clause of the
proposition you used, and be careful about the one case where the proposition
gives no information.
(d) Give the intuitive reading of non-orientability in terms of loops, and say
what it corresponds to in $\widetilde{M}$.

*(Hatcher §3.3, pp. 234–235, Proposition 3.25)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\mathbb{RP}^2$ has $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$. Does $\mathbb{Z}_2$ have a subgroup
of index two?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The proposition gives a sufficient condition for orientability, not a necessary
one; when the condition fails you must decide by other means.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathbb{Z}_2$ has the trivial subgroup as a subgroup of index two, so the last
clause of Proposition 3.25 does not apply to $\mathbb{RP}^2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\widetilde{M} = \{\mu_x : x \in M,\ \mu_x$ a local orientation of $M$ at
$x\}$, with $\mu_x \mapsto x$ the projection; it is two-to-one because
$H_n(M|x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ has exactly two generators. Given an open ball
$B \subset \mathbb{R}^n \subset M$ of finite radius and a generator
$\mu_B \in H_n(M|B)$, the set $U(\mu_B)$ consists of those $\mu_x$ with $x \in B$
whose value is the image of $\mu_B$ under $H_n(M|B) \to H_n(M|x)$; these form a
basis making the projection a covering space.
(b) Each point $\mu_x \in \widetilde{M}$ carries a canonical local orientation
$\widetilde{\mu}_x$ corresponding to $\mu_x$ under
$H_n(\widetilde{M}|\mu_x) \cong H_n(U(\mu_B)|\mu_x) \cong H_n(B|x)$, and by
construction these satisfy the local consistency condition.
(c) Proposition 3.25: if $M$ is connected then $M$ is orientable iff
$\widetilde{M}$ has two components; in particular $M$ is orientable if it is
simply connected, or more generally if $\pi_1(M)$ has no subgroup of index two.
$S^n$ ($n \geq 2$) is simply connected, hence orientable. The Klein bottle has
the torus as a two-sheeted covering space and is nonorientable; equivalently its
orientation cover is connected. For $\mathbb{RP}^2$, $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$ does
have a subgroup of index two, so the sufficient condition does not apply and the
proposition decides nothing — and in fact $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is nonorientable.
(d) Non-orientability is the possibility of going round a closed loop and
returning with the opposite orientation. In the covering space this is a loop in
$M$ that lifts to a path in $\widetilde{M}$ joining two distinct points with the
same image, and the existence of such paths is equivalent to $\widetilde{M}$
being connected.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — $R$-orientations and which rings work)

(a) Define an $R$-orientation, being precise about what "generator" means for a
commutative ring $R$ with identity, and why it is the same as being a unit.
(b) Using $H_n(M|x;R) \cong H_n(M|x) \otimes R$, describe the subcovering spaces
$M_r \subset M_R$ and say when $M_r$ is a copy of $M$ and when it is a copy of
$\widetilde{M}$.
(c) Prove that an orientable manifold is $R$-orientable for every $R$, and that a
nonorientable manifold is $R$-orientable if and only if $R$ contains a unit of
order two, which happens exactly when $2 = 0$ in $R$.
(d) Deduce that every manifold is $\mathbb{Z}_2$-orientable, and decide
$R$-orientability of the Klein bottle for $R = \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}_2, \mathbb{Z}_3, \mathbb{Q}$.

*(Hatcher §3.3, p. 235)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A unit of order two is an element $u$ with $u^2 = 1$ and $u = -u$; the latter is
$2u = 0$, and multiplying by $u^{-1}$ gives $2 = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
An $R$-orientation is a section of $M_R \to M$ taking a generator value at each
point; the question is whether such a section exists, which is a question about
the components of $M_R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $r$ has order $2$ in $R$ then $r = -r$, so $M_r$ is a single copy of $M$
rather than a two-sheeted cover.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) An $R$-orientation assigns to each $x \in M$ a generator of
$H_n(M|x;R) \cong R$, subject to the local consistency condition, where a
generator of $R$ is an element $u$ with $Ru = R$. Since $R$ has an identity this
says exactly that $u$ is invertible.
(b) Each $r \in R$ gives $M_r \subset M_R$ consisting of the points
$\pm\mu_x \otimes r$ for $\mu_x$ a generator of $H_n(M|x)$. If $r$ has order $2$
then $r = -r$, the two signs coincide, and $M_r$ is a copy of $M$; otherwise
$M_r \cong \widetilde{M}$. $M_R$ is the union of these, disjoint apart from
$M_r = M_{-r}$.
(c) If $M$ is orientable, a $\mathbb{Z}$-orientation $x \mapsto \mu_x$ gives an
$R$-orientation $x \mapsto \mu_x \otimes 1$, since $1$ is a unit and local
consistency is preserved. If $M$ is nonorientable, $\widetilde{M}$ is connected,
so a section taking generator values can exist only in a component $M_u$ that is
a copy of $M$ — which by (b) requires the unit $u$ to have order two. Conversely
a unit $u$ of order two gives the section $x \mapsto \mu_x \otimes u$, well
defined because the sign ambiguity is invisible. Finally $2u = 0$ with $u$
invertible gives $2 = 0$, and conversely if $2 = 0$ then $u = 1$ has order two.
(d) $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_2$, so every manifold is $\mathbb{Z}_2$-orientable.
The Klein bottle is nonorientable, so it is $\mathbb{Z}_2$-orientable but not
$\mathbb{Z}$-, $\mathbb{Z}_3$- or $\mathbb{Q}$-orientable, none of those having
$2 = 0$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — reading Theorem 3.26 and Corollary 3.28)

Let $M$ be a closed connected $n$-manifold.

(a) State Theorem 3.26 in full and compute $H_n(M;R)$ for
$R = \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}_2, \mathbb{Z}_3, \mathbb{Q}$, in the orientable case
and in the nonorientable case.
(b) Define a fundamental class, and prove the converse direction stated after
the theorem: if a fundamental class $\mu \in H_n(M;R)$ exists then $M$ is
$R$-orientable and compact.
(c) State Corollary 3.28 and use it, with (a), to say what the torsion of
$H_{n-1}(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is in each case.
(d) Describe the explicit simplicial construction of a fundamental class for a
closed $n$-manifold with a $\Delta$-complex structure, and say precisely what
goes wrong with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients when $M$ is nonorientable, and why
nothing goes wrong with $\mathbb{Z}_2$ coefficients.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Theorem 3.26 p. 236, the fundamental class discussion p. 236,
the simplicial construction and Corollary 3.28, p. 238)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), the point about compactness is that a cycle representing $\mu$ has
compact image.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), the coefficients $k_i$ of $\sum_i k_i\sigma_i$ must all be $\pm 1$ for
the class to be a local generator, and the cycle condition then propagates a
sign choice across shared faces.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H_n(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ if $M$ is orientable and $0$ if not; and
$H_n(M;\mathbb{Z}_2) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ either way.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Theorem 3.26: for $M$ closed connected of dimension $n$, (a) if $M$ is
$R$-orientable then $H_n(M;R) \to H_n(M|x;R) \cong R$ is an isomorphism for all
$x$; (b) if not, that map is injective with image $\{r \in R : 2r = 0\}$; (c)
$H_i(M;R) = 0$ for $i > n$. Orientable: $H_n = \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}_2,
\mathbb{Z}_3, \mathbb{Q}$ respectively. Nonorientable: the image is the
$2$-torsion of $R$, giving $0, \mathbb{Z}_2, 0, 0$ respectively.
(b) A fundamental class is an element of $H_n(M;R)$ whose image in $H_n(M|x;R)$
is a generator for every $x$. Given such a $\mu$, put $\mu_x$ for its image; the
map $H_n(M;R) \to H_n(M|x;R)$ factors through $H_n(M|B;R)$ for any open ball $B$
containing $x$, which is exactly the local consistency condition, so
$x \mapsto \mu_x$ is an $R$-orientation. And $M$ is compact because $\mu_x$ can
be non-zero only for $x$ in the image of a cycle representing $\mu$, and that
image is compact.
(c) Corollary 3.28: the torsion subgroup of $H_{n-1}(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is trivial if
$M$ is orientable and $\mathbb{Z}_2$ if $M$ is nonorientable.
(d) A simplicial fundamental class is $\sum_i k_i \sigma_i$ over the
$n$-simplices. Mapping to a generator of $H_n(M|x;\mathbb{Z})$ for $x$ interior
to $\sigma_i$ forces $k_i = \pm 1$, and the cycle condition forces the sign of
$k_j$ once $k_i$ is chosen, whenever $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$ share an
$(n-1)$-face. A globally consistent choice of signs exists precisely when $M$ is
orientable; on a nonorientable $M$ the propagation returns to some simplex
demanding both signs. With $\mathbb{Z}_2$ coefficients there is no sign to
propagate, and $\sum_i \sigma_i$ is always a fundamental class.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the Mayer–Vietoris step of Lemma 3.27)

(a) State Lemma 3.27 in full.
(b) Prove step (1) of its proof: if the lemma holds for compact $A$, $B$ and
$A \cap B$, then it holds for $A \cup B$. Write out the Mayer–Vietoris sequence
used, justify the zero on the left, and prove both the existence and the
uniqueness halves of part (a) of the lemma for $A \cup B$.
(c) Deduce Theorem 3.26 from Lemma 3.27, defining the $R$-module $\Gamma_R(M)$
of sections and the homomorphism $H_n(M;R) \to \Gamma_R(M)$, and saying exactly
which part of the lemma makes it an isomorphism.
(d) Proposition 3.29 states that a connected noncompact $n$-manifold has
$H_i(M;R) = 0$ for $i \geq n$. Explain why this is consistent with, and not
implied by, Theorem 3.26(c), and say what it means for the existence of a
fundamental class on a noncompact manifold.

*(Hatcher §3.3, Lemma 3.27 and steps (1)–(4) of its proof, pp. 236–238;
Proposition 3.29, p. 239)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The zero on the left of the Mayer–Vietoris sequence is $H_{n+1}(M|A \cap B)$,
which vanishes by the hypothesis applied to $A \cap B$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The uniqueness half reduces to showing that a class in $H_n(M|A\cup B)$ with
zero image in every $H_n(M|x)$ is itself zero; apply the hypothesis to $A$ and
to $B$ and use injectivity of $\Phi$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\Phi(\alpha) = (\alpha, -\alpha)$ and $\Psi(\alpha,\beta) = \alpha + \beta$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Lemma 3.27: for $M$ an $n$-manifold and $A \subset M$ compact, (a) every
section $x \mapsto \alpha_x$ of $M_R \to M$ has a unique class
$\alpha_A \in H_n(M|A;R)$ with image $\alpha_x$ in $H_n(M|x;R)$ for all
$x \in A$; (b) $H_i(M|A;R) = 0$ for $i > n$.
(b) Consider
$0 \to H_n(M|A\cup B) \xrightarrow{\Phi} H_n(M|A) \oplus H_n(M|B) \xrightarrow{\Psi} H_n(M|A \cap B)$
with $\Phi(\alpha) = (\alpha,-\alpha)$, $\Psi(\alpha,\beta) = \alpha+\beta$. The
zero on the left is $H_{n+1}(M|A\cap B)$, zero by hypothesis. Terms
$H_i(M|A\cup B)$ further left sit between groups that vanish by hypothesis, so
$H_i(M|A\cup B) = 0$ for $i > n$, proving (b). For existence in (a): the
hypothesis gives unique $\alpha_A, \alpha_B, \alpha_{A\cap B}$; the images of
$\alpha_A$ and $\alpha_B$ in $H_n(M|A\cap B)$ both satisfy the defining property
of $\alpha_{A\cap B}$, hence equal it, so $(\alpha_A,-\alpha_B) \in \ker\Psi = \operatorname{im}\Phi$
and $(\alpha_A, -\alpha_B) = \Phi(\alpha_{A\cup B})$ for some class
$\alpha_{A\cup B}$, which then has image $\alpha_x$ for every $x \in A \cup B$.
For uniqueness: if $\alpha \in H_n(M|A\cup B)$ has zero image in every
$H_n(M|x)$, then so do its images in $H_n(M|A)$ and $H_n(M|B)$, which are
therefore zero by hypothesis; injectivity of $\Phi$ gives $\alpha = 0$. Applying
this to the difference of two candidates gives uniqueness.
(c) Take $A = M$, compact by assumption. Part (b) of the lemma gives
Theorem 3.26(c) immediately. Let $\Gamma_R(M)$ be the set of sections of
$M_R \to M$; it is an $R$-module under pointwise sum and scalar multiplication.
The map $H_n(M;R) \to \Gamma_R(M)$ sending $\alpha$ to $x \mapsto \alpha_x$ is a
homomorphism, and part (a) of the lemma — existence and uniqueness of $\alpha_A$
for $A = M$ — says exactly that it is an isomorphism. For connected $M$ a
section is determined by its value at one point, so parts (a) and (b) of the
theorem follow from the description of the structure of $M_R$ in Problem 3(b).
(d) Theorem 3.26(c) concerns closed, hence compact, manifolds and gives
vanishing only above $n$; Proposition 3.29 concerns noncompact manifolds and
gives vanishing from $n$ upwards, one degree lower. Neither implies the other:
they have disjoint hypotheses. The consequence is that a noncompact connected
manifold has $H_n(M;R) = 0$ and so admits no fundamental class at all — matching
the compactness half of Problem 4(b), which showed a fundamental class forces
compactness.
</details>
