## Problem 1 (easy — direct proof)

Prove: if $n$ is odd, then $n^2 + n$ is even.

*(Cummings, chapter-2 Exercises, Exercise 2.5(a), p. 69 — where he sets it for
**every** integer $n$, not only odd $n$; prove the stated form first, then say
what changes if you drop the hypothesis. Not Exercise 2.1, which is the
five-part parity list on p. 68.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
What does Definition 2.2 let you write $n$ as, if $n$ is odd?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Write $n = 2a+1$ for an integer $a$, substitute into $n^2 + n$, and expand. You are aiming to show the result is $2k$ for some integer $k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$n^2 + n = (2a+1)^2 + (2a+1) = 4a^2 + 4a + 1 + 2a + 1 = 4a^2 + 6a + 2$. Now factor out a 2.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$n^2+n = 2(2a^2+3a+1)$. Since $2a^2+3a+1$ is an integer, set $k = 2a^2+3a+1$; then $n^2+n = 2k$, so by Definition 2.2, $n^2+n$ is even. Write this up with the full "Assume… Then… Therefore…" scaffold.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — direct proof, cases)

Determine conditions on integers $m$ and $n$ for which $mn$ is even. State your conjecture, then prove it.

*(Cummings, chapter-2 Exercises, Exercise 2.6, p. 69 — stated verbatim; the
conjecture-then-prove framing is his own wording.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Try small examples: $2\times 3$, $3\times 5$, $4\times 7$. When does the product come out even?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Your conjecture should be an "or" statement about $m$ and $n$. Proof by cases (still a direct proof, per the pw-01 lesson's self-check 2) handles an "or" hypothesis cleanly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Conjecture: $mn$ is even if and only if $m$ is even or $n$ is even. For the "if" direction, split into two cases: $m$ even, or $m$ odd and $n$ even.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Case 1: $m = 2a$. Then $mn = 2(an)$, even. Case 2: $m$ odd and $n = 2b$. Then $mn = 2(mb)$, even. Both cases give $mn$ even, so the "if" direction holds. The converse (only if) needs its own argument — consider the contrapositive: if both $m,n$ are odd, show $mn$ is odd.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — contrapositive)

Prove: if $3n + 5$ is even, then $n$ is odd.

*(Cummings §6.2, Proposition 6.3, p. 202 — the exact proposition the pw-01 lesson's self-check 4 references)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Try a direct proof first and notice it stalls — "3n+5 even" doesn't hand you an easy statement about $n$ alone.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use the contrapositive (Theorem 5.14): assume $n$ is not odd (i.e. $n$ is even) and derive that $3n+5$ is not even (i.e. odd).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Assume $n$ is even, so $n = 2a$. Then $3n + 5 = 6a + 5 = 2(3a+2) + 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $3a+2$ is an integer, $3n+5 = 2(3a+2)+1$ is odd by Definition 2.2 — i.e. not even. We have shown $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$; therefore, by the contrapositive, $P \Rightarrow Q$: if $3n+5$ is even, $n$ is odd. Do not omit the closing sentence invoking the contrapositive explicitly — Cummings flags this as mandatory (§6.2, footnote 6).
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — contrapositive, interleaves the direct-proof idiom) [interleaves pw-01]

Prove: if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd, for $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Then use this result directly (without re-proving it) to prove: if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even is *not* a proof of the converse — briefly explain in one or two sentences why proving $P\Rightarrow Q$ never automatically proves $Q\Rightarrow P$.

*(Cummings §6.2, Proposition 6.2, pp. 201–202, extended with a converse-fallacy check; the second part composed)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The first part is the contrapositive twin of Problem 1's direct-proof case — notice both problems attack "even/odd of $n^2$" but from opposite directions.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For the main proof, assume $n$ is not odd (even), write $n = 2a$, and compute $n^2$. For the converse-fallacy discussion, recall that $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ are logically independent statements — a truth table or a concrete counterexample settles it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$n^2 = (2a)^2 = 4a^2 = 2(2a^2)$, so $n^2$ is even, i.e. not odd. This gives $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By the contrapositive, $n^2$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $n$ odd. For the second part: proving $P \Rightarrow Q$ says nothing about $Q \Rightarrow P$ in general — e.g. "$n=4 \Rightarrow n$ is even" is true but "$n$ is even $\Rightarrow n = 4$" is false. Name this explicitly as the *converse fallacy*, distinct from the contrapositive (which *is* logically equivalent to the original implication).
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — contradiction, proof problem)

Prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

*(Cummings §7.4, Theorem 7.6, p. 225 — §7.3 is Euclid's infinitude of primes,
Theorem 7.5, pp. 220–221.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
This is a "there is no…" style claim in disguise ("there is no rational number equal to $\sqrt2$") — which technique does the pw-01 heuristic table recommend for such claims?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Assume for contradiction that $\sqrt2 = p/q$ in lowest terms. Square both sides, deduce $2\mid p^2$, hence $2\mid p$, substitute back, and chase the same conclusion for $q$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
From $2q^2 = p^2$, $2 \mid p^2$, so $2 \mid p$ by Lemma 2.17 part (iii), which is the result Cummings invokes
here (p. 225); write $p = 2k$. Substituting: $2q^2 = 4k^2$, so $q^2 = 2k^2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Now $2 \mid q^2$, so by the same lemma $2 \mid q$. But then both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting the assumption that $p/q$ was in lowest terms. Hence no such $p,q$ exist, and $\sqrt2$ is irrational. Write the full four-part structure: assumption, derivation, the exact contradiction named, and the concluding sentence. **Any logically valid alternative proof earns full marks** (e.g. a direct argument via unique factorisation / the rational root theorem is acceptable if fully justified).
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — contradiction, composed)

Prove: there is no smallest positive rational number.

*(Cummings §7.1, Proposition 7.2, p. 216 — stated there and proved by
contradiction. It was previously marked "composed" here; it is not. Cummings
also sets a second proof of it as practice on p. 217, ending at a numeric
falsehood rather than at minimality — do that one too, and previews the
well-ordering failure for ℚ used in pw-02.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The claim again has the "there is no…" shape — contradiction is the natural key.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Assume such a smallest positive rational $q$ exists. Construct an explicit positive rational strictly smaller than $q$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Consider $q/2$. Since $q > 0$, $q/2 > 0$ also, and $q/2$ is rational (a ratio of integers, since $q$ is).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Then $q/2 < q$ (since $q > 0$), so $q/2$ is a positive rational strictly smaller than $q$ — contradicting that $q$ was the smallest positive rational. Therefore no smallest positive rational exists. State explicitly which assumption was contradicted.
</details>
