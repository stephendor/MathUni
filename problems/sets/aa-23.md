## Problem 1 (easy–medium — the axioms, minus the ones that are gone)

(a) State Definition 11.1 (p. 229) and list, clause by clause, what a group has that an abelian group (Definition 8.11, p. 157) does not, and what it lacks that a ring (Definition 3.1, p. 41) has.

(b) Prove that the identity of a group is unique, and that inverses are unique.

(c) Prove $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$, and give a group where $(gh)^{-1}\ne g^{-1}h^{-1}$.

(d) Verify that $S_3$, the bijections of a three-element set, is a group, and that it is not abelian — Example 11.16 (p. 234).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) is aa-05 Problem 2 with one operation instead of two.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Multiply $gh$ by $h^{-1}g^{-1}$ and watch the middle cancel.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A group has one associative operation with an identity and inverses. Against an abelian group it drops commutativity and nothing else — so every abelian group is a group, written multiplicatively. Against a ring it drops the entire additive structure and the distributive laws, keeping only what a ring's *multiplication* would have if every nonzero element were invertible. That is the sense in which $(R,+)$ is a group for every ring and $(R,\cdot)$ usually is not.

(b) Two identities: $e = ee' = e'$. Two inverses of $g$: $h = he = h(gh') = (hg)h' = eh' = h'$. Both are aa-05 Problem 2 verbatim.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $(gh)(h^{-1}g^{-1}) = g(hh^{-1})g^{-1} = gg^{-1} = e$, and the same on the other side. By uniqueness of inverses, $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$. In $S_3$, take $g = (1\,2)$ and $h = (1\,3)$: then $gh$ and $hg$ are the two different $3$-cycles, so $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} = hg \ne gh = g^{-1}h^{-1}$. The order reversal is not a convention; it is forced.

(d) Composition of bijections is a bijection and is associative; the identity map is an identity; and a bijection has an inverse function, which is again a bijection. So $S_3$ is a group, of order $6$. It is not abelian by (c). Aluffi's Example 11.16 makes the point that the commutative examples of the previous chapters are the unrepresentative ones.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — one condition instead of three)

(a) Prove Proposition 11.7 (p. 232): a nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if $gh^{-1}\in H$ for all $g,h\in H$.

(b) Show the "nonempty" cannot be dropped.

(c) Compare with the subgroup criteria you already have: Proposition 10.4 (p. 204) for abelian groups, aa-16 Problem 1(a) for modules, and Proposition 5.9 (p. 81) for ideals. What is doing the work of "closed under negation" in each?

(d) Decide whether each is a subgroup of $S_3$: the three-cycles together with the identity; the transpositions; the transpositions together with the identity.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Take $g=h$ to get $e$, then $g=e$ to get inverses, then combine.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Check closure under composition, which Proposition 11.7 reduces to one condition.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) A subgroup is closed under products and inverses, so $gh^{-1}\in H$. ($\Leftarrow$) $H\ne\varnothing$, so pick $h\in H$; then $e = hh^{-1}\in H$. Then $h^{-1} = eh^{-1}\in H$ for every $h\in H$. Then for $g,h\in H$, $gh = g(h^{-1})^{-1}\in H$. Associativity is inherited. So $H$ is a subgroup.

(b) The empty set satisfies the condition vacuously and is not a subgroup — it has no identity. This is the same gap Proposition 5.9 (p. 81) and Proposition 8.23 (p. 161) close with the same word.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) In the abelian and module criteria, closure under negation is *listed*: for ideals it is derived from absorption by $-1_R$, for modules from the action of $-1_R$, and for abelian groups it must be assumed, since there is no ring to supply a $-1$ — which is aa-21 Problem 1(d)'s positive integers. Here, the single condition $gh^{-1}\in H$ builds inversion into the statement, so nothing has to be assumed separately. Four criteria, and the differences between them are entirely about where the inverse comes from.

(d) *Three-cycles with the identity* — this is $A_3 = \{e,(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2)\}$, a subgroup: it is closed, and each three-cycle inverts to the other.

*The transpositions alone* — not a subgroup: no identity, and $(1\,2)(1\,2)^{-1} = e\notin H$.

*The transpositions with the identity* — not a subgroup: $(1\,2)(1\,3)^{-1} = (1\,2)(1\,3)$ is a three-cycle, outside the set. Closure fails, and this is the first place a subset can contain the identity and every inverse and still not be a subgroup.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — subgroups from homomorphisms)

Let $\varphi : G\to G'$ be a group homomorphism (Definition 11.2, p. 230).

(a) Prove $\varphi(e_G) = e_{G'}$ and $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$.

(b) Prove Example 11.9 (p. 232): $\varphi(H)$ is a subgroup of $G'$ for every subgroup $H\subseteq G$.

(c) Prove Example 11.10 (p. 232): $\varphi^{-1}(H')$ is a subgroup of $G$ for every subgroup $H'\subseteq G'$; in particular $\ker\varphi = \varphi^{-1}(\{e\})$ is one.

(d) Prove Example 11.8 (p. 232): any intersection of subgroups is a subgroup — and show a union of two need not be, using $S_3$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $\varphi(e) = \varphi(ee) = \varphi(e)\varphi(e)$; now cancel.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use Proposition 11.7 throughout: each part is one line once the criterion is available.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\varphi(e)\varphi(e) = \varphi(ee) = \varphi(e)$, and multiplying by $\varphi(e)^{-1}$ gives $\varphi(e)=e'$. Then $\varphi(g)\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(gg^{-1}) = e'$, so $\varphi(g^{-1})$ is the inverse of $\varphi(g)$. Note this is *derived*, unlike the ring case where $f(1_R)=1_S$ had to be an axiom (aa-08 Problem 2(c)) — because in a group everything is invertible, so the cancellation is available.

(b) $\varphi(H)$ is nonempty, and for $\varphi(h),\varphi(h')$ in it, $\varphi(h)\varphi(h')^{-1} = \varphi(hh'^{-1})\in\varphi(H)$. Proposition 11.7 applies.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\varphi^{-1}(H')$ contains $e$ by (a). If $\varphi(g),\varphi(h)\in H'$ then $\varphi(gh^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(h)^{-1}\in H'$, so $gh^{-1}\in\varphi^{-1}(H')$. Taking $H' = \{e'\}$, which is a subgroup, gives $\ker\varphi$.

(d) An intersection $\bigcap_\alpha H_\alpha$ contains $e$ and, for $g,h$ in all of them, contains $gh^{-1}$, since each $H_\alpha$ does. For the union: in $S_3$, $H_1 = \{e,(1\,2)\}$ and $H_2 = \{e,(1\,3)\}$ are subgroups, and their union is not — it is the third case of Problem 2(d) minus one element, and $(1\,2)(1\,3)$ is a three-cycle outside it. That is the same obstruction as aa-12 Problem 3(c) and aa-16 Problem 2(c): a union closes only when one side contains the other.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — isomorphism, and a set that is not a group)

(a) Prove that Definition 11.5 (p. 231) — $\varphi$ is an isomorphism when a homomorphism $\psi$ exists with $\psi\varphi = \operatorname{id}$ and $\varphi\psi = \operatorname{id}$ — is equivalent to "$\varphi$ is a bijective homomorphism".

(b) Prove Proposition 11.3 (p. 230): composites of homomorphisms are homomorphisms, and identities are.

(c) Remark 11.4 (p. 231) observes that $\operatorname{Hom}(G,H)$ is generally **not** a group, unlike $\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)$ for abelian groups. Show the pointwise product of two homomorphisms need not be one.

(d) Show it *is* one when $H$ is abelian, and identify the step of your computation in (c) that the hypothesis repairs.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) Compute $(\varphi\psi)(g_1g_2)$ and compare with $(\varphi\psi)(g_1)(\varphi\psi)(g_2)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The two expressions differ by swapping a middle pair.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) Such a $\psi$ is a two-sided inverse function, so $\varphi$ is a bijection. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\varphi$ is a bijective homomorphism, its inverse function $\psi$ satisfies $\psi(h_1h_2) = \psi(\varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)) = \psi(\varphi(g_1g_2)) = g_1g_2 = \psi(h_1)\psi(h_2)$. This is aa-08 Problem 4(a) again, with one clause fewer.

(b) $(\chi\varphi)(g_1g_2) = \chi(\varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)) = \chi\varphi(g_1)\cdot\chi\varphi(g_2)$, and $\operatorname{id}$ is visibly a homomorphism.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Define $(\varphi\psi)(g) := \varphi(g)\psi(g)$. Then
$$(\varphi\psi)(g_1g_2) = \varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)\psi(g_1)\psi(g_2),$$
while
$$(\varphi\psi)(g_1)\cdot(\varphi\psi)(g_2) = \varphi(g_1)\psi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)\psi(g_2).$$
These differ by exchanging the middle pair $\varphi(g_2)\psi(g_1)$ for $\psi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)$. Concretely take $G=H=S_3$ with $\varphi = \operatorname{id}$ and $\psi = \operatorname{id}$: then $(\varphi\psi)(g) = g^2$, and $g\mapsto g^2$ is not a homomorphism on $S_3$, since $((1\,2)(1\,3))^2$ is a three-cycle's square, namely the other three-cycle, while $(1\,2)^2(1\,3)^2 = e$.

(d) If $H$ is abelian the middle pair commutes and the two expressions agree, so $\varphi\psi$ is a homomorphism and $\operatorname{Hom}(G,H)$ becomes an abelian group. The step repaired is the swap and nothing else — which is why aa-21 Problem 2(a) could build the ring $\operatorname{End}(M)$ for abelian $M$ and there is no analogue for general groups.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — finding all the subgroups)

(a) Find every subgroup of $S_3$ and check that there are six.

(b) Find every subgroup of $D_8$, the symmetries of a square, and check that there are ten. (Aluffi writes $D_{2n}$ for the symmetries of a regular $n$-gon, so the square's group is $D_8$ and has order $8$ — Definition 11.32, p. 242. Many texts, Carter among them, write $D_4$ for the same group; this module follows Aluffi.)

(c) Carter §6.2 (pp. 99–100) and §6.3 (p. 101) describe how a subgroup shows up in a Cayley diagram: as a copy of the subgroup's own diagram sitting inside. Describe what the subgroups of $D_8$ look like in its Cayley diagram with generators a rotation and a reflection.

(d) Define $\langle S\rangle$ as in Definition 11.11 (p. 232) and prove it equals the set of all finite products of elements of $S$ and their inverses.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Every subgroup has order dividing 6 — or, without that, note that a subgroup is generated by its elements and check the cyclic ones first.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Take the cyclic subgroups generated by each element, then look for subgroups of order 4 not generated by one element.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The trivial subgroup; three of order two, $\{e,(1\,2)\}$, $\{e,(1\,3)\}$, $\{e,(2\,3)\}$; one of order three, $A_3 = \{e,(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2)\}$; and $S_3$ itself. **Six.**

(b) Write $D_8 = \{e,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s\}$ with $r^4=e$, $s^2=e$, $srs = r^{-1}$. The subgroups are: the trivial one; five of order two, generated by $r^2$, $s$, $rs$, $r^2s$, $r^3s$; three of order four, namely $\langle r\rangle$, $\{e,r^2,s,r^2s\}$ and $\{e,r^2,rs,r^3s\}$; and $D_8$. **Ten.**
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) In the Cayley diagram of $D_8$ — two concentric squares of rotation arrows joined by reflection edges — $\langle r\rangle$ is one of the two squares taken alone, a closed cycle of four rotation arrows. The order-two reflection subgroups need more care than the picture first suggests. Only $\langle s\rangle = \{e,s\}$ is a reflection edge at the identity; the other reflection edges join $r^k$ to $r^ks$, so their endpoint pairs $\{r^k, r^ks\}$ are *cosets* of $\langle s\rangle$, not subgroups. The remaining reflection subgroups are $\{e, r^ks\}$, whose two elements sit at opposite ends of the diagram rather than on one drawn edge — which is the standard trap: an edge of the Cayley diagram shows a coset, and only the edge through $e$ shows a subgroup. The two remaining order-four subgroups are each a rectangle using two opposite reflection edges and the two half-turn arrows, and they are exactly Carter's point in §6.3 (p. 101): the subgroup appears as a sub-diagram whose own arrows are inherited, and the cosets appear as the parallel copies of it filling out the rest of the picture.

(d) Let $P$ be the set of finite products of elements of $S$ and their inverses (with the empty product $e$). $P$ is a subgroup: it is nonempty, and a product of two such products, one of them inverted, is again one, by $(x_1\cdots x_k)^{-1} = x_k^{-1}\cdots x_1^{-1}$ from Problem 1(c). It contains $S$. And any subgroup containing $S$ is closed under products and inverses, hence contains $P$. So $P$ is the smallest such subgroup, which is $\langle S\rangle$. Remark 11.12 (p. 233) is the warning that in a noncommutative group these words cannot be tidied into $x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ — the letters may have to alternate.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a group where every element is its own inverse)

Let $G$ be a group in which $g^2 = e$ for every $g$.

(a) Prove $G$ is abelian.

(b) Prove that $G$ is then a vector space over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and deduce that a finite such $G$ has order a power of $2$.

(c) Classify all groups of order $4$.

(d) Compare (a) with aa-05 Problem 6. What is the same, and what makes the group version shorter?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Apply the hypothesis to $gh$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Either some element has order 4, or every non-identity element has order 2.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $g^2=e$ says $g^{-1}=g$ for every $g$. So
$$gh = (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} = hg,$$
using Problem 1(c) in the middle. Three equalities, and $G$ is abelian.

(b) Being abelian, $G$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module (aa-15 Problem 3(c)), and $2g = 0$ for all $g$ means $2\mathbb{Z}$ annihilates it, so the action descends to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ by aa-10 Problem 3(a) — that is, $G$ is a vector space over the field $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) A finite vector space over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is free of some finite rank $n$ (Theorem 9.35, p. 190), hence has $2^n$ elements.

(c) Let $|G| = 4$. If some $g$ has order $4$ then $G = \langle g\rangle\cong C_4$. Otherwise no element has order $4$, and the order of a non-identity element is $2$ or $3$. Rule out $3$ first: a subgroup $H = \{e,g,g^2\}$ of order 3 would have cosets of *three* elements each, so $H$ together with the coset of the one remaining element $h$ would already contain six distinct elements of a four-element group. So every non-identity element has order $2$. (aa-28's Lagrange says the same thing in one line — $3 \nmid 4$ — and a bare-hands version also works: $\{e,g,g^2\}$ leaves one element $h$, and $gh$ must be one of the four, each case giving a contradiction.) So $g^2=e$ for all $g$, and by (a) and (b), $G$ is a $2$-dimensional $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$-vector space, i.e. $C_2\times C_2$. **Two groups of order 4**, and both are abelian.

(d) It is aa-05 Problem 6 with $a^2=a$ replaced by $g^2=e$, and the same shape of proof: apply the hypothesis to a combination of the two elements, and read off commutativity. The group version is shorter because there is only one operation, so the expansion of $(gh)^2$ has no distributive law to invoke and no additive statement to prove first — the ring version needed $a+a=0$ as a separate step, and here the corresponding fact *is* the hypothesis. The parallel is exact enough to be worth noticing: in both cases the hypothesis is applied to an element neither of the two you were given.
</details>
