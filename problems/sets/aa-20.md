## Problem 1 (easy–medium — Smith normal form by hand)

(a) State Lemma 9.38 (p. 193): every matrix over a Euclidean domain is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_s)$, padded with zeros, with $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s$.

(b) Reduce $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&4\\6&8\end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ to that form, showing every operation.

(c) Read off $\operatorname{coker} f_A$ and check its order two ways.

(d) Which two invariants of $A$ did you use in (b) without needing the reduction, and why do they determine $d_1$ and $d_2$ here?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Get a $2$ into the corner first — it is the gcd of all four entries — then clear its row and column.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The gcd of the entries and the determinant are unchanged (up to sign) by elementary operations.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $R_2 \mapsto R_2 - 3R_1$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\0&-4\end{pmatrix}$. Then $C_2\mapsto C_2 - 2C_1$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-4\end{pmatrix}$, and scaling the second row by $-1$ gives $\operatorname{diag}(2,4)$. And $2\mid4$, so the divisibility condition holds and no further work is needed.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) By aa-18 Problem 5(a) the cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ modulo the columns, and for a diagonal matrix that is
$$\operatorname{coker} f_A\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z},$$
of order $8$. Check: $|\det A| = |2\cdot8 - 4\cdot6| = 8$ ✓. That the cokernel of a square integer matrix is finite of order $|\det|$ whenever $\det\ne0$ is a useful sanity check on every such computation, and it is why (b) could be predicted before it was performed.

(d) The **gcd of all entries** and the **determinant**. Elementary operations change neither the gcd of the entries (each new entry is a combination of old ones, and the operations are invertible, so the gcds divide each other) nor the determinant beyond a sign. For a $2\times2$ matrix that pins both diagonal entries: $d_1 = \gcd(\text{entries}) = 2$, and $d_1d_2 = |\det| = 8$, so $d_2 = 4$. For larger matrices the same idea uses the gcds of the $k\times k$ minors, and those are the invariants Remark 9.41 (p. 196) is about.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the classification)

(a) State Theorem 9.40 (pp. 195–196) precisely, including the conditions on $r$ and on $d_1,\dots,d_s$.

(b) Derive it from Lemma 9.38 (p. 193) and Proposition 9.25 (p. 184).

(c) Explain why Theorem 9.40 needs $M$ to be finitely presented, and why that is automatic here.

(d) Remark 9.42 (p. 196) says the theorem holds over every PID, not just Euclidean domains. Which step of your derivation is the one that used a valuation?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Present $M$ as $\operatorname{coker}$ of a matrix, replace the matrix by an equivalent diagonal one, and read off the quotient.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Chain the results of aa-19: Euclidean ⟹ PID ⟹ Noetherian ⟹ finitely generated implies finitely presented.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For $M$ finitely generated over a Euclidean domain $R$, there are an integer $r\ge0$ and a list — possibly **empty**, when $M$ is free and $s=0$ — of **nonzero non-units** $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s$ of $R$ such that
$$M\ \cong\ R^{\oplus r}\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s).$$

(b) $M$ is the cokernel of some $f_A : R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}$ (aa-19 Problem 1). By Lemma 9.38, $A$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_s)$ padded with zeros; by Proposition 9.25 the cokernel is unchanged. The cokernel of a padded diagonal matrix splits coordinatewise into $R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s)$ for the diagonal part and $R^{\oplus(m-s)}$ for the zero rows. Two clean-ups then give the stated form. Discard any $d_i$ that is a unit — those contribute $R/(1)=0$ — which leaves $d_1$ a non-unit. And move any $d_i$ that is *zero* into the free part, since $R/(0)\cong R$; that is why the $d_i$ must be required nonzero, and without it the same module would have many such presentations and the uniqueness of (c) would fail. After both, $r$ counts the zero rows together with the discarded zero $d_i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Without a finite presentation there is no matrix to reduce: the whole argument runs through $M = \operatorname{coker} f_A$ for a matrix of finite size. It is automatic because a Euclidean domain is a PID (Theorem 6.26, p. 113), a PID is Noetherian (Proposition 6.32, p. 116, with aa-19 Problem 2(b)), and over a Noetherian ring finitely generated implies finitely presented (Theorem 9.29, p. 187). Three results from three different chapters, and the chain is why the theorem can say "finitely generated" in its hypothesis.

(d) Lemma 9.38, and only Lemma 9.38. Its proof picks an entry of least valuation and drives the valuation down, which needs the Euclidean valuation and the well-ordering of $\mathbb{Z}^{\ge0}$. Over a general PID the same normal form exists, but the reduction cannot be run as an algorithm on entries — Remark 9.16 (p. 181) already warned that elementary operations do not capture equivalence over every ring — and the proof goes through ideals instead. The *statement* is the same over any PID; the *method* is what the valuation buys.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — two ways to list the same module)

Corollary 9.44 (p. 197) gives a second normal form: a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(q^a)$ with $q$ irreducible. The $d_i$ of Theorem 9.40 are the **invariant factors**; the $q^a$ are the **elementary divisors**.

(a) Convert $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}$ to invariant-factor form.

(b) Convert $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ to elementary-divisor form, and vice versa.

(c) Describe the general recipe in both directions.

(d) Which form makes it obvious that $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, and which makes it obvious that $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\not\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Split each summand into prime-power pieces first, then reassemble greedily.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Elementary divisors come from splitting by primes (the Chinese Remainder Theorem); invariant factors come from taking, for each prime, the largest remaining power and multiplying across primes.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, so the elementary divisors are $2,2,3,5$. Reassembling: the largest power at each prime gives $2\cdot3\cdot5 = 30$, and what is left is a single $2$. So
$$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/30\mathbb{Z},$$
with invariant factors $2\mid30$. Order check: $6\cdot10 = 60 = 2\cdot30$ ✓.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is already in both forms: the invariant factors are $2\mid4$, and the elementary divisors are $2$ and $2^2$ — every summand is already a prime power, and the divisibility chain already holds.

(c) *Invariant factors to elementary divisors:* factor each $d_i$ into prime powers and split the summand by the Chinese Remainder Theorem (aa-10 Problem 5). *Elementary divisors to invariant factors:* for each prime, sort its powers; then $d_s$ is the product over primes of the largest power, $d_{s-1}$ the product of the next largest, and so on, with missing entries treated as $1$. The divisibility $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s$ is automatic because at each prime the exponents are taken in increasing order.

(d) The **elementary-divisor** form makes the first obvious: $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ have the same list $\{2,3\}$. The **invariant-factor** form makes the second obvious: $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has the single invariant factor $4$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has $2\mid2$, and by uniqueness those cannot be the same module. Each form is designed to make a different question easy, which is why the theorem is usually stated twice.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — uniqueness, and two classifications)

(a) State the uniqueness assertion of Remark 9.41 (p. 196).

(b) Use it to classify all abelian groups of order $8$ up to isomorphism.

(c) Do the same for order $12$.

(d) How many abelian groups of order $p^3q$ are there, for distinct primes $p,q$? Justify from (b) and (c) rather than by listing.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) A group of order 8 is torsion, so $r=0$, and the invariant factors multiply to $8$ with each dividing the next.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Count the ways to write the exponent at each prime as a partition.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The integer $r$ and the ideals $(d_1),\dots,(d_s)$ — equivalently the $d_i$ up to units — are determined by $M$. So two such decompositions of isomorphic modules have the same rank and the same invariant factors.

(b) Order $8$, so $r = 0$ and $d_1\cdots d_s = 8$ with $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s$ and each $d_i > 1$. The possibilities are $(8)$, $(2,4)$ and $(2,2,2)$:
$$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z},\qquad \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z},\qquad \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.$$
Three groups, and by (a) no two are isomorphic.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Order $12 = 2^2\cdot3$. The chains with product $12$ and each term dividing the next are $(12)$ and $(2,6)$:
$$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z},\qquad \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}.$$
Two groups. Note $(3,4)$ is not admissible — $3\nmid4$ — and indeed $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ is already on the list under its proper name.

(d) The elementary-divisor form separates the primes completely: a choice of group is a choice, independently at each prime, of a partition of that prime's exponent. The number of abelian groups of order $p^3q$ is therefore $p(3)\cdot p(1) = 3\cdot1 = 3$, where $p(n)$ is the number of partitions of $n$ — the partitions of $3$ being $3$, $2{+}1$ and $1{+}1{+}1$, which is exactly the list in (b), and the single partition of $1$ contributing the $q$ factor. Order $8 = 2^3$ gave $3$ and order $12 = 2^2\cdot3$ gives $p(2)p(1) = 2$, matching (c).
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the free part, and what falls out of the proof)

(a) State Remark 9.43 (p. 196): every submodule of a finitely generated free module over a Euclidean domain is free, of rank at most that of the ambient module.

(b) Prove the case of $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m}$ by induction, generalising aa-18 Problem 6(b).

(c) Deduce that every subgroup of a finitely generated free abelian group is free, and that a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ of finite index has rank exactly $2$.

(d) Show the rank can drop: give a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ that is free of rank $1$, and one of rank $0$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Project onto the last coordinate and split the submodule as in aa-18 Problem 6(b).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Finite index means the quotient is finite, so the rank of the quotient is $0$; ranks add across a short exact sequence of free modules.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Induct on $m$. For $m=1$: a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $d\mathbb{Z}$, free of rank $\le1$ (aa-18 Problem 6(a)). For the step, let $N\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m}$ and $\pi$ the last coordinate. Then $\pi(N) = c\mathbb{Z}$, and $N_0 = N\cap\ker\pi$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus(m-1)}$, free of rank $\le m-1$ by induction. If $c=0$ then $N = N_0$; otherwise choose $v\in N$ with $\pi(v)=c$, and $N = N_0\oplus\mathbb{Z}v$ exactly as in aa-18 Problem 6(b). Rank at most $m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) A finitely generated free abelian group is $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m}$, so (b) applies. For the index claim: if $N\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ has finite index, then $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}/N$ is finite, hence torsion, hence has rank $0$ in the sense of Theorem 9.40. Were $N$ of rank $\le1$, the quotient would contain a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ — for instance $N\subseteq\mathbb{Z}v$ for a single $v$ leaves the complementary direction untouched — and would be infinite. So the rank is $2$.

(d) Rank $1$: $N = \mathbb{Z}\cdot(1,0) = \mathbb{Z}\oplus0$, with quotient $\mathbb{Z}$, infinite. Rank $0$: $N = \{(0,0)\}$. Both are free, and both have rank strictly below $2$ — which is why (a) says "at most", and why "finite index" is the extra hypothesis that forces equality.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a linear map is a k[t]-module, and Cayley–Hamilton falls out)

Let $k$ be a field, $V$ a **nonzero** finite-dimensional $k$-vector space, and $\alpha : V\to V$ a linear map. Define an action of $k[t]$ on $V$ by
$$f(t)\cdot v := f(\alpha)(v).$$

(The hypothesis $V\ne0$ is only to keep parts (c)–(e) from being vacuous. For $V=0$ the decomposition has no invariant factors at all, $s=0$, so there is no $f_s$ to name; the annihilator and the minimal polynomial are both generated by $1$, and the characteristic polynomial is the empty product $1$.)

(a) Check this makes $V$ a $k[t]$-module, and that the $k[t]$-submodules are exactly the $\alpha$-invariant subspaces.

(b) Show $V$ is finitely generated and **torsion** as a $k[t]$-module.

(c) Apply Theorem 9.40 to get $V\cong k[t]/(f_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus k[t]/(f_s)$ with $f_1\mid\cdots\mid f_s$, and explain why the free part is absent.

(d) Show $f_s$ is the **minimal polynomial** of $\alpha$: the monic generator of $\{f : f(\alpha)=0\}$.

(e) Granting that the characteristic polynomial of $\alpha$ equals $f_1f_2\cdots f_s$, deduce Theorem 9.45 (p. 199), the Cayley–Hamilton theorem. Identify precisely which step of your argument is the one being granted.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) $k[t]$ is infinite-dimensional over $k$ and $V$ is not, so the map $k[t]\to V$, $f\mapsto f\cdot v$, cannot be injective.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The annihilator of the whole module is the intersection of the annihilators of the summands, which is $(f_s)$ because $f_i \mid f_s$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The module axioms are the statements that $f\mapsto f(\alpha)$ is a ring homomorphism $k[t]\to\operatorname{End}_k(V)$ — which it is, since substituting a fixed $\alpha$ respects sums and products of polynomials and sends $1$ to the identity — together with Example 8.6 (p. 155)'s construction of a module from such a map. A subset is a submodule exactly when it is a $k$-subspace ($k\subseteq k[t]$ acts) and is closed under $t$, i.e. under $\alpha$.

(b) A $k$-basis of $V$ generates $V$ over $k[t]$, since $k\subseteq k[t]$; so $V$ is finitely generated. For torsion: fix $v\ne0$ and consider $v,\alpha v,\alpha^2v,\dots$. These cannot be $k$-independent, $V$ being finite-dimensional, so some nontrivial $\sum c_i\alpha^iv = 0$, i.e. $f\cdot v = 0$ for a nonzero $f$. So every element is torsion.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $k[t]$ is a Euclidean domain (Theorem 7.2, p. 132), so Theorem 9.40 applies and gives $V\cong k[t]^{\oplus r}\oplus\bigoplus_i k[t]/(f_i)$. The free part must be absent: $k[t]^{\oplus r}$ has elements killed by nothing nonzero, contradicting (b) unless $r=0$. Equivalently $k[t]$ is infinite-dimensional over $k$ and $V$ is not.

(d) Let $A = \{f\in k[t] : f(\alpha) = 0\}$, an ideal — it is the kernel of the ring homomorphism $f\mapsto f(\alpha)$ — hence $(g)$ for a unique monic $g$, since $k[t]$ is a PID (Corollary 7.7, p. 134). Now $f\cdot V = 0$ means $f$ kills every summand $k[t]/(f_i)$, i.e. $f_i\mid f$ for every $i$; because $f_1\mid\cdots\mid f_s$, that is equivalent to $f_s\mid f$. So $A = (f_s)$ and $g = f_s$ up to a unit, i.e. up to making it monic.

(e) Write $P(t) = f_1f_2\cdots f_s$ for the characteristic polynomial — this is the granted step. Since $f_s\mid P$, and $f_s(\alpha) = 0$ by (d), we get $P(\alpha) = (P/f_s)(\alpha)\,f_s(\alpha) = 0$. That is Cayley–Hamilton.

The step being granted is precisely **$\det(tI-\alpha) = f_1\cdots f_s$**: that the characteristic polynomial, defined by a determinant, equals the product of the invariant factors. It is proved by computing the determinant blockwise on the decomposition of (c), where each block is a companion matrix whose characteristic polynomial is its own $f_i$. Everything else above is the structure theorem, and once the identification is made, Cayley–Hamilton is the single observation that $f_s$ divides the product of the $f_i$ — which the theorem's divisibility chain hands over for free.
</details>
