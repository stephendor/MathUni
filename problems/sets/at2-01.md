## Problem 1 (easy — simplicial cohomology of a graph)

Let $X$ be the oriented graph with vertices $v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3$ and edges
$a = [v_0,v_1]$, $b = [v_1,v_2]$, $c = [v_0,v_2]$, $d = [v_2,v_3]$. Fix an
abelian group $G$.

(a) Write down $\delta : \Delta^0(X;G) \to \Delta^1(X;G)$ explicitly as four
equations, one per edge.
(b) Compute $H^0(X;G) = \operatorname{Ker}\delta$ directly from those equations.
(c) Choose a maximal tree $T \subseteq X$ and use it to compute $H^1(X;G)$.
State how many copies of $G$ you get and which edge is responsible.
(d) Compare with $H_1(X;G)$. For which $G$ would the answers to (c) and (d)
differ as groups, and why does the difference not show up here?

*(Hatcher, "The Idea of Cohomology", pp. 186–187)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\delta\varphi([v_i,v_j]) = \varphi(v_j) - \varphi(v_i)$. A maximal tree in a
connected graph on $4$ vertices has $3$ edges.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b) read the four equations as "$\varphi$ takes the same value at both ends
of every edge" and use connectedness. For (c) follow Hatcher's argument: the
values of $\psi$ on tree edges determine $\varphi$ up to one constant, and the
value of $\psi$ on each non-tree edge is then forced — so the failure is
measured by one free copy of $G$ per non-tree edge.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^0(X;G) \cong G$. A maximal tree is $T = \{a, b, d\}$, leaving $c$ outside, so
$H^1(X;G) \cong G$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\delta\varphi(a) = \varphi(v_1)-\varphi(v_0)$,
$\delta\varphi(b) = \varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_1)$,
$\delta\varphi(c) = \varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_0)$,
$\delta\varphi(d) = \varphi(v_3)-\varphi(v_2)$.
(b) $\delta\varphi = 0$ forces $\varphi(v_0)=\varphi(v_1)=\varphi(v_2)=\varphi(v_3)$;
$X$ is connected, so $H^0(X;G)$ is the group of functions from the one-element
set of components to $G$, i.e. $\cong G$.
(c) Take $T = \{a,b,d\}$ (a spanning tree: it touches every vertex and has no
loop). Given $\psi \in \Delta^1(X;G)$, fix $\varphi(v_0)$ arbitrarily; the tree
edges then determine $\varphi(v_1) = \varphi(v_0)+\psi(a)$,
$\varphi(v_2) = \varphi(v_1)+\psi(b)$, $\varphi(v_3) = \varphi(v_2)+\psi(d)$.
For $\delta\varphi = \psi$ we would also need
$\psi(c) = \varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_0) = \psi(a)+\psi(b)$, which is a genuine
constraint: $\psi(c)$ was arbitrary. So the obstruction is one copy of $G$, the
class of $\psi(c) - \psi(a) - \psi(b)$, and $H^1(X;G) \cong G$.
(d) Both are one copy of $G$ here. Hatcher's point is that in general
$H^1(X;G)$ is a direct *product* of copies of $G$ and $H_1(X;G)$ a direct
*sum*, one for each non-tree edge; with finitely many non-tree edges — as here,
just one — product and sum agree.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — what $\delta\psi = 0$ says)

Let $X$ be a $2$-dimensional $\Delta$-complex, $\psi \in \Delta^1(X;G)$, and
recall $\delta\psi([v_0,v_1,v_2]) = \psi([v_0,v_1]) + \psi([v_1,v_2]) - \psi([v_0,v_2])$.

(a) Prove that $\delta\psi = 0$ if and only if $\psi$ is *additive* on every
$2$-simplex, i.e. $\psi([v_0,v_2]) = \psi([v_0,v_1]) + \psi([v_1,v_2])$.
(b) Verify by direct computation that $\delta\delta\varphi = 0$ for every
$\varphi \in \Delta^0(X;G)$, writing out all six terms.
(c) Hatcher calls $\delta\psi$ a *local* obstruction to solving
$\psi = \delta\varphi$, and the resulting class in $H^1(X;G)$ the *global*
obstruction. Explain in two or three sentences what "local" and "global" mean
here, and give the calculus statement each one is analogous to.

*(Hatcher, "The Idea of Cohomology", p. 188)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c): the local condition is checkable inside a single $2$-simplex; the
global one is not checkable anywhere in particular.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) is bookkeeping — substitute $\delta\varphi$ into the formula for $\delta$ on
$\Delta^1$ and watch the six values of $\varphi$ cancel in pairs.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\delta\delta\varphi([v_0,v_1,v_2]) = (\varphi(v_1)-\varphi(v_0)) + (\varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_1)) - (\varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_0))$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Immediate from the formula: $\delta\psi([v_0,v_1,v_2]) = 0$ *is* the
equation $\psi([v_0,v_1]) + \psi([v_1,v_2]) = \psi([v_0,v_2])$, and $\delta\psi$
vanishes iff this holds on every $2$-simplex.
(b) Substituting, $\delta\delta\varphi([v_0,v_1,v_2]) = \delta\varphi([v_0,v_1]) + \delta\varphi([v_1,v_2]) - \delta\varphi([v_0,v_2]) = (\varphi(v_1)-\varphi(v_0)) + (\varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_1)) - (\varphi(v_2)-\varphi(v_0)) = 0$.
(c) Local: $\delta\psi = 0$ is a condition inside each individual $2$-simplex,
checkable simplex by simplex — the analogue of a vector field having zero curl.
Global: even with $\delta\psi = 0$ everywhere, $\psi$ need not be $\delta\varphi$;
what remains is the class of $\psi$ in $H^1(X;G)$ — the analogue of every line
integral around a closed loop vanishing. Hatcher's annulus is exactly a case
where the local obstruction vanishes and the global one does not.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — dualising a chain complex, and where the torsion goes)

Consider the chain complex of free abelian groups
$$0 \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\;0\;} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\;2\;} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\;0\;} \mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow 0$$
with the four terms being $C_3, C_2, C_1, C_0$ and $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z}$ the map $x \mapsto 2x$.

(a) Compute $H_n(C)$ for $n = 0,1,2,3$.
(b) Write out the dual cochain complex $\operatorname{Hom}(C_n, \mathbb{Z})$ with
its coboundary maps, and compute $H^n(C;\mathbb{Z})$ for $n = 0,1,2,3$.
(c) State precisely what happened to the torsion.
(d) Redo (b) with $G = \mathbb{Z}_2$ and then with $G = \mathbb{Z}_3$. Explain
why the torsion is visible for one of these coefficient groups and invisible for
the other.

*(Hatcher §3.1, p. 190)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},G) \cong G$, and under this identification the
dual of multiplication by $m$ is again multiplication by $m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Dualising reverses every arrow but keeps every map's formula. Then just read off
kernels and images four times. For (d) ask which of $\mathbb{Z}_2, \mathbb{Z}_3$
has $2$ acting invertibly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$, $H_2 = 0$, $H_3 = \mathbb{Z}$;
$H^0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H^1 = 0$, $H^2 = \mathbb{Z}_2$, $H^3 = \mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\partial_1 = 0$ so $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$. $\operatorname{Ker}\partial_1 = \mathbb{Z}$
and $\operatorname{Im}\partial_2 = 2\mathbb{Z}$, so $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
$\operatorname{Ker}\partial_2 = 0$ so $H_2 = 0$. $\operatorname{Ker}\partial_3 = \mathbb{Z}$
so $H_3 = \mathbb{Z}$.
(b) The dual complex is
$0 \leftarrow \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{2} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \leftarrow 0$
with $C_0^* \xrightarrow{\delta = 0} C_1^* \xrightarrow{\delta = 2} C_2^* \xrightarrow{\delta = 0} C_3^*$.
Hence $H^0 = \operatorname{Ker}(0) = \mathbb{Z}$;
$H^1 = \operatorname{Ker}(\times 2)/\operatorname{Im}(0) = 0$;
$H^2 = \operatorname{Ker}(0)/\operatorname{Im}(\times 2) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_2$;
$H^3 = \mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{Im}(0) = \mathbb{Z}$.
(c) The free parts sat still (dimensions $0$ and $3$) and the $\mathbb{Z}_2$
moved up one dimension, from $H_1$ to $H^2$. Hatcher's general statement for
finitely generated free chain complexes is exactly this: cohomology equals
homology with the torsion shifted up a dimension.
(d) With $G = \mathbb{Z}_2$ the map $\times 2$ is the zero map, so
$H^1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$ and $H^2 = \mathbb{Z}_2$. With $G = \mathbb{Z}_3$ the map
$\times 2$ is invertible, so $H^1 = 0$ and $H^2 = 0$: the $2$-torsion is
completely invisible. Torsion is only detected by coefficient groups in which
the relevant multiplication fails to be invertible — the reason TDA's choice of
coefficient field is not cosmetic.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — $\delta$ really is the dual of $\partial$)

(a) For a homomorphism $\alpha : A \to B$ of abelian groups, the dual is
$\alpha^* : \operatorname{Hom}(B,G) \to \operatorname{Hom}(A,G)$,
$\alpha^*(\varphi) = \varphi\alpha$. Prove $(\alpha\beta)^* = \beta^*\alpha^*$,
$\mathbb{1}^* = \mathbb{1}$ and $0^* = 0$.
(b) Deduce $\delta\delta = 0$ from $\partial\partial = 0$ in one line, using only
(a). Note where you used which identity.
(c) Show that the simplicial formula
$\delta\varphi([v_0,\dots,v_{i+1}]) = \sum_j (-1)^j \varphi([v_0,\dots,\hat{v}_j,\dots,v_{i+1}])$
is exactly the statement $\delta\varphi = \varphi\partial$ — that is, that the
$\delta$ of "The Idea of Cohomology" and the $\delta = \partial^*$ of §3.1 are
the same map.
(d) Why is $\Delta^i(X;G)$ the same thing as $\operatorname{Hom}(\Delta_i(X),G)$?
Name the property of $\Delta_i(X)$ that makes this work, and give an example of
an abelian group for which the corresponding statement would fail.

*(Hatcher, "The Idea of Cohomology", p. 189; §3.1, p. 191)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(d) turns on $\Delta_i(X)$ being free abelian with the $i$-simplices as basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Everything in (a) is a two-line associativity check on composites. For (b),
$\delta$ is $\partial^*$ by definition, so $\delta\delta = \partial^*\partial^* = (\partial\partial)^*$
— being careful about the order, which is the content of the first identity.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(\alpha\beta)^*(\varphi) = \varphi(\alpha\beta) = (\varphi\alpha)\beta = \beta^*(\varphi\alpha) = \beta^*\alpha^*(\varphi)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For $\varphi$ in the appropriate dual group,
$(\alpha\beta)^*(\varphi) = \varphi\alpha\beta$, while
$\beta^*\alpha^*(\varphi) = \beta^*(\varphi\alpha) = \varphi\alpha\beta$; equal by
associativity of composition. $\mathbb{1}^*(\varphi) = \varphi\mathbb{1} = \varphi$
and $0^*(\varphi) = \varphi 0 = 0$.
(b) $\delta\delta = \partial^*\partial^* = (\partial\partial)^* = 0^* = 0$,
using $(\alpha\beta)^* = \beta^*\alpha^*$ to combine the two duals and $0^* = 0$
at the end.
(c) A homomorphism out of the free abelian group $\Delta_{i+1}(X)$ is determined
by its values on the basis of $(i{+}1)$-simplices. Evaluating $\varphi\partial$
on the basis element $[v_0,\dots,v_{i+1}]$ gives
$\varphi(\partial[v_0,\dots,v_{i+1}]) = \varphi(\sum_j (-1)^j [v_0,\dots,\hat v_j,\dots,v_{i+1}]) = \sum_j (-1)^j \varphi([v_0,\dots,\hat v_j,\dots,v_{i+1}])$,
which is the displayed formula. So the two definitions agree on a basis, hence
agree.
(d) $\Delta_i(X)$ is free abelian with basis the $i$-simplices, and a
homomorphism out of a free abelian group may be prescribed arbitrarily on a
basis — so functions {$i$-simplices} $\to G$ correspond exactly to homomorphisms
$\Delta_i(X) \to G$. It fails for non-free groups: a function
$\{\bar 0, \bar 1\} \to \mathbb{Z}$ on the elements of $\mathbb{Z}_2$ sending
$\bar 1 \mapsto 1$ extends to no homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}_2 \to \mathbb{Z}$ at
all, since $\bar 1 + \bar 1 = \bar 0$ would force $1 + 1 = 0$.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the evaluation map $h$) [interleaves la-10]

Let $C$ be a chain complex of free abelian groups, $Z_n = \operatorname{Ker}\partial$,
$B_n = \operatorname{Im}\partial$, and let $G$ be an abelian group.

(a) Show that a cocycle $\varphi \in C_n^* = \operatorname{Hom}(C_n,G)$ vanishes
on $B_n$, and that consequently $\varphi|_{Z_n}$ induces
$\overline{\varphi}_0 : H_n(C) \to G$.
(b) Show that if $\varphi \in \operatorname{Im}\delta$ then
$\overline{\varphi}_0 = 0$, so that
$h : H^n(C;G) \to \operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C), G)$, $[\varphi] \mapsto \overline{\varphi}_0$,
is well defined.
(c) Prove that $0 \to Z_n \to C_n \xrightarrow{\partial} B_{n-1} \to 0$ splits,
and use a splitting $p : C_n \to Z_n$ to prove that $h$ is surjective.
(d) Conclude that $0 \to \operatorname{Ker} h \to H^n(C;G) \xrightarrow{h} \operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C),G) \to 0$
is a *split* short exact sequence. Then explain, in one paragraph, why this does
**not** yet prove $H^n(C;G) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C),G)$, using the
complex of Problem 3 as the counterexample and identifying which group
$\operatorname{Ker} h$ is there.

*(Hatcher §3.1, pp. 191–193)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
"Cocycle" means $\delta\varphi = \varphi\partial = 0$, i.e. $\varphi$ kills
everything in the image of $\partial$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the splitting is available because $B_{n-1}$ is free — a subgroup of
the free abelian group $C_{n-1}$. Given $p$, extend any
$\varphi_0 : Z_n \to G$ vanishing on $B_n$ to $\varphi_0 p : C_n \to G$, and
check that this is a cocycle mapping to the class you started with.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
In Problem 3 with $G = \mathbb{Z}$: $H^2(C;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ while
$\operatorname{Hom}(H_2(C),\mathbb{Z}) = \operatorname{Hom}(0,\mathbb{Z}) = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\delta\varphi = \varphi\partial = 0$ says precisely that $\varphi$ vanishes
on $\operatorname{Im}\partial = B_n$. Restricting to $Z_n$ therefore gives a
homomorphism killing $B_n$, which factors through $Z_n/B_n = H_n(C)$; call the
induced map $\overline{\varphi}_0$.
(b) If $\varphi = \delta\psi = \psi\partial$ then $\varphi$ vanishes on all of
$Z_n$ (since $\partial$ kills $Z_n$), so $\varphi_0 = 0$ and
$\overline{\varphi}_0 = 0$. Hence $h$ depends only on the class of $\varphi$.
(c) $B_{n-1} \subseteq C_{n-1}$ is a subgroup of a free abelian group, hence
free; a short exact sequence with free quotient splits, giving
$p : C_n \to Z_n$ with $p|_{Z_n} = \mathbb{1}$. Now let
$\overline{\varphi}_0 \in \operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C),G)$ be arbitrary, lift it to
$\varphi_0 : Z_n \to G$ vanishing on $B_n$, and set $\varphi = \varphi_0 p$.
Then $\varphi\partial = \varphi_0 p \partial = 0$ because $p\partial$ lands in
$B_n \subseteq Z_n$ where $\varphi_0$ vanishes, so $\varphi$ is a cocycle; and
$h[\varphi] = \overline{\varphi}_0$ because $\varphi|_{Z_n} = \varphi_0$.
(d) Surjectivity gives exactness at the right, $\operatorname{Ker} h$ supplies
exactness at the left by definition, and the construction in (c) is a
homomorphic section $\operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C),G) \to H^n(C;G)$, so the sequence
splits. It does not follow that $h$ is an isomorphism, because
$\operatorname{Ker} h$ need not vanish: in Problem 3, $H_2(C) = 0$ so
$\operatorname{Hom}(H_2(C),\mathbb{Z}) = 0$, yet $H^2(C;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
So there $\operatorname{Ker} h = \mathbb{Z}_2$ — the whole group. Identifying
$\operatorname{Ker} h$ in general is the business of at2-02, where it turns out
to be $\operatorname{Ext}(H_{n-1}(C),G)$.
</details>
