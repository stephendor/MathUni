## Problem 1 (easy–medium — the ideals of ℤ, sorted)

For each $n \ge 0$, decide whether $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ is prime, maximal, both, or neither, working directly from Definition 6.1 (p. 106) and Definition 6.2 (p. 106) — that is, by identifying the quotient.

(a) $n = 0$. (b) $n = 7$. (c) $n = 12$. (d) $n = 1$.

(e) State the general rule, and check it against Example 6.6 (p. 107) and Example 6.11 (p. 108).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 6.1 says $I$ is prime when $R/I$ is a domain; Definition 6.2 says $I$ is maximal when $R/I$ is a field. So the whole problem is: what is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Theorem 2.15 (p. 32) already told you when $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain and when it is a field, and gave the same answer to both questions.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\mathbb{Z}/(0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, an integral domain but not a field. So $(0)$ is prime and not maximal.

(b) $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ is a field by Theorem 2.15, hence also a domain. So $(7)$ is both.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ has $[3][4] = [0]$, so it is not a domain and $(12)$ is neither prime nor maximal.

(d) $(1) = \mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the zero ring, which is neither a domain nor a field — both definitions require $1 \ne 0$. Definition 6.1 and Definition 6.2 (p. 106) therefore exclude $R$ itself automatically. That is why Theorem 6.4 (p. 107) and Theorem 6.9 (p. 108) both carry "$I \ne (1)$" as a separate clause when they restate the conditions in terms of elements.

(e) **$(n)$ is prime exactly when $n=0$ or $|n|$ is an ordinary positive prime; it is maximal exactly when $|n|$ is an ordinary positive prime.** Say it that way rather than "prime", because this course follows Aluffi in counting $0$ as a prime *integer* (aa-02) — and under that convention "maximal exactly when $n$ is prime" would wrongly include $(0)$, contradicting (a). Maximality needs a **nonzero** prime. So in $\mathbb{Z}$ the only gap between the two notions is the zero ideal — Example 6.11 (p. 108) puts it as: an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is maximal if and only if it is a *nonzero* prime ideal. That $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain and a field under exactly the same condition is Theorem 2.15, and it happens because those quotients are finite (aa-06 Problem 4).
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — maximal implies prime, and the two witnesses that the converse fails)

(a) Prove Proposition 6.3 (p. 106): every maximal ideal is prime.

(b) Give an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ that is prime and not maximal.

(c) Give an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ that is prime and not maximal, and prove both halves. Compare Example 6.7 (p. 107) and Example 6.12 (p. 109).

(d) Example 6.5 (p. 107) says a commutative ring is an integral domain if and only if $(0)$ is prime. State and prove the corresponding statement for maximal, and identify which earlier result it is.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is one line once you name the right earlier result about fields.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Use the quotient. $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain that is not a field.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $I$ is maximal then $R/I$ is a field, and every field is an integral domain — Proposition 3.31 (p. 55). So $R/I$ is a domain and $I$ is prime. The proposition is a translation of a fact about rings into a fact about ideals, and carries no new content; that is the point of defining both notions through the quotient.

(b) $(0)$, by Problem 1(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $(x)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Prime: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ by aa-10 Problem 2(c), and $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain. Not maximal: $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field. Concretely, $(x)\subsetneq(2,x)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[x]$ — the middle ideal is proper because it consists of the polynomials with even constant term, and $1$ has odd constant term — so $(x)$ is not maximal by Theorem 6.9 (p. 108) either. And $(2,x)$ *is* maximal, since $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2,x)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a field, which is Example 6.12 (p. 109).

Note this is a strictly better witness than (b): in $\mathbb{Z}$ the only prime-not-maximal ideal is $(0)$, which can look like a degenerate exception. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ there is a nonzero one, and a chain of three distinct ideals to see it in.

(d) **$R$ is a field if and only if $(0)$ is maximal.** By Definition 6.2 this says $R/(0)\cong R$ is a field, which is a tautology in one direction; the content is the version of it Example 6.10 (p. 108) gives — $(0)$ is maximal exactly when $R$ has exactly two ideals — and that is aa-09 Problem 3, proved there from scratch. Theorem 6.9 turns it into a statement about a chain: no ideal strictly between $(0)$ and $R$ means no ideals besides those two.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — prime, said with elements)

(a) Prove Theorem 6.4 (p. 107): $I$ is prime if and only if $I \ne (1)$ and, for all $a,b\in R$, $ab\in I$ implies $a\in I$ or $b\in I$.

(b) Check the "$I\ne(1)$" clause is not redundant: verify that $I = R$ satisfies the element condition and is not prime.

(c) Use (a) to prove directly that $(x)$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, without computing the quotient.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) "$ab \in I$" says $ab + I = 0_{R/I}$, and "$a\in I$ or $b\in I$" says $a+I = 0$ or $b+I=0$. Those are the two halves of "$R/I$ has no zero-divisors" written in $R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $ab \in (x)$ means the product has zero constant term. What does that say about the constant terms of $a$ and $b$?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) Let $I$ be prime, so $R/I$ is a domain and in particular $1_{R/I}\ne 0_{R/I}$, giving $I\ne(1)$. If $ab\in I$ then $(a+I)(b+I) = ab+I = 0_{R/I}$, so one factor is zero, i.e. $a\in I$ or $b\in I$.

($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, $I\ne(1)$ gives $1_{R/I}\ne 0_{R/I}$. If $(a+I)(b+I)=0$ then $ab\in I$, so $a\in I$ or $b\in I$, i.e. $a+I=0$ or $b+I=0$. So $R/I$ is a nonzero commutative ring with no zero-divisors: a domain.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) With $I = R$: for any $a, b$, certainly $ab\in R$, and also $a\in R$ — so the implication holds vacuously in the strongest way. Yet $R/R$ is the zero ring, not a domain. So the element condition alone is satisfied by an ideal that must not be called prime, and the clause $I\ne(1)$ is doing real work. It is the same clause as the "$1\ne 0$" in Definition 3.23 (p. 53), transported across the quotient.

(c) The constant term of a product of polynomials over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the product of the constant terms. So $\alpha\beta\in(x)$ says $\alpha(0)\beta(0)=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$; since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain, $\alpha(0)=0$ or $\beta(0)=0$, i.e. $\alpha\in(x)$ or $\beta\in(x)$. And $(x)\ne(1)$ because $1\notin(x)$. So $(x)$ is prime by (a).

The argument used that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain, and would have worked over any domain — the general statement being that $R[x]/(x)\cong R$, so $(x)$ is prime in $R[x]$ exactly when $R$ is a domain.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — maximal, said with ideals)

(a) Prove the **correspondence**: for an ideal $I$ of $R$, the ideals of $R/I$ are exactly the sets $J/I$ for ideals $J$ with $I\subseteq J\subseteq R$, and this correspondence preserves inclusion in both directions.

(b) Use (a) to prove Theorem 6.9 (p. 108): $I$ is maximal if and only if $I\ne(1)$ and there is no ideal $J$ with $I\subsetneq J\subsetneq R$.

(c) Deduce Proposition 6.3 (p. 106) a second time, now from Theorem 6.4 and Theorem 6.9 rather than from fields being domains.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Send an ideal $\bar J$ of $R/I$ to $\pi^{-1}(\bar J)$, where $\pi : R\to R/I$ is the projection. (b) Combine (a) with aa-09 Problem 3.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Suppose $I$ is maximal and $ab\in I$ with $a\notin I$. Consider the ideal $I + (a)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $\bar J$ is an ideal of $R/I$ then $J := \pi^{-1}(\bar J)$ is an ideal of $R$ containing $I = \pi^{-1}(0)$, and $J/I = \bar J$ since $\pi$ is surjective. Conversely if $I\subseteq J$ then $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$, as checked in aa-10 Problem 6(c). The two constructions are mutually inverse, and both plainly preserve $\subseteq$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) By Definition 6.2, $I$ is maximal iff $R/I$ is a field; by aa-09 Problem 3, a nonzero commutative ring is a field iff its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and itself. Under (a), the ideals of $R/I$ correspond to the ideals of $R$ between $I$ and $R$, with $\{0\}$ corresponding to $I$ and $R/I$ to $R$. So "exactly two ideals" reads as "no ideal strictly between $I$ and $R$", and "nonzero" reads as $I\ne(1)$.

(c) Let $I$ be maximal and let $ab\in I$ with $a\notin I$. Then $I + (a)$ is an ideal strictly containing $I$, so by (b) it is $R$; write $1 = i + ra$ with $i\in I$, $r\in R$. Multiply by $b$:
$$b = ib + r(ab),$$
and both terms lie in $I$ — the first by absorption, the second because $ab\in I$. So $b\in I$, and $I$ is prime by Theorem 6.4.

This is the same theorem twice, and the two proofs are worth having side by side: the first is a translation of "fields are domains", and the second is Bézout's argument in disguise — $1 = i + ra$ is the linear combination, and multiplying through by $b$ is exactly the step in aa-02 Problem 3(a).
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — prime elements and irreducible elements)

Let $R$ be an integral domain.

(a) Unwind Definition 6.18 (p. 111) with Theorem 6.4: state what "$p$ is a prime element" says about $p$ using only divisibility.

(b) Prove Theorem 6.23 (p. 112): a nonzero prime element is irreducible in the sense of Definition 6.20 (p. 111).

(c) Show the converse fails. In $R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,] = \{a+b\sqrt{-5} : a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}$, use the norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2+5b^2$ to prove that $2$ is irreducible and not prime.

(d) In $\mathbb{Z}$ the two notions agree — that was aa-02 Problem 5. Which hypothesis of that argument does $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ fail?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) First check $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$, and that $\alpha$ is a unit iff $N(\alpha)=1$. Then ask which integers are values of $N$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) For "not prime", factor $6$ two ways.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(p)$ prime means $ab\in(p)\Rightarrow a\in(p)$ or $b\in(p)$, and by Definition 6.15 (p. 110) "$x\in(p)$" is exactly "$p\mid x$". So: $p$ is not a unit, and $p\mid ab$ implies $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$ — Aluffi's Definition 1.19 (p. 14) for $\mathbb{Z}$, now stated in any domain. (Definition 6.18 admits $p=0$; Remark 6.19, p. 111, discusses that choice.)

(b) Let $p\ne 0$ be prime and suppose $p = ab$. Then $p\mid ab$, so $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$; say $a = pc$. Then $p = pcb$, and cancelling $p$ — legitimate since $R$ is a domain and $p\ne0$ — gives $1 = cb$, so $b$ is a unit. Also $p$ is not a unit, since $(p)$ prime forces $(p)\ne(1)$. So $p$ is irreducible.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $N$ is multiplicative: $N(\alpha) = \alpha\bar\alpha$ for the conjugate $\overline{a+b\sqrt{-5}} = a - b\sqrt{-5}$, and conjugation is a ring homomorphism, so $N(\alpha\beta) = \alpha\beta\overline{\alpha\beta} = (\alpha\bar\alpha)(\beta\bar\beta)$. If $\alpha\beta = 1$ then $N(\alpha)N(\beta)=1$ with both non-negative integers, so $N(\alpha)=1$; conversely $N(\alpha)=1$ gives $\alpha\bar\alpha = 1$, so $\alpha$ is a unit. Hence the units are $\pm1$, since $a^2+5b^2=1$ forces $b=0$, $a=\pm1$.

*Irreducible.* Suppose $2 = \alpha\beta$. Then $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = N(2) = 4$. The value $2$ is not attained by $N$: $a^2+5b^2=2$ needs $b=0$ and $a^2=2$. So $\{N(\alpha),N(\beta)\} = \{1,4\}$, and whichever has norm $1$ is a unit. So $2$ is irreducible.

*Not prime.* $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, so $2 \mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. But $2\nmid(1\pm\sqrt{-5})$: if $1\pm\sqrt{-5} = 2\gamma$ then taking norms gives $6 = 4N(\gamma)$, and $4\nmid 6$. So $2$ divides a product without dividing either factor.

(d) aa-02 Problem 5(b) proved irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime using **Lemma 1.18 and then Corollary 1.11 — that is, Bézout**. What $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ lacks is Bézout: $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ have no common divisor but a unit, yet $1$ is not of the form $2\alpha + (1+\sqrt{-5})\beta$. Equivalently, the ideal $(2,\,1+\sqrt{-5})$ is proper and not principal, so $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ is not a PID — which aa-12 will show is exactly the condition that repairs this.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the two things a gcd can fail to be)

Definition 6.16 (p. 110) calls $d$ a **gcd** of $a$ and $b$ when $(d)$ is the smallest principal ideal containing both $a$ and $b$. Remark 6.17 (p. 110) raises two questions about it. This problem answers both.

(a) Show that if $d$ is a gcd of $a$ and $b$, so is $du$ for every unit $u$; so "the" gcd is determined only up to units.

(b) In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, show that $1$ is a gcd of $2$ and $x$, but that $1$ is **not** an integer linear combination $2\alpha(x) + x\beta(x)$. Conclude that Bézout's theorem is not a consequence of the definition of gcd.

(c) Deduce that $(2,x)$ is not a principal ideal, and hence that $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID.

(d) In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$, show that $6$ and $2 + 2\sqrt{-5}$ have **no** gcd at all: exhibit two common divisors, neither dividing the other, such that no common divisor is divisible by both.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Evaluate a hypothetical identity $1 = 2\alpha(x)+x\beta(x)$ at $x=0$. (d) Consider $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ as common divisors.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) A gcd $d$ would have to be divisible by both $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ and to divide $6$. Take norms.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(du) = (d)$ for a unit $u$, since $d = (du)u^{-1}$ and $du = d\cdot u$. Two elements generating the same principal ideal are interchangeable in Definition 6.16, so the property "is a gcd" is a property of the ideal, not of the element.

(b) The common divisors of $2$ and $x$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ are the units $\pm1$: a common divisor $d$ divides $2$, so $\deg d = 0$ and $d\in\{\pm1,\pm2\}$; and $d\mid x$ rules out $\pm 2$, since $x/2\notin\mathbb{Z}[x]$. So $(1)=\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is the smallest principal ideal containing both — trivially, being the only one — and $1$ is a gcd.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) Suppose $1 = 2\alpha(x)+x\beta(x)$. Evaluating at $x = 0$ gives $1 = 2\alpha(0)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, which is impossible. So the gcd is not a linear combination, and the implication "gcd $=1$ $\Rightarrow$ Bézout" that aa-02 relied on is a theorem about $\mathbb{Z}$, not a consequence of Definition 6.16.

(c) $(2,x)$ consists of all $2\alpha+x\beta$, i.e. all polynomials with even constant term. If $(2,x) = (d)$ then $d\mid 2$ and $d\mid x$, so $d$ is a unit by the argument in (b), so $(d) = \mathbb{Z}[x]$ — but $1\notin(2,x)$. Contradiction; so $(2,x)$ is not principal and $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID.

(d) Put $a = 6$ and $b = 2+2\sqrt{-5} = 2(1+\sqrt{-5})$. Both $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ are common divisors: they divide $a$ because $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, and they divide $b$ visibly. Neither divides the other, since $N(2)=4$ and $N(1+\sqrt{-5})=6$ and neither of $4, 6$ divides the other.

Suppose $d$ were a gcd. Definition 6.16 asks two things of it, and both are used.

*Every common divisor divides $d$* — because $(d)$ is the smallest principal ideal containing $a$ and $b$, so $(d)\subseteq(c)$ for every common divisor $c$. Hence $2\mid d$ and $(1+\sqrt{-5})\mid d$, so $4\mid N(d)$ and $6\mid N(d)$, giving $12\mid N(d)$.

*$d$ is itself a common divisor* — so $d\mid 6$ and $d\mid b$, giving $N(d)\mid N(6)=36$ and $N(d)\mid N(b)=24$. Hence $N(d)\mid\gcd(36,24)=12$.

So $N(d)=12$ exactly. But no element has norm $12$: $a^2+5b^2=12$ forces $b^2\le 2$, and neither $b=0$ nor $b=\pm1$ leaves a perfect square. So no gcd exists.

Both of Remark 6.17's questions are therefore answered no. A gcd may fail to be a linear combination, and it may fail to exist. aa-12 restores both at once by restricting to PIDs, where every ideal $(a)+(b)$ is principal — and its generator is simultaneously a gcd and, by construction, a linear combination.
</details>
