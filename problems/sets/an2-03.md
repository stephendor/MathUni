**The one sentence this unit is about.** In ℝⁿ, compact means closed and bounded
(Corollary 3.5.5). That equivalence is a *fact about ℝⁿ* and it survives into no
other setting. Every generalisation below replaces "bounded" with a stronger,
uniform condition — **totally bounded** in a general complete space
(Theorem 3.5.13), **bounded and equicontinuous** in a space of continuous
functions (Theorem 4.8.9) — and the shape of the repair is the same both times.

**What Lindström proves and what he sets.** He *proves* Propositions 3.5.4,
3.5.7, 3.5.8, 3.5.9, 3.5.12, Corollary 3.5.5, Lemma 3.5.6, Theorem 3.5.10,
Theorem 3.5.13, Proposition 3.6.2, Lemma 3.6.3, Theorem 3.6.4,
Proposition 4.8.3, Lemma 4.8.5, Proposition 4.8.6, Corollary 4.8.7,
Lemma 4.8.8 and Theorem 4.8.9. He *sets as exercises* Proposition 3.5.1
(Problem 2 to §3.5), the minimum half of Theorem 3.5.10 (Problem 3), and
Corollary 3.6.5 (Problem 7 to §3.6). He also supplies his own negative control,
which is rarer than it should be: Theorem 3.5.13 needs the ambient space
complete, and he says so — "without this condition, the statement is false (see
Exercise 10)" — and Exercise 10 is the witness.

## Problem 1 (easy — the definition, and the trap in it)

(a) State **Definition 3.5.2**. Lindström adds a Remark: "It is easy to overlook
that the limit of the subsequence has to lie in the set $K$, but this is a
crucial part of the definition." Take him seriously — give a subset of
$\mathbb{R}$ in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence *in
$\mathbb{R}$* but which is not compact, and say which clause it fails.

(b) Prove **Proposition 3.5.1** (Lindström's Problem 2 to §3.5): if
$\{x_n\} \to a$ then so does every subsequence. Then prove
**Proposition 3.5.4**: every compact set is closed and bounded, stating
**Definition 3.5.3** first. Lindström argues contrapositively in two halves —
reproduce both and note which half uses the Triangle Inequality.

(c) State **Corollary 3.5.5** and prove it from the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem
(an-08's, quoted by Lindström as Theorem 2.3.3). Then read the corollary's
hypothesis out loud: *a subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$*. Give Lindström's Example 1 to
§3.5 and say which of "closed", "bounded", "complete" it has and which
conclusion it refuses.

(d) Prove **Lemma 3.5.6** (a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence
converges) and deduce **Proposition 3.5.7**: every compact metric space is
complete. Then say why the converse fails, using part (c)'s example, and connect
this to **Definition 3.4.3** from an2-02.

(e) Prove **Proposition 3.5.8** (a closed subset of a compact set is compact) and
**Proposition 3.5.9** (continuous images of compact sets are compact). Then
answer the question Lindström raises just before 3.5.9: *inverse* images of
compact sets need not be compact. Give an example. (Problem 8 to §3.5 asks for
one and also asks you to show the inverse image is at least closed.)

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the Extreme Value Theorem, which is what compactness is for)

(a) Prove **Theorem 3.5.10**, the Extreme Value Theorem, by Lindström's longer
route: put $M = \sup\{f(x) : x \in K\}$, allowing $M = \infty$ for now; take
$x_n$ with $f(x_n) \to M$; extract a convergent subsequence; and apply
**Proposition 3.2.9**. Then prove the minimum half, which is his Problem 3.

(b) Now give his shorter proof, from the Remark on p. 66: **Proposition 3.5.9**
makes $f(K)$ compact, hence closed and bounded by **Proposition 3.5.4**, hence
containing its own supremum and infimum. Say which of the two proofs you would
rather have if you also wanted to know *where* the maximum is, and why Lindström
says the longer one "gives a better feeling for what is going on".

(c) Problem 7 to §3.5: let $(X,d)$ be compact and $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$
continuous with $f(x) > 0$ for all $x$. Show there is a real $a > 0$ with
$f(x) > a$ everywhere. Then show by example that this fails on
$X = (0,1)$ — so the conclusion is not "positive continuous functions are
bounded away from zero" but "positive continuous functions *on a compact space*
are".

(d) Problem 15 to §3.5: for disjoint compact $C, K$ in a metric space, put
$a = \inf\{d(x,y) : x \in C, y \in K\}$. Show $a > 0$ and that it is *attained*
at some pair $(x_0, y_0)$. Then weaken one hypothesis — let $C$ be compact and
$K$ merely closed and disjoint from it — and separate the two conclusions:
one of them survives the weakening and one does not. Prove the survivor and
give a counterexample to the other. **Both halves matter**: a counterexample
built from two non-compact closed sets does not test this hypothesis at all.

Part (d) is the shape of argument this unit exists to install, so say in one
sentence what it is: *an infimum over a compact set is attained*. You will meet
it again in Problem 5.

(e) Problem 16 to §3.5, which is worth doing next to **Theorem 3.4.5** from
an2-02. Let $(X,d)$ be compact and $f : X \to X$ satisfy
$d(f(x),f(y)) < d(x,y)$ for all $x \neq y$ — note the *strict* inequality with no
uniform factor $s < 1$. Show $g(x) = d(x,f(x))$ is continuous with a minimum
point, and deduce that $f$ has a unique fixed point. Then say exactly what
compactness has bought: which hypothesis of Banach's theorem has been dropped,
and what has replaced it.

## Problem 3 (medium — the repair: total boundedness)

(a) State **Definition 3.5.11** and prove **Proposition 3.5.12**: every compact
set is totally bounded. Lindström argues contrapositively by constructing a
sequence with $d(x_n,x_m) \geq \epsilon$ for all $n \neq m$. Say why such a
sequence has no convergent subsequence, in one line, and note that you have seen
this sequence before — it is Example 1 to §3.5 in disguise.

(b) Problem 9 to §3.5: show that a totally bounded set is bounded, and give a
bounded set in some metric space that is not totally bounded. Then say precisely
what total boundedness adds: **Definition 3.5.3** asks for one number $M$;
**Definition 3.5.11** asks for a finite covering at *every* scale $\epsilon$.
Which of the two is a statement you could check by looking at one pair of points?

(c) Prove **Theorem 3.5.13**: a subset $A$ of a *complete* metric space $X$ is
compact if and only if it is closed and totally bounded. Lindström's proof of the
hard direction nests balls of radius $1, 1/2, 1/3, \dots$, each containing
infinitely many terms of the sequence, and reads a subsequence off the nesting.
Two things to identify: where completeness is used, and where closedness is used.
They are different lines and neither can be dropped.

(d) The negative control, and it is Lindström's own. He writes that
Theorem 3.5.13 needs $X$ complete and that "without this condition, the statement is
false (see Exercise 10)". Do Exercise 10: with the usual metric on $\mathbb{Q}$,
show that $A = \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : 0 \leq q \leq 2\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{Q}$
and totally bounded, but not compact. Then say which of the three conclusions of
Theorem 3.5.13 fails and why the ambient completeness was the load-bearing
hypothesis.

(e) Problem 11 to §3.5 — an argument with a hole in it, and finding the hole is
the exercise. A space is *locally compact* if every point has a compact closed
ball around it. Show $\mathbb{R}^n$ is locally compact; show
$X = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ with the usual metric is locally compact but not
complete; and then say what is wrong with this argument, which Lindström quotes
in order to have it refuted:

> We must show that all Cauchy sequences $\{x_n\}$ in $X$ converge. Since we can
> get the distance $d(x_n,x_m)$ as small as we want by choosing $n$ and $m$ large
> enough, we can find an $r > 0$ such that $\overline{B}(x_n, r)$ is compact, and
> $x_m \in \overline{B}(x_n,r)$ for all $m \geq n$. Hence $\{x_m\}_{m \geq n}$ has
> a subsequence converging to a point $a \in \overline{B}(x_n,r)$, and this
> sequence is also a subsequence of $\{x_n\}$. Thus the Cauchy sequence
> $\{x_n\}$ has a subsequence converging to $a$, and hence $\{x_n\}$ also
> converges to $a$.

Name the step that fails and the quantifier that has been reversed.

<details><summary>Partial</summary>

many bounded sets have bounded union. An infinite set with the discrete metric is
bounded (diameter 1) and not totally bounded: for $\epsilon < 1$ each ball is a
singleton, so no finite collection covers. Boundedness is a statement about one
scale and can be settled by exhibiting a single $M$; total boundedness is a
statement at every scale, with the finite count allowed to grow as $\epsilon$
shrinks — that uniformity across scales is the whole of the difference.

(d) The failing conclusion is compactness. $A$ is closed and totally bounded and
still has a sequence with no subsequence converging *in $A$*, so the equivalence
of Theorem 3.5.13 is broken. Completeness of the ambient space was carrying the
existence of the limit — exactly the role an2-02 gave it. Note that this is not a
statement about $A$ having a "hole" that closedness should have caught: closed is
relative, and $A$ *is* closed in the space it lives in.

(e) The failing step is "we can find an $r > 0$ such that $\overline B(x_n,r)$ is
compact, and $x_m \in \overline B(x_n,r)$ for all $m \geq n$". Local compactness
gives, for each point $a$, *some* $r_a > 0$ with $\overline B(a;r_a)$ compact —
the radius depends on the point, and as $x_n$ approaches the missing point 0 in
$\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ the available radii shrink to zero. The argument reads
the quantifiers as though a single $r$ served every point, which is the same
uniformity error as an2-02's Self-check 3.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the description that does not mention sequences)

Lindström opens §3.6 by saying the covering description "is also the right one to
use if one wants to extend the concept of compactness to even more general
spaces, *topological spaces*. In such spaces, sequences are not always an
efficient tool." This problem is about earning that sentence. (top-08 is the
syllabus unit that takes compactness up in a topological space; nothing here
assumes it.)

(a) State **Definition 3.6.1**, the open covering property, and prove
**Proposition 3.6.2**: a set with the open covering property is compact.
Lindström prefaces the proof with an observation — if no subsequence of
$\{x_n\}$ converges to $x$, then some ball $B(x;r)$ contains only finitely many
terms. Prove that observation, since the proposition is three lines once you
have it.

(b) Prove **Lemma 3.6.3**. Lindström defines
$$f(x) = \sup\{r \in \mathbb{R} : r < 1 \text{ and } B(x;r) \subseteq O \text{ for some } O \in \mathcal{O}\}$$
and notes in a parenthesis that "the strange cut-off at 1 in the definition of
$f(x)$ below is just to make sure that the function is finite". Say what would go
wrong without it, in the language of an2-01 Problem 2(d), and then prove the
continuity claim by establishing $|f(x) - f(y)| \leq d(x,y)$ — a Lipschitz bound,
so $\delta = \epsilon$.

(c) Prove **Theorem 3.6.4**: compact if and only if it has the open covering
property. The half you have not done uses **Theorem 3.5.10** applied to the
function of Lemma 3.6.3, and then **Proposition 3.5.12**. Trace the dependency:
the covering characterisation is proved *from* the sequential one, via the
Extreme Value Theorem and total boundedness. List those three ingredients in
order and say which of them would be unavailable in a space with no metric.

(d) **Corollary 3.6.5** and a note about the extract. For $K$ a subset of a
metric space $X$, the corollary states that the following are **equivalent**:
(i) $K$ is compact; (ii) if a collection $\mathcal{F}$ of closed sets has the
property that $K \cap F_1 \cap \dots \cap F_n \neq \varnothing$ for every finite
subcollection, then $K \cap \bigcap_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F \neq \varnothing$.
Prove (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii), following Lindström's hint in Problem 7: observe
that $K \cap \bigcap_{F} F \neq \varnothing$ fails exactly when $\{F^c\}$ is an
open covering of $K$. Then use it to do Problem 2 to §3.6: a decreasing sequence
of nonempty compact sets has nonempty intersection. Before you start, show
yourself why (i) cannot be dropped, with $K = \mathbb{R}$ and $F_n = [n,\infty)$.

**The note about the extract.** The repository's markdown of printed 70 prints
clause (ii) and no clause (i) — the line "(i) $K$ is compact" is simply missing,
while the label "(ii)" survives to prove that something was lost. The PDF page
carries both. This is the failure mode the folio discipline exists for, one level
down: an extraction that drops a hypothesis does not announce that it has, and
the surviving text reads as a complete statement. Whenever a numbered result in
