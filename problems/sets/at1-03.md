## Problem 1 (easy — path homotopy, and why the endpoints must be nailed down)

A homotopy of paths in $X$ is a family $f_t : I \to X$ such that (1) the
endpoints $f_t(0) = x_0$ and $f_t(1) = x_1$ are independent of $t$, and (2) the
associated map $F : I \times I \to X$, $F(s,t) = f_t(s)$, is continuous.

(a) Let $f_0, f_1$ be any two paths in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the same endpoints
$x_0$ and $x_1$. Verify in full that the linear homotopy
$f_t(s) = (1-t)f_0(s) + t f_1(s)$ satisfies both clauses. In particular check
that each $f_t$ really is a path from $x_0$ to $x_1$.
(b) Extend the conclusion: show that if $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is convex,
then all paths in $X$ with given endpoints are homotopic.
(c) Now drop clause (1). Show that in $X = S^1$ *any* two paths whatsoever are
homotopic if the endpoints are allowed to move, by exhibiting a homotopy from
an arbitrary path to a constant path. Conclude that clause (1) is not a
technical convenience but the entire content of the definition.
(d) Give an example of two paths in $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ with the
same endpoints that are *not* homotopic through paths with those endpoints.
You are not asked to prove non-homotopy — only to produce the example and say
which unit will supply the proof.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Example 1.1, p. 25)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), a path $f : I \to S^1$ can be pushed along itself: try
$f_t(s) = f((1-t)s)$ and see what happens at $t = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Everything in (a) reduces to the two clauses read literally. For the endpoints,
substitute $s = 0$ and $s = 1$ and use that $f_0, f_1$ share them. For
continuity, note that the formula is built from vector addition and scalar
multiplication, both continuous. In (c) the point is that the free homotopy you
build moves the endpoint, so it is not a homotopy of paths.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) At $s = 0$: $f_t(0) = (1-t)x_0 + tx_0 = x_0$, independent of $t$.
(c) $f_t(s) = f((1-t)s)$ is continuous on $I \times I$ and $f_1$ is the constant
path at $f(0)$ — but $f_t(1) = f(1-t)$ moves.
(d) The two semicircular paths in $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ from $(-1,0)$
to $(1,0)$, one through the upper half plane and one through the lower.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Clause (1).* Setting $s = 0$ gives
$f_t(0) = (1-t)f_0(0) + tf_1(0) = (1-t)x_0 + tx_0 = x_0$ for every $t$, since
$f_0(0) = f_1(0) = x_0$. The same computation at $s = 1$ gives
$f_t(1) = x_1$ for every $t$. So the endpoints are independent of $t$, and in
particular each $f_t$ is a path from $x_0$ to $x_1$.

from⟩

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the group axioms, via reparametrisation)

Hatcher's proof that $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ is a group runs entirely through one
device. A *reparametrisation* of a path $f$ is a composition $f\varphi$ where
$\varphi : I \to I$ is continuous with $\varphi(0) = 0$ and $\varphi(1) = 1$.

(a) Prove that reparametrising preserves the homotopy class: $f\varphi \simeq f$
via $f\varphi_t$ where $\varphi_t(s) = (1-t)\varphi(s) + ts$. Check in
particular that $\varphi_t(s) \in I$, so that the composition is defined at all.
(b) Show the product $[f][g] = [f \cdot g]$ is well defined — that is, that the
homotopy class of $f \cdot g$ depends only on the classes of $f$ and $g$.
(c) Prove associativity: given paths $f, g, h$ with $f(1) = g(0)$ and
$g(1) = h(0)$, exhibit the piecewise-linear $\varphi$ making
$f \cdot (g \cdot h)$ a reparametrisation of $(f \cdot g) \cdot h$, and write
down its formula explicitly. (Hatcher gives only the graph.)
(d) Prove that the class of the constant path at $x_0$ is a two-sided identity,
again by producing the two reparametrisation functions explicitly.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Proposition 1.3 and its proof, pp. 26–27)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$(f\cdot g)\cdot h$ traverses $f$ on $[0,1/4]$, $g$ on $[1/4,1/2]$, $h$ on
$[1/2,1]$. Where does $f\cdot(g\cdot h)$ traverse each?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a) the containment $\varphi_t(s) \in I$ holds because
$(1-t)\varphi(s) + ts$ is a convex combination of two elements of $I$. For (c),
write down where each of the three paths is traversed in each bracketing, then
$\varphi$ is the piecewise-linear map carrying one subdivision to the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) $f\cdot(g\cdot h)$ traverses $f$ on $[0,1/2]$, $g$ on $[1/2,3/4]$, $h$ on
$[3/4,1]$. So $\varphi$ must send $1/2 \mapsto 1/4$ and $3/4 \mapsto 1/2$,
linearly on each of $[0,1/2]$, $[1/2,3/4]$, $[3/4,1]$.
(d) For $f \cdot c \simeq f$ take $\varphi(s) = \min(2s, 1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) First, $\varphi_t(s) = (1-t)\varphi(s) + ts$ is a convex combination of
$\varphi(s) \in I$ and $s \in I$, hence lies in $I$; so $f\varphi_t$ is defined.
It is continuous in $(s,t)$ jointly, being built from continuous operations and
the continuous $f$ and $\varphi$. At the ends, $\varphi_t(0) = 0$ and
$\varphi_t(1) = 1$ for every $t$, so the endpoints of $f\varphi_t$ do not move —
clause (1) holds. Finally $\varphi_0 = \varphi$ and $\varphi_1 = \mathrm{id}$,
so $f\varphi_0 = f\varphi$ and $f\varphi_1 = f$. Hence $f\varphi \simeq f$.

(b) ⟨your step: if $f_0 \simeq f_1$ via $f_t$ and $g_0 \simeq g_1$ via $g_t$,
what is the homotopy from $f_0 \cdot g_0$ to $f_1 \cdot g_1$?⟩

(c) $(f\cdot g)\cdot h$ traverses $f$ on $[0, 1/4]$, $g$ on $[1/4, 1/2]$ and
$h$ on $[1/2, 1]$. ⟨your step: the other bracketing, then $\varphi$⟩

(d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the inverse, and Hatcher's "V")

The one group axiom reparametrisation does not deliver is inverses. For a path
$f$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$, the inverse path is $\overline{f}(s) = f(1-s)$.

(a) Hatcher's homotopy is $h_t = f_t \cdot g_t$, where $f_t$ equals $f$ on
$[0, 1-t]$ and is stationary at $f(1-t)$ on $[1-t, 1]$, and $g_t$ is the
inverse path of $f_t$. Write down $f_t$ as an explicit formula in $s$ and $t$,
and check it is continuous.
(b) Verify that $h_0 = f \cdot \overline{f}$ and that $h_1$ is the constant
path $c$ at $x_0$. (Hatcher writes the endpoint as $c \cdot \overline{c} = c$;
say why that product is the constant path and not something longer.)
(c) Hatcher's alternative description is in terms of $H : I \times I \to X$:
on the bottom edge $H$ is given by $f \cdot \overline{f}$; below the "V" let
$H(s,t)$ be independent of $t$; above the "V" let $H(s,t)$ be independent of
$s$. Draw the square, mark the "V", and explain why the two prescriptions
agree along the "V" itself — that is, why $H$ is well defined.
(d) Conclude that $[\overline{f}]$ is a two-sided inverse for $[f]$ in
$\pi_1(X, x_0)$, and state what the same argument gives for a path $f$ that is
not a loop.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Proposition 1.3, p. 26; its proof continues on p. 27)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$f_t(s) = f(s)$ for $s \leq 1-t$ and $f_t(s) = f(1-t)$ for $s \geq 1-t$. The
two branches agree at $s = 1-t$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The whole construction says: retract the path back along itself, and follow it
by the reverse of the retracted path. At time $t$ you go out only as far as
$f(1-t)$ and come straight back. For (c), the "V" is the locus where the
outward and return journeys meet; above it, nothing is left to traverse.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $f_t(s) = f\big(\min(s,\, 1-t)\big)$, continuous as a composition of $f$
with the continuous $\min$.
(b) At $t = 1$ the path $f_1$ is constant at $f(0) = x_0$, so
$h_1 = c \cdot \overline{c}$; the product of the constant path with itself
reversed is the constant path, since reparametrising a constant path gives a
constant path.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $f_t(s) = f(\min(s, 1-t))$. This is $f$ composed with
$(s,t) \mapsto \min(s, 1-t)$, which is continuous on $I \times I$ as the
minimum of two continuous functions; so $f_t$ is jointly continuous.
On $[0, 1-t]$ we get $f(s)$, and on $[1-t, 1]$ we get $f(1-t)$ — exactly the
description. ⟨your step: check the endpoints do not move as $t$ varies, so this
is legitimately a homotopy of paths⟩

(b) ⟨your step⟩ (c) ⟨your step⟩ (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — changing the basepoint)

For a path $h$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$, define
$\beta_h : \pi_1(X, x_1) \to \pi_1(X, x_0)$ by
$\beta_h[f] = [h \cdot f \cdot \overline{h}]$.

(a) Show $\beta_h$ is well defined: if $f_i$ is a homotopy of loops based at
$x_1$, then $h \cdot f_i \cdot \overline{h}$ is a homotopy of loops at $x_0$.
(b) Show $\beta_h$ is a homomorphism, filling in every step of Hatcher's
computation
$\beta_h[f\cdot g] = [h\cdot f\cdot g\cdot \overline{h}]
= [h\cdot f\cdot \overline{h}\cdot h\cdot g\cdot \overline{h}]
= \beta_h[f]\beta_h[g]$.
State precisely which earlier result licenses inserting
$\overline{h}\cdot h$ in the middle.
(c) Show $\beta_h$ is an isomorphism with inverse $\beta_{\overline{h}}$.
(d) Deduce that for path-connected $X$ the group $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ is independent
of basepoint up to isomorphism. Then answer the sharper question: is the
isomorphism *canonical*? Consider two paths $h, h'$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$ and
compute $\beta_{h'}^{-1}\beta_h$ as conjugation by an element of
$\pi_1(X, x_1)$, using aa-23's notion of an inner automorphism. Under what
condition on $\pi_1$ is the isomorphism independent of the chosen path?

Note that Dexter's notes set this up in the opposite direction, sending a loop
$\gamma$ at $x_0$ to $u^{-1}\cdot\gamma\cdot u$ for a path
$u : x_0 \leadsto x_1$. State the relation between the two conventions.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Proposition 1.5, p. 28; Dexter §2.4, the change-of-basepoint
Prop)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d), $\beta_{h'}^{-1}\beta_h = \beta_{\overline{h'}}\beta_h$, and
$\overline{h'} \cdot h$ is a loop at — which basepoint?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Parts (a)–(c) are Hatcher's proof written out slowly; the only subtlety is that
every equality is between *homotopy classes*, so each step needs a homotopy,
supplied by Proposition 1.3's axioms. For (d), the composite
$\beta_{\overline{h'}}\beta_h$ sends $[f]$ to
$[\overline{h'}\cdot h \cdot f \cdot \overline{h}\cdot h']$, which is
conjugation by the class of the loop $\overline{h'}\cdot h$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The insertion is licensed by the inverse axiom of Proposition 1.3:
$[\overline{h}\cdot h]$ is the class of the constant path at $x_1$, which is
the identity, so inserting it changes nothing in the homotopy class.
(d) The isomorphism is canonical exactly when $\pi_1$ is abelian, since then
every inner automorphism is the identity.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) The middle equality is the only one with content. In $\pi_1$-land we may
insert the identity anywhere: $[\overline{h} \cdot h]$ is the homotopy class of
a loop at $x_1$, and by the inverse axiom of Proposition 1.3 that class is the
identity element, the class of the constant path at $x_1$. So
$h\cdot f\cdot g\cdot \overline{h}$ and
$h\cdot f\cdot \overline{h}\cdot h\cdot g\cdot \overline{h}$ are homotopic rel
endpoints. Regrouping — legitimate by associativity, again Proposition 1.3 —
gives $(h\cdot f\cdot \overline{h})\cdot(h\cdot g\cdot \overline{h})$, whose
class is $\beta_h[f]\beta_h[g]$.

(a) ⟨your step⟩ (c) ⟨your step⟩ (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — simple connectivity, functoriality, and what π₁ is not yet)

(a) Prove Proposition 1.6: a space $X$ is simply-connected if and only if there
is a unique homotopy class of paths connecting any two points of $X$. Write out
both directions, and identify exactly where $\pi_1(X) = 0$ is used in the
forward direction.
(b) Dexter's notes record the functoriality of $\pi_1$: to a based map
$f : (X,x_0) \to (Y,y_0)$ there is an associated
$f_* = \pi_1(f) : \pi_1(X,x_0) \to \pi_1(Y,y_0)$, $[\gamma] \mapsto [f\circ\gamma]$,
satisfying (i) $\pi_1(f)$ is a group homomorphism, (ii) $f \simeq f'$ implies
$\pi_1(f) = \pi_1(f')$, (iii) $\pi_1(k\circ h) = \pi_1(k)\circ\pi_1(h)$, and
(iv) $\pi_1(\mathrm{id}_X) = \mathrm{id}$. Dexter leaves all four as an
exercise. Prove (i), (iii) and (iv).
(c) Prove (ii), being careful about basepoints: state the hypothesis you need
on the homotopy $f \simeq f'$ for the conclusion to hold as stated, and give
the reason. (This is the one clause where the based setting matters, and
Dexter's Defn of a based homotopy — a homotopy rel $\{x_0\}$ — is the relevant
definition.)
(d) Conclude that $\pi_1$ takes homotopy-equivalent based spaces to isomorphic
groups. Then state honestly what you have *not* proved: that $\pi_1(S^1)$ is
non-trivial. Cite the two results that will supply it — Theorem 1.7 in at1-04 —
and explain in one sentence why nothing in this unit could have given a
non-trivial example, quoting Hatcher's own remark on p. 28.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Proposition 1.6, p. 28, and the remark on the difficulty of
non-trivial examples, p. 28; Theorem 1.7, p. 29. Dexter §2.4, Defn (Based
space) and the functoriality Prop)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d): every technique in this unit produces homotopies. To show a group is
non-trivial you must show a homotopy *does not exist*, and no amount of
constructing homotopies does that.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) and (c) are short once you write $[\gamma] \mapsto [f\circ\gamma]$ and
recall that $f\circ(\gamma_0\cdot\gamma_1) = (f\circ\gamma_0)\cdot(f\circ\gamma_1)$
directly from the definition of the product path. For (c), an unbased homotopy
moves $f(x_0)$, so the two induced maps need not even have the same target
basepoint — which is why the based condition is needed.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Forward: given $f, g$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$, we have
$f \simeq f\cdot\overline{g}\cdot g \simeq g$, the first step using that
$\overline{g}\cdot g$ is homotopic to a constant and the second that
$f\cdot\overline{g}$ is a loop at $x_0$, hence nullhomotopic since
$\pi_1(X,x_0)=0$.
(d) Hatcher: "It is not so easy to show that a space has a nontrivial
fundamental group since one must somehow demonstrate the nonexistence of
homotopies between certain loops."
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b)(i) Let $\gamma_0, \gamma_1$ be loops at $x_0$. Directly from the definition
of the product path,
$$f\circ(\gamma_0\cdot\gamma_1)(s) = \begin{cases}
f(\gamma_0(2s)), & 0 \le s \le 1/2\\ f(\gamma_1(2s-1)), & 1/2 \le s \le 1
\end{cases}$$
which is exactly $(f\circ\gamma_0)\cdot(f\circ\gamma_1)$. Hence
$f_*([\gamma_0][\gamma_1]) = f_*[\gamma_0\cdot\gamma_1]
= [(f\circ\gamma_0)\cdot(f\circ\gamma_1)] = f_*[\gamma_0]\,f_*[\gamma_1]$.
⟨your step: also check $f_*$ is well defined on homotopy classes, which (i)
tacitly assumes⟩

(a) ⟨your step⟩ (c) ⟨your step⟩ (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>
