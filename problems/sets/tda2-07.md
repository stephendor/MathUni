## Problem 1 (easy — maps of covers, and the theorem that is not available)

(a) Define a *map of covers* $\xi : \mathcal{U} \to \mathcal{V}$ for two covers of
the same space $X$. State Proposition 9.1 and prove it.
(b) State Proposition 9.2 and prove it. Then explain why, together with the fact
that contiguous simplicial maps induce identical maps on homology, it lets one
call the induced map $\mathsf{H}_*(N(\mathcal{U})) \to \mathsf{H}_*(N(\mathcal{V}))$
*canonical*.
(c) State the Nerve Theorem in the form Dey and Wang quote it from Theorem 2.1:
the hypothesis on the cover and the conclusion about $N(\mathcal{U})$ and $X$.
(d) Immediately after quoting it, Dey and Wang say the hypothesis is not
available in general. Say what they replace it with, and what they warn can fail
without it — quote the dimension in which their counterexample lives.

*(Dey §9.1, maps of covers and Proposition 9.1 p. 257, Proposition 9.2 p. 258,
the Nerve Theorem quoted at pp. 256 and 259, Figure 9.3's failure of surjectivity
in $\mathsf{H}_2$ p. 259)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a) and (b), everything follows from one inclusion:
$U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\xi(\alpha)}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
To show a vertex map is simplicial, take $\sigma \in N(\mathcal{U})$ and show the
corresponding intersection downstairs is nonempty.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For (b), the two maps $N(\zeta)$ and $N(\xi)$ are contiguous when
$\zeta(\sigma) \cup \xi(\sigma)$ spans a simplex of $N(\mathcal{V})$ for every
$\sigma \in N(\mathcal{U})$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For covers $\mathcal{U} = \{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ and
$\mathcal{V} = \{V_\beta\}_{\beta \in B}$ of $X$, a map of covers is a set map
$\xi : A \to B$ with $U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\xi(\alpha)}$ for every $\alpha$.
Proposition 9.1: such a $\xi$ induces a simplicial map
$N(\xi) : N(\mathcal{U}) \to N(\mathcal{V})$ given on vertices by $\xi$. Proof:
take $\sigma \in N(\mathcal{U})$; then
$\bigcap_{\beta \in \xi(\sigma)} V_\beta = \bigcap_{\alpha \in \sigma} V_{\xi(\alpha)} \supseteq \bigcap_{\alpha \in \sigma} U_\alpha \neq \varnothing$,
the last step because $\sigma$ is a simplex of $N(\mathcal{U})$. So
$\xi(\sigma) \in N(\mathcal{V})$.
(b) Proposition 9.2: any two maps of covers
$\zeta, \xi : \mathcal{U} \to \mathcal{V}$ induce contiguous simplicial maps.
Proof: for each $\alpha$, $U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\zeta(\alpha)}$ and
$U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\xi(\alpha)}$, so
$U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\zeta(\alpha)} \cap V_{\xi(\alpha)}$. Hence for
$\sigma \in N(\mathcal{U})$,
$\bigcap_{\beta \in \zeta(\sigma) \cup \xi(\sigma)} V_\beta = \bigcap_{\alpha \in \sigma}(V_{\zeta(\alpha)} \cap V_{\xi(\alpha)}) \supseteq \bigcap_{\alpha \in \sigma} U_\alpha \neq \varnothing$,
so $\zeta(\sigma) \cup \xi(\sigma)$ spans a simplex of $N(\mathcal{V})$.
Contiguous maps induce identical maps on homology, so whichever map of covers one
picks, the induced homomorphism on homology is the same — it depends on the pair
of covers, not on the choice, which is what "canonical" means here.
(c) The Nerve Theorem, as quoted: if every intersection of cover elements in
$\mathcal{U}$ is contractible, then the nerve $N(\mathcal{U})$ is homotopy
equivalent to the space $X = \bigcup \mathcal{U}$; equivalently the nerve map
$\phi_\mathcal{U}$ is a homotopy equivalence and $\phi_{\mathcal{U}*}$ an
isomorphism on all homology. Dey and Wang add that contractibility can be weakened
to a homology-ball condition and still keep the isomorphism.
(d) They say we cannot hope for such a good cover all the time, and replace the
hypothesis by the far weaker one that every cover element is *path connected*.
Without a good cover, $\phi_{\mathcal{U}*}$ need be neither injective nor
surjective; their Figure 9.3 — a map $\mathbb{S}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ whose
pullback band around the equator pinches the nerve into two 2-cycles — shows
failure of surjectivity in $\mathsf{H}_2$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — what a merely path connected cover still buys)

(a) Describe the blow-up space $X_\mathcal{U}$ and the three maps
$\zeta : X \to X_\mathcal{U}$, $\pi : X_\mathcal{U} \to |N(\mathcal{U})|$ and
$\phi_\mathcal{U} = \pi \circ \zeta$. What is $\phi_\mathcal{U}$ called?
(b) State Definition 9.1 and Definition 9.2, and Fact 9.1 and Fact 9.2. Which
hypothesis on $X$ is Fact 9.1 buying, and what is it used for?
(c) State Proposition 9.3, Proposition 9.4 and Theorem 9.5. Explain the role
path connectedness of the cover plays in the proof of Proposition 9.4 — name the
one step that would break without it.
(d) State Proposition 9.6, Corollary 9.7, Proposition 9.8 and Theorem 9.9, and
give the three-line proof of Theorem 9.9. Then state the persistence reading Dey
and Wang draw: which of "born" and "died" is possible for a one-dimensional class
passing along a tower of nerves?

*(Dey §9.1, the blow-up space pp. 259–261, Definition 9.1 and Definition 9.2
p. 260, Fact 9.1 and Fact 9.2 p. 261, Proposition 9.3 p. 261, Proposition 9.4
and Theorem 9.5 p. 262, Proposition 9.6 p. 263, Claim 9.1 and Corollary 9.7 and
Proposition 9.8 p. 264, Theorem 9.9 p. 265)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The nerve map is $\phi_\mathcal{U}(x) = \sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha(x) u_\alpha$ —
a partition of unity read as barycentric coordinates.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the cycle upstairs is built by alternating edge paths $e_i \times x_i$
with vertex paths inside a single lifted cover element.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Theorem 9.9 follows from $\tau_* \circ \bar\phi_{\mathcal{U}*} = \bar\phi_{\mathcal{V}*}$
plus surjectivity of $\bar\phi_{\mathcal{V}*}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For each nonempty finite intersection
$U_{\alpha_0 \ldots \alpha_n} = \bigcap_i U_{\alpha_i}$ form the product
$U_{\alpha_0 \ldots \alpha_n} \times \Delta^n_{\alpha_0 \ldots \alpha_n}$, take
the disjoint union over all such tuples, and glue by the face identifications
coming from the inclusions
$U_{\alpha_0 \ldots \alpha_n} \subset U_{\alpha_0 \ldots \widehat{\alpha_i} \ldots \alpha_n}$;
the quotient is $X_\mathcal{U}$. With a partition of unity
$\{\varphi_\alpha\}$ subordinate to $\mathcal{U}$, set
$\zeta(x) = \sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha(x)x_\alpha$; let $\pi$ be induced by the
projections onto the simplex factors; then
$\phi_\mathcal{U} = \pi \circ \zeta$ satisfies
$\phi_\mathcal{U}(x) = \sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha(x)u_\alpha$ and is called the
*nerve map*.
(b) Definition 9.1: $\mathcal{U}$ is a *refinement* of $\mathcal{V}$ if every
$U_\alpha$ is contained in some $V_\beta$; $\mathcal{U}$ is *locally finite* if
every point has a neighbourhood meeting only finitely many elements of
$\mathcal{U}$. Definition 9.2: $\{\varphi_\alpha : X \to [0,1]\}$ is a *partition
of unity* if $\sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha(x) = 1$ for all $x$ and only finitely
many $\varphi_\alpha(x)$ are positive at each $x$; it is *subordinate* to
$\mathcal{U}$ if $\mathrm{supp}(\varphi_\alpha) \subseteq U_\alpha$. Fact 9.1:
every open cover of a compact space admits a subordinate partition of unity.
Fact 9.2: the map $\zeta$ is a homotopy equivalence. Fact 9.1 is what compactness
of $X$ is assumed for; without a partition of unity the nerve map
$\phi_\mathcal{U}$ cannot be written down at all.
(c) Proposition 9.3: every 1-cycle $\gamma$ in $|N(\mathcal{U})|$ has a 1-cycle
$\gamma'$ in $N(\mathcal{U})$ with $[\gamma] = [|\gamma'|]$. Proposition 9.4: if
$\mathcal{U}$ is path connected then
$\phi_{\mathcal{U}*} : \mathsf{H}_1(X) \to \mathsf{H}_1(|N(\mathcal{U})|)$ is a
surjection. Theorem 9.5: if $\mathcal{U}$ is path connected then
$\tilde\phi_{\mathcal{U}*} : \mathsf{H}_1(X) \to \mathsf{H}_1(N(\mathcal{U}))$ is
a surjection. In the proof of Proposition 9.4 one lifts an edge cycle
$e_0, \ldots, e_{r-1}$ of $N(\mathcal{U})$ to $X_\mathcal{U}$ by choosing points
$x_i \in U_{\alpha_i} \cap U_{\alpha_{i+1}}$ and joining consecutive lifts
$x_{\alpha_i} \rightsquigarrow x_{\alpha_{i+1}}$ *inside the lifted
$U_{\alpha_i}$*. That vertex path exists precisely because $U_{\alpha_i}$ is path
connected; it is the one step that fails otherwise. The rest is formal: $\zeta_*$
is an isomorphism by Fact 9.2, and $\phi_{\mathcal{U}*} = \pi_* \circ \zeta_*$.
(d) Proposition 9.6: given a cover map $\theta : \mathcal{U} \to \mathcal{V}$ of
covers of a compact $X$, there is a partition of unity $\{\varphi_\alpha\}$
subordinate to $\mathcal{U}$ whose pushforward
$\psi_\beta = \sum_{\alpha \in \theta^{-1}(\beta)} \varphi_\alpha$ (and $0$ off
the image) is a partition of unity subordinate to $\mathcal{V}$ — *coherent*
partitions of unity. Claim 9.1 then gives
$\hat\tau(\phi_\mathcal{U}(x)) = \phi_\mathcal{V}(x)$, whence Corollary 9.7:
$\phi_{\mathcal{V}*} = \hat\tau_* \circ \phi_{\mathcal{U}*}$; and after passing
to simplicial homology, Proposition 9.8:
$\bar\phi_{\mathcal{V}*} = \tau_* \circ \bar\phi_{\mathcal{U}*}$. Theorem 9.9:
if $\tau : N(\mathcal{U}) \to N(\mathcal{V})$ is induced by a cover map between
path connected covers, then
$\tau_* : \mathsf{H}_1(N(\mathcal{U})) \to \mathsf{H}_1(N(\mathcal{V}))$ is a
surjection. Proof: by Proposition 9.8,
$\tau_* \circ \bar\phi_{\mathcal{U}*} = \bar\phi_{\mathcal{V}*}$; by Theorem 9.5
the right-hand side is surjective; a composite that is surjective forces the last
map to be surjective. The persistence reading: along a tower of nerves induced by
cover maps, one-dimensional classes can die, but they cannot be born.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — mapper) [interleaves lab-08 and tda2-06]

(a) Given $f : X \to Z$ continuous and a cover $\mathcal{U}$ of $Z$, define the
pullback cover $f^*\mathcal{U}$. Why is every element of it path connected, and
what does that fact let you import from Problem 2?
(b) What does it mean for $f$ to be *well behaved*, and which pathology is the
condition ruling out? State Definition 9.4.
(c) State Remark 9.1's two choices of cover of $\mathbb{R}$ and the structure each
produces. Which unit did you meet those two structures in?
(d) Describe the mapper construction for a point cloud: the graph $G^r(P)$, the
cover induced by a set of intervals, and the nerve taken at the end. What does
Dey and Wang's observation about intervals $U_i = (i - \varepsilon, i + \varepsilon)$
with $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$ say the output approximates, and what happens in the
limit?

*(Dey §9.3, the pullback cover and well-behavedness p. 268, Definition 9.4 and
Remark 9.1 p. 269, mapper for point cloud data p. 270)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The pullback cover is not $\{f^{-1}(U_\alpha)\}$ — it is the collection of path
connected *components* of those pre-images.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), one choice gives sublevel sets and one gives thickened level sets.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Well behaved: for every path connected open $U \subseteq Z$, $f^{-1}(U)$ has
finitely many open path connected components.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Since $f$ is continuous, $\{f^{-1}(U_\alpha)\}$ is an open cover of $X$;
decompose each $f^{-1}(U_\alpha) = \bigcup_{i=1}^{j_\alpha} V_{\alpha,i}$ into its
path connected components and let $f^*\mathcal{U}$ be the collection of all the
$V_{\alpha,i}$. By construction every element of $f^*\mathcal{U}$ is path
connected — it is a path connected component of something. That is exactly the
hypothesis of Theorem 9.5 and Theorem 9.9, so both apply to any mapper, with no
extra assumption on $f$ or $\mathcal{U}$ beyond well-behavedness.
(b) There are $f$ for which some $f^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ shatters into infinitely many
path components; to exclude that, $f$ is called *well behaved* if for every path
connected open $U \subseteq Z$ the pre-image $f^{-1}(U)$ has finitely many open
path connected components, so all nerves of pullbacks of finite covers are finite.
Definition 9.4: for topological spaces $X$ and $Z$, a well-behaved continuous
$f : X \to Z$, and a finite open cover $\mathcal{U}$ of $Z$, the *mapper* is the
nerve of the pullback cover, $\mathrm{M}(\mathcal{U}, f) := N(f^*(\mathcal{U}))$.
(c) Remark 9.1, with $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ and an infinite cover allowed:
$U_\alpha = (-\infty, \alpha)$ for $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ gives sublevel sets,
which lead to merge trees; $U_\alpha = (\alpha - \varepsilon, \alpha + \varepsilon)$
for fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ gives $\varepsilon$-thick level sets, which induce a
relaxed notion of Reeb graph. Both structures are tda2-06's — the merge tree is
its Definition 7.2 and the Reeb graph its Definition 7.1 — so mapper subsumes
them as cover choices.
(d) For a finite metric space $(P, \mathsf{d}_P)$ and $r \geq 0$, let $G^r(P)$
have $P$ as vertices and an edge $(p,p')$ whenever
$\mathsf{d}_P(p,p') \leq r$. For $f : P \to \mathbb{R}$ and a set $\mathcal{U}$ of
intervals covering $\mathbb{R}$, put $P_{(a,b)} = f^{-1}((a,b))$ and split it as
$\bigsqcup_i P^i_{(a,b)}$, where each $P^i_{(a,b)}$ is the vertex set of a
connected component of the subgraph of $G^r(P)$ spanned by $P_{(a,b)}$. These
vertex sets over all intervals form a cover of $P$, and the mapper
$\mathbf{M}(P,f)$ is its nerve, intersections being intersections of finite sets.
Taking $U_i = (i-\varepsilon, i+\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$, so
that only consecutive intervals overlap, yields a discretised approximation of the
Reeb graph of the function that $f$ approximates on the sample $P$; in the limit
in which each interval degenerates to a point, the discretised Reeb graph
converges to the Reeb graph.
</details>

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>

</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), recall that two maps of covers with the same source and target induce

</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Corollary 9.19 concludes that the bottleneck distance between
$\mathrm{Dgm}_p\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f)$ and

</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Proposition 9.13: for $f : X \to Z$ and covers $\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{V}$ of
$Z$ with a cover map $\xi : \mathcal{U} \to \mathcal{V}$, there is an induced
cover map $f^*(\xi) : f^*(\mathcal{U}) \to f^*(\mathcal{V})$. Proof: if
$U_\alpha \subseteq V_{\xi(\alpha)}$ then
$f^{-1}(U_\alpha) \subseteq f^{-1}(V_{\xi(\alpha)})$, so each path connected
component $\widehat U_{\alpha,i}$ of $f^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ is contained in exactly
one path connected component $\widehat V_{\xi(\alpha),j}$ of
$f^{-1}(V_{\xi(\alpha)})$ — connected sets cannot straddle two components — and
$f^*(\xi)$ sends $\widehat U_{\alpha,i}$ to that unique component. Composition is
respected, $f^*(\zeta \circ \xi) = f^*(\zeta) \circ f^*(\xi)$, so applying this
along a whole tower gives Proposition 9.14: if $\mathfrak{U}$ is a cover tower of
$Z$ and $f : X \to Z$ is continuous, then $f^*(\mathfrak{U})$ is a cover tower of
$X$.
(b) Definition 9.5: for $f : X \to Z$ continuous and $\mathfrak{U}$ a cover tower
of $Z$, the *multiscale mapper* is the simplicial tower
$\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U}, f) := N(f^*(\mathfrak{U}))$, with simplicial maps
induced by the cover maps. For $\mathrm{res}(\mathfrak{U}) \leq a_1 < \cdots < a_n$
it reads
$N(f^*(\mathfrak{U}_{a_1})) \to \cdots \to N(f^*(\mathfrak{U}_{a_n}))$, and
applying $\mathsf{H}_p$ with field coefficients gives
$\mathsf{H}_p(N(f^*(\mathfrak{U}_{a_1}))) \to \cdots \to \mathsf{H}_p(N(f^*(\mathfrak{U}_{a_n})))$.
Finiteness of the covers and well-behavedness of $f$ make every nerve finite,
hence every homology group finite dimensional, so the module is pointwise finite
dimensional and has a persistence diagram
$\mathrm{Dgm}_p\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U}, f)$.
(c) Definition 9.7: cover towers $\mathfrak{U}, \mathfrak{V}$ of $X$ with the same
resolution $r$ are *$\eta$-interleaved* if there are cover maps
$\zeta_a : \mathfrak{U}_a \to \mathfrak{V}_{a+\eta}$ and
$\xi_{a'} : \mathfrak{V}_{a'} \to \mathfrak{U}_{a'+\eta}$ for all $a, a' \geq r$;
replacing $+$ by $\cdot$ gives *multiplicatively $\eta$-interleaved*. Nothing is
required to commute. Proposition 9.2 repairs it: the two composites
$\xi_{a+\eta} \circ \zeta_a$ and $u_{a,a+2\eta}$ are both maps of covers from
$\mathfrak{U}_a$ to $\mathfrak{U}_{a+2\eta}$, so the simplicial maps they induce
are contiguous, hence equal on homology — which is all the interleaving of
simplicial towers actually needs.
(d) Proposition 9.17: (a) interleaving composes, $\eta_1$ then $\eta_2$ giving
$\eta_1 + \eta_2$ (multiplicatively $\eta_1\eta_2$); (b) pullback preserves it —
if $\mathfrak{U}, \mathfrak{V}$ are $\eta$-interleaved cover towers of $Z$ then
$f^*(\mathfrak{U})$ and $f^*(\mathfrak{V})$ are $\eta$-interleaved. Proposition
9.18: $\eta$-interleaved cover towers of $X$ with equal resolution have
$\eta$-interleaved nerve towers $N(\mathfrak{U})$, $N(\mathfrak{V})$.
Corollary 9.19: for $\eta \geq 0$ and finite cover towers $\mathfrak{U},
\mathfrak{V}$ of $Z$ with equal positive resolution, $f$ well behaved and the
towers $\eta$-interleaved, $\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f)$ and
$\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{V},f)$ are $\eta$-interleaved, and the bottleneck distance
between $\mathrm{Dgm}_p\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f)$ and
$\mathrm{Dgm}_p\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{V},f)$ is at most $\eta$ for all $p$. The
stability is in the *cover tower*: $f$ is held fixed and only the lens is
perturbed. Dey and Wang note immediately that this says nothing about perturbing
$f$, which is what §9.4.2 is for.
</details>

Proposition 9.21 pp. 277–278, Corollary 9.22 p. 278; §9.4.3, Definition 9.10 and

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$(c,s)$-good asks two things of every scale: cover elements are not too big, and
the cover $c$ scales up has a Lebesgue number at least $\varepsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), Claim 9.2 needs only the two clauses of Definition 9.8 read against each
other: diameter at most $\varepsilon$ on one side, Lebesgue number at least
$\varepsilon$ on the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Theorem 9.23 gives multiplicative $2c$-interleaving with the intrinsic Čech
filtration, and Corollary 9.24 turns that into a bottleneck bound of $2c$ at log
scale.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) From §9.2: the size of $X' \subseteq X$ is its diameter
$s(X') = \sup_{x,x' \in X'} \mathsf{d}(x,x')$, the size of a class is the infimum
of the sizes of its cycles, an *optimal cycle basis* minimises the sum of the
sizes (Definition 9.3); $s_{max}(\mathcal{U}) = \max_{U \in \mathcal{U}} s(U)$;
and $\lambda(\mathcal{U})$, the Lebesgue number, is the largest real such that any
subset of size at most $\lambda(\mathcal{U})$ lies in some element of
$\mathcal{U}$. Definition 9.8: a cover tower
$\mathfrak{U} = \{\mathfrak{U}_\varepsilon\}_{\varepsilon \geq s > 0}$ is
*$(c,s)$-good* if for every $\varepsilon \geq s > 0$, (i)
$s_{max}(\mathfrak{U}_\varepsilon) \leq \varepsilon$ and (ii)
$\lambda(\mathfrak{U}_{c\varepsilon}) \geq \varepsilon$. For the example, a ball
of radius $\varepsilon/2$ has diameter at most $\varepsilon$, giving (i); and at
index $2\varepsilon$ the balls have radius $\varepsilon$, so any set of size at
most $\varepsilon$ lies inside one of them, giving
$\lambda(\mathfrak{U}_{2\varepsilon}) \geq \varepsilon$, which is (ii) with
$c = 2$.
(b) Theorem 9.20: for $f : X \to Z$ and two $(c,s)$-good cover towers
$\mathfrak{U}, \mathfrak{V}$ of $Z$, the multiscale mappers
$\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f)$ and $\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{V},f)$ are
multiplicatively $c$-interleaved. Claim 9.2: any two $(c,s)$-good cover towers are
multiplicatively $c$-interleaved. Proof of the claim: for $U \in
\mathfrak{U}_\varepsilon$, clause (i) gives $\mathrm{diam}(U) \leq \varepsilon$;
clause (ii) for $\mathfrak{V}$ gives
$\lambda(\mathfrak{V}_{c\varepsilon}) \geq \varepsilon$, so $U$ lies in some
$V \in \mathfrak{V}_{c\varepsilon}$; set $\zeta_\varepsilon(U) = V$, choosing
arbitrarily among ties. The symmetric construction gives
$\xi_{\varepsilon'} : \mathfrak{V}_{\varepsilon'} \to \mathfrak{U}_{c\varepsilon'}$.
Theorem 9.20 then follows: Proposition 9.17(b) pushes the $c$-interleaving through
the pullback to $f^*(\mathfrak{U})$ and $f^*(\mathfrak{V})$, and Proposition 9.18
pushes it through the nerve to the two multiscale mappers.
(c) Definition 9.9: the *$\varepsilon_0$-truncation* of $\mathfrak{U}$ is
$\mathrm{Tr}_{\varepsilon_0}(\mathfrak{U}) = \{\mathfrak{U}_\varepsilon\}_{\varepsilon_0 \leq \varepsilon}$,
of resolution $\varepsilon_0$. Proposition 9.21: for $X$ compact, $(Z,d_Z)$
compact path connected, $f,g : X \to Z$ continuous with
$\delta = \max_x \mathsf{d}_Z(f(x),g(x))$, and $\mathfrak{W}$ any $(c,s)$-good
cover tower of $Z$, the $\varepsilon_0$-truncations of $f^*(\mathfrak{W})$ and
$g^*(\mathfrak{W})$ with $\varepsilon_0 = \max(1,s)$ are multiplicatively

with $f, g$ well behaved,
$\mathsf{d}_b(\mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{W},f), \mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{W},g)) \leq \log(2c\max(s,\delta)+c) + \max(0,\log(1/s))$.
The extra term is the price of the truncation: Proposition 9.21 controls the
towers only from index $\max(1,s)$ upwards, so when $s < 1$ the missing initial
stretch can introduce bars of length at most $\log(1/s)$ at log scale, or stretch
existing ones leftwards by at most that much.
(d) Definition 9.10: the *intrinsic Čech complex* $\mathbb{C}^r(Y)$ of a
(pseudo)metric space at scale $r$ is the nerve of the intrinsic $r$-balls
$\{B(y;r)\}_{y \in Y}$. Definition 9.11: the *intrinsic Čech filtration* is
$\mathcal{C}(Y) = \{\mathbb{C}^r(Y) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}^{r'}(Y)\}_{0 < r < r'}$,
and $\mathcal{C}_s(Y)$ is the same from resolution $s$. Theorem 9.23: for
$\mathcal{C}_s(X)$ the intrinsic Čech filtration of $(X, \mathsf{d}_f)$ and
$\mathfrak{U}$ a $(c,s)$-good cover tower of the compact connected metric space
$Z$, $\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f)$ and $\mathcal{C}_s(X)$ are multiplicatively
$2c$-interleaved. Corollary 9.24:
$\mathsf{d}_b(\mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{U},f), \mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathcal{C}_s) \leq 2c$.
Definition 9.13: for a graph $G$ and $O \subseteq V(G)$, the *$G$-induced
connected components* $\mathrm{cc}_G(O)$ partition $O$ into maximal vertex sets
connected in the subgraph $G_O$ spanned by $O$. Definition 9.14: the *$G$-induced
mapper* is $\mathrm{M}_G(\mathcal{W},f) = N(f_G^*(\mathcal{W}))$, and the
$G$-induced multiscale mapper is the corresponding tower; taking $G = K^1$, the
1-skeleton, and $f_V$ the restriction of $f$ to vertices gives the *combinatorial
multiscale mapper*. Theorem 9.28: for $(Z,\mathsf{d}_Z)$ compact connected,
$f : |K| \to Z$, and a $(c,s)$-good cover tower $\mathfrak{W}$ with
$(K,f,\mathfrak{W})$ satisfying the minimum diameter condition (Condition 9.1),
$\mathsf{d}_b(\mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathrm{MM}(\mathfrak{W},f), \mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathrm{MM}_{K^1}(\mathfrak{W},f_V)) \leq 3\log(3c) + 3\max(0,\log(1/s))$.
For a practitioner: nothing here applies to an arbitrary cover. Every theorem
after Corollary 9.19 requires the tower to be $(c,s)$-good, which is a checkable
property of the cover one designs — balls of radius $\varepsilon/2$ give $c = 2$
— and $c$ is then the error bar. Perturbing the lens: Corollary 9.19 bounds the
bottleneck distance by the interleaving parameter $\eta$ of the two cover towers.
Perturbing the map: Corollary 9.22 bounds it by
$\log(2c\max(s,\delta)+c) + \max(0,\log(1/s))$ where $\delta$ is the sup-norm
change in $f$. Comparing to a filtration one might have built instead:
Corollary 9.24 bounds it by $2c$. And Theorem 9.28 is the cost of computing only
on the 1-skeleton — a factor of $3$ on the bound, because the towers interleave in
the weaker sense of Definition 6.6 rather than the strong one, plus the extra
hypothesis Condition 9.1 relating the cover's minimum diameter to the diameters of
$f(\sigma)$. What is given up is exactness: the combinatorial mapper approximates
the multiscale mapper rather than computing it.
