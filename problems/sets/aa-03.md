## Problem 1 (easy — congruence is an equivalence relation, and $n = 0$ is allowed)

(a) Prove directly from Definition 2.1 (p. 23) that congruence mod $n$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, for every integer $n \ge 0$.

(b) Aluffi's definition permits $n = 0$. Describe the congruence classes mod $0$ and the set $\mathbb{Z}/0\mathbb{Z}$ explicitly, and check your description against Definition 2.5 (p. 25).

(c) Theorem 2.7 (p. 26) says $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly $n$ elements. Why does that not contradict (b)?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 2.1 says $a \equiv b \bmod n$ means $b - a \in n\mathbb{Z}$. For (b), what is $0\mathbb{Z}$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Each of the three properties is a one-line statement about the set $n\mathbb{Z}$: that it contains $0$, that it is closed under negation, and that it is closed under addition. Prove those three and the rest is substitution.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Reflexive: $a - a = 0 = n\cdot 0 \in n\mathbb{Z}$. Symmetric: if $b - a = nk$ then $a - b = n(-k)$. Transitive: if $b - a = nk$ and $c - b = n\ell$ then $c - a = n(k+\ell)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $0\mathbb{Z} = \{0\}$, so $a \equiv b \bmod 0$ means $b - a = 0$, i.e. $a = b$. Congruence mod $0$ is equality, every class $[a]_0 = \{a\}$ is a singleton, and $\mathbb{Z}/0\mathbb{Z}$ is in bijection with $\mathbb{Z}$ itself — infinite.

(c) Theorem 2.7 (p. 26) is stated for **positive** $n$. Its proof runs through Lemma 2.6 (p. 25), which invokes division with remainder — Theorem 1.3, p. 6 — and that theorem requires $b \ne 0$. The hypothesis $n > 0$ is not decoration: it is the exact point at which the argument would otherwise have nothing to divide by.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — every equivalence relation is "same value under some function")

Let $f : A \to B$ be any function, and define $a \sim a'$ to mean $f(a) = f(a')$.

(a) Show $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $A$.

(b) Show the quotient $A/\sim$ is in bijection with the image $f(A)$.

(c) Now go the other way: given an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $A$, produce a set $B$ and a function $f$ for which $\sim$ is exactly "same value under $f$". Conclude that (a) loses no generality.

(d) Identify the $f$ that produces congruence mod $n$ for $n > 0$, and say what its image is.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c) the set you want is the quotient itself. For (d), Lemma 2.6 (p. 25) tells you which representative to send $a$ to.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Send the class of $a$ to $f(a)$; check this does not depend on the representative chosen, then check injectivity and surjectivity onto $f(A)$. (c) Take $B = A/\sim$ and $f$ the map sending each element to its own class.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) is immediate from the three properties of equality: $f(a) = f(a)$; $f(a) = f(a')$ gives $f(a') = f(a)$; and two equalities chain. This is the sense in which equality is the prototype every equivalence relation imitates (§2.1, p. 22).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Define $\bar f([a]) = f(a)$. Well defined: if $[a] = [a']$ then $a \sim a'$, which *means* $f(a) = f(a')$ — here the definition of $\sim$ does the work that in general requires a proof. Injective: $\bar f([a]) = \bar f([a'])$ says $f(a) = f(a')$, i.e. $a \sim a'$, i.e. $[a] = [a']$. Surjective onto $f(A)$ by construction.

(d) Take $f(a) = $ the remainder of $a$ on division by $n$, a function $\mathbb{Z} \to \{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$ by Theorem 1.3 (p. 6). Lemma 2.6 says $[a]_n = [r]_n$, and its second half says the $n$ classes listed are distinct — so $f$ is onto that set of $n$ values, and (b) recovers Theorem 2.7 (§2.2, p. 26) as a special case. Well-definedness is free here and will not be free again: from aa-09 onwards it is the standing obligation every quotient construction owes.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the three axioms are independent)

Give, for each of the following, a relation on $\mathbb{Z}$ (or on a set you specify) with exactly the stated properties, and prove your claims:

(a) reflexive and symmetric but not transitive;

(b) reflexive and transitive but not symmetric;

(c) symmetric and transitive but not reflexive.

(d) Here is a "proof" that (c) is impossible: *if $a \sim b$ then $b \sim a$ by symmetry, and then $a \sim a$ by transitivity, so $\sim$ is reflexive.* Find the error, and say exactly which of your examples in (c) exposes it.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), a relation may be symmetric and transitive without relating some element to anything at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) "$|a - b| \le 1$". (b) "$a \le b$", or divisibility. (c) the empty relation on a nonempty set, or "$a \sim b$ iff $a = b$ and $a$ is even".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $|a-a| = 0 \le 1$ and the condition is symmetric in $a, b$; but $0 \sim 1$ and $1 \sim 2$ while $|0 - 2| = 2 > 1$. (b) $\le$ is reflexive and transitive, and $0 \le 1$ with $1 \not\le 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) On $\mathbb{Z}$, let $a \sim b$ mean "$a = b$ and $a$ is even". Symmetric and transitive hold vacuously or trivially, but $1 \not\sim 1$, so it is not reflexive.

(d) The argument silently assumes that every $a$ is related to *something*. It shows only that $a \sim a$ holds for those $a$ appearing in some pair, which is a weaker statement than reflexivity. The example exposes it precisely at the odd integers, which appear in no pair at all. This is worth doing once by hand, because "reflexive" is the axiom most often assumed to be redundant, and the whole partition picture of §2.1 (p. 22) collapses without it: a non-reflexive $\sim$ leaves elements in no class.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — Lemma 2.6 and Theorem 2.7, in full)

Let $n$ be a positive integer.

(a) Prove that for every $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $[a]_n = [r]_n$ where $r$ is the remainder of $a$ on division by $n$.

(b) Prove that $[0]_n, [1]_n, \dots, [n-1]_n$ are pairwise distinct.

(c) Conclude Theorem 2.7 (p. 26): $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly $n$ elements. Point out where each of the two halves is used.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is existence and (b) is uniqueness — both halves of Theorem 1.3 (p. 6) are doing exactly one of the two jobs.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Suppose $[i]_n = [j]_n$ with $0 \le i \le j \le n-1$; then $n \mid (j - i)$ and $0 \le j - i < n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Write $a = nq + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Then $a - r = nq \in n\mathbb{Z}$, so $a \equiv r \bmod n$, hence $[a]_n = [r]_n$ by (2.1) (p. 24).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) The only multiple of $n$ in the window $[0, n)$ is $0$, so $j - i = 0$ and $i = j$.

(c) Every class equals one of the $n$ listed, by (a) — this is what stops the count exceeding $n$. No two of the listed classes coincide, by (b) — this is what stops it falling below $n$. Both halves are needed, and each corresponds to one half of the uniqueness-and-existence statement of Theorem 1.3. A count is two inequalities, and it is a common slip to prove one and announce both.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — when does a class mod $n$ determine a class mod $m$?)

Fix positive integers $m, n$ and consider the proposed rule
$$\pi : \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}, \qquad \pi([a]_n) = [a]_m.$$

(a) Prove that $\pi$ is well defined if and only if $m \mid n$.

(b) When $m \mid n$, show $\pi$ is surjective and compute the number of elements in each fibre.

(c) Take $m = 2$, $n = 6$ and write out $\pi$ on all six classes. Then take $m = 4$, $n = 6$ and exhibit two representatives of one class mod $6$ whose classes mod $4$ differ.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
"Well defined" means: whenever $[a]_n = [b]_n$, it must follow that $[a]_m = [b]_m$. Unfold both sides with Definition 2.1 (p. 23).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$[a]_n = [b]_n$ says $n \mid (b-a)$, and you need $m \mid (b-a)$. For the "only if", the class $[n]_n = [0]_n$ is the one to test.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $m \mid n$ and $n \mid (b-a)$, then $m \mid (b-a)$ by transitivity of divisibility, so $[a]_m = [b]_m$ and $\pi$ is well defined.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Conversely, suppose $\pi$ is well defined. Since $[n]_n = [0]_n$, well-definedness forces $[n]_m = [0]_m$, i.e. $m \mid n$. So the condition is not merely sufficient — it is read off a single instance.

(b) Surjective because $[a]_m = \pi([a]_n)$ for any $a$. Each fibre has $n/m$ elements: $[a]_m$ is hit by $[a]_n, [a+m]_n, \dots, [a + (n/m - 1)m]_n$, and these are distinct mod $n$ while $[a + (n/m)m]_n = [a+n]_n = [a]_n$ closes the list.

(c) For $m=2$, $n=6$: $[0],[2],[4] \mapsto [0]_2$ and $[1],[3],[5] \mapsto [1]_2$ — three per fibre, as $6/2 = 3$. For $m=4$, $n=6$: $0$ and $6$ represent the same class mod $6$, but $[0]_4 \ne [6]_4 = [2]_4$. The rule is not a rule at all here, and $4 \nmid 6$ is the whole reason.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — congruence mod $n$ is the only translation-invariant equivalence on ℤ)

Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$ that is **compatible with addition** in the weak sense that
$$a \sim b \implies a + c \sim b + c \quad\text{for every } c \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

Prove that there is a **unique** integer $n \ge 0$ such that $\sim$ is congruence mod $n$.

Suggested route:

(a) Let $N = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : x \sim 0\}$. Show $0 \in N$, that $x, y \in N \implies x + y \in N$, and that $x \in N \implies -x \in N$.

(b) Show $a \sim b$ if and only if $b - a \in N$.

(c) Show that $N = n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \ge 0$, using well-ordering and Theorem 1.3 (p. 6).

(d) Conclude, and check the two extreme cases $n = 0$ and $n = 1$ against Problem 1(b).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (a), to get $x + y \in N$ from $x \sim 0$, translate by $y$: $x + y \sim 0 + y = y$. Then use $y \sim 0$ and transitivity.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) If $N = \{0\}$ take $n = 0$. Otherwise $N$ contains a nonzero element and hence, by (a), a positive one; let $n$ be the least positive element of $N$, which exists by well-ordering. Divide an arbitrary $x \in N$ by $n$ and show the remainder lies in $N$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Reflexivity gives $0 \sim 0$, so $0 \in N$. If $x \sim 0$, translating by $-x$ gives $0 \sim -x$, so $-x \in N$ by symmetry. If $x, y \in N$: translating $x \sim 0$ by $y$ gives $x + y \sim y$, and $y \sim 0$, so $x+y \sim 0$ by transitivity.

(b) Translating $a \sim b$ by $-a$ gives $0 \sim b - a$, so $b - a \in N$; translating back recovers $a \sim b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Suppose $N \ne \{0\}$ and let $n$ be its least positive element. Certainly $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq N$, by repeated use of closure under addition and negation. Conversely let $x \in N$ and write $x = nq + r$ with $0 \le r < n$ (Theorem 1.3, p. 6). Then $r = x - nq$ is a sum of elements of $N$, so $r \in N$; and $0 \le r < n$ with $n$ least positive forces $r = 0$. Hence $x \in n\mathbb{Z}$ and $N = n\mathbb{Z}$. Uniqueness: $n\mathbb{Z} = n'\mathbb{Z}$ with $n, n' \ge 0$ gives $n \mid n'$ and $n' \mid n$, hence $n = n'$ once both are nonnegative.

(d) With (b), $a \sim b \iff b - a \in n\mathbb{Z}$, which is Definition 2.1 (p. 23). At $n = 0$ this is equality — Problem 1(b) — and at $n = 1$ every pair is related and $\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z}$ has one element, which is Theorem 2.7 (p. 26) at $n = 1$ rather than an exception to it.

What has been proved is that $\mathbb{Z}$ has no translation-invariant equivalence relations beyond the ones already in §2.2. The set $N$ is the object doing all the work — closed under addition and negation, and forced to be $n\mathbb{Z}$ — and it will reappear under the name *ideal* in aa-09, where the same well-ordering argument becomes the statement that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain.
</details>
