## Problem 1 (easy — two rings with four elements)

Example 4.2 (p. 60) puts $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ beside $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$.

(a) Write out the addition and multiplication tables of both.

(b) Find a statement in the language of rings that is true in one and false in the other, so that no relabelling of elements could turn one into the other.

(c) Do the same for the two rings with six elements in Example 4.3 (p. 60) — or explain why you cannot.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In one of the four-element rings, $a + a = 0$ for every $a$. Check whether that is true in the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Any statement quantified over all elements, mentioning only $+$, $\cdot$, $0$ and $1$, is immune to relabelling — that is what makes it usable as a distinguishing property.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) In $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$, every element satisfies $a + a = (0,0)$, since each coordinate does. In $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $[1] + [1] = [2] \ne [0]$. So the sentence "$\forall a,\ a + a = 0$" separates them.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Here you cannot, and the reason is worth seeing now rather than in aa-10. Try the same test: in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $[1]+[1] = [2] \ne [0]$, and in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})$, $(1,1)+(1,1) = (0,2) \ne (0,0)$. Try zero-divisors: both have them. Try counting units: $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has $[1]$ and $[5]$, and the product has $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$ — two each.

Every test agrees, and it is not an accident: the map $[a]_6 \mapsto ([a]_2, [a]_3)$ matches the two rings element for element and respects both operations. Chapter 5 will call that an isomorphism, and the general statement is Theorem 5.52, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, p. 97. The contrast with (b) is the whole point: $4 = 2\cdot 2$ has a repeated factor and $6 = 2\cdot 3$ does not.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — $\mathbb{C}$ is not $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$)

Example 4.4 (p. 60) notes that a complex number is determined by a pair of reals.

(a) Show that $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, with the product ring structure of Definition 4.1 (p. 59), has zero-divisors.

(b) Show that $\mathbb{C}$ has none.

(c) Conclude that the bijection $a + bi \leftrightarrow (a,b)$ is not compatible with multiplication, and identify precisely which structure it *does* respect.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The product ring multiplies coordinatewise; $\mathbb{C}$ does not.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $\mathbb{C}$ is a field, and Proposition 3.31 (p. 55) applies. Or argue directly with $|zw| = |z||w|$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(1,0)\cdot(0,1) = (0,0)$, and neither factor is $(0,0)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The bijection carries $(1,0) \mapsto 1$ and $(0,1)\mapsto i$, whose product in $\mathbb{C}$ is $i \ne 0$, while in $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ the product is $(0,0)$. So multiplication is not respected. Addition is: $(a,b) + (c,d) = (a+c, b+d)$ matches $(a+bi) + (c+di)$ exactly. The plane picture of $\mathbb{C}$ is a picture of its additive structure, and the multiplication is extra data laid on top — which is why "$\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$" is a true statement about vector spaces and a false one about rings.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the subring criterion, and what it saves you)

(a) Prove Proposition 4.14 (p. 63): a subset $S \subseteq R$ containing $0_R$ and $1_R$ and closed under $+$ and $\cdot$ is a subring if and only if it is also closed under additive inverses.

(b) Explain why associativity, commutativity of $+$ and distributivity never need checking for a subset — and why the two conditions in (a) do.

(c) Apply the criterion to the diagonal $\Delta = \{(a,a) : a \in R\} \subseteq R\times R$ of Example 4.9 (p. 62), and to $S = \{(a, 0) : a \in R\} \subseteq R\times R$ of Example 4.10 (p. 62). Which is a subring, and which clause fails for the other?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A universally quantified *equation* that holds for all elements of $R$ holds in particular for all elements of $S$. An *existence* claim does not transfer.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Sort the clauses of Definition 3.1 (p. 41) into equations and existence statements. The existence ones are: $0$, $1$, and additive inverses — and Definition 4.5 (p. 61) has already demanded the first two.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Associativity, commutativity and distributivity are equations holding for every element of $R$; restricting attention to a subset cannot break them. The existence clauses are different: $S$ might contain $a$ and not $-a$, and the element $-a$ that exists in $R$ is of no use if it is outside $S$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) If $S$ is a subring it is a ring in its own right, so it has additive inverses, and by uniqueness (Proposition 3.15, p. 48) these agree with those of $R$. Conversely, given the three closure conditions plus $0_R, 1_R \in S$, every clause of Definition 3.1 holds in $S$: the equational ones are inherited, and the existence ones are exactly what was assumed.

(c) $\Delta$ passes: $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ lie in it, $(a,a)+(b,b) = (a+b,a+b)$, $(a,a)(b,b) = (ab,ab)$, and $-(a,a) = (-a,-a)$. So $\Delta$ is a subring of $R \times R$.

$S = \{(a,0)\}$ fails on $1$: the ambient ring is $R\times R$, whose identity is $(1_R,1_R)$, and that is not of the form $(a,0)$ unless $R$ is the zero ring. Everything else holds — $S$ is closed under $+$, under $\cdot$, and under negation, and it even has its own identity $(1_R,0)$, which is simply not the identity of the big ring. That mismatch is the reason Aluffi's definition asks for $1_R \in S$ rather than "$S$ has some identity", and this subset is the first sighting of an *ideal*: closed under everything except containing $1$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — six candidates)

For each of the following, decide whether it is a subring in the sense of Definition 4.5 (p. 61), and prove your answer.

(a) $2\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$.

(b) $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ — Aluffi's Example 4.12 (p. 62).

(c) $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}] = \{a + b\sqrt 2 : a,b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

(d) $\{a + b\sqrt[3]{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

(e) The upper-triangular matrices $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & c\end{pmatrix}$ inside $M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$.

(f) $\{f \in C[0,1] : f(0) = 0\}$ inside $C[0,1]$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Use Proposition 4.14 (p. 63) every time: check $0$, check $1$, check closure under $+$, $\cdot$ and negation, and stop at the first failure.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), multiply $\sqrt[3]{2}$ by itself and ask whether the answer has the stated form.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) No: $1 \notin 2\mathbb{Z}$. Every other condition holds, which makes $2\mathbb{Z}$ a "ring without identity" of the kind Example 3.5 (p. 42) already showed.

(b) Yes: $0 = 0+0i$ and $1 = 1+0i$ are of the form; sums and negations are obvious; and $(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i$ has integer coordinates.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Yes, by the same computation: $(a+b\sqrt2)(c+d\sqrt2) = (ac+2bd) + (ad+bc)\sqrt2$.

(d) No. $\sqrt[3]{2}\cdot\sqrt[3]{2} = \sqrt[3]{4}$, which is not of the form $a + b\sqrt[3]{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ — indeed not with $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$, since $1, \sqrt[3]2, \sqrt[3]4$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. The set is closed under $+$ and $-$ and contains $0$ and $1$, and fails only on multiplication. The repair is to allow a $\sqrt[3]4$ term, and the reason two coordinates sufficed in (b) and (c) but three are needed here is the degree of the minimal polynomial — the subject of aa-14.

(e) Yes: the identity matrix is upper-triangular, and sums, negatives and products of upper-triangular matrices are upper-triangular. Note this subring is not commutative, so a subring of a noncommutative ring can be noncommutative — and a subring of a *commutative* ring is always commutative, for the reason in Problem 3(b).

(f) No: the constant function $1$ is not in it. Like (a) and like $\{(a,0)\}$, it is closed under everything else — another ideal in waiting.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — what the projection does and does not do)

Let $R$ and $S$ be nonzero rings and let $\pi : R\times S \to R$ be $\pi(a,b) = a$.

(a) Show $\pi$ is surjective, respects both operations, and sends $1_{R\times S}$ to $1_R$.

(b) Show that $\pi^{-1}(0_R) = \{0_R\}\times S$ is closed under $+$, under negation, and under multiplication by *anything* in $R\times S$ — but is not a subring.

(c) Now consider $\iota : R \to R\times S$, $\iota(a) = (a, 0_S)$. Show $\iota$ respects both operations but does **not** send $1_R$ to $1_{R\times S}$.

(d) Explain why (b) and (c) together mean that "$R$ sits inside $R\times S$" is a claim that needs care, and say which of the two maps deserves to be called structure-preserving.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) For the absorption property, compute $(r,s)\cdot(0,t)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Everything here is one line of coordinatewise computation. The interest is entirely in which conditions survive.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Surjective because $\pi(a, 0_S) = a$. $\pi((a,b)+(c,d)) = a + c = \pi(a,b)+\pi(c,d)$, and likewise for products. $\pi(1_R,1_S) = 1_R$.

(b) $(0,s)+(0,t) = (0,s+t)$ and $-(0,s) = (0,-s)$. And $(r,s)(0,t) = (0, st) \in \{0\}\times S$ — the product with an arbitrary element of the big ring stays inside. But $1_{R\times S} = (1_R,1_S) \notin \{0\}\times S$, since $R$ is nonzero.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\iota(a) + \iota(c) = (a+c, 0) = \iota(a+c)$ and $\iota(a)\iota(c) = (ac, 0) = \iota(ac)$. But $\iota(1_R) = (1_R, 0_S) \ne (1_R,1_S)$ whenever $S$ is nonzero.

(d) $\pi$ preserves everything Definition 4.5 (p. 61) cares about; $\iota$ preserves the arithmetic but lands on a copy of $R$ whose identity is not the ambient identity, so the copy is not a subring by Example 4.10 (p. 62). "$R$ sits inside $R\times S$" is true as a statement about sets and additive structure and false as a statement about subrings.

The two failures are the same failure seen from two sides, and both are the same as Problem 3(c) and Problem 4(a) and 4(f): a subset closed under addition, negation and multiplication-by-anything, but missing $1$. Four sightings of one object is enough to suspect it has a name, and in aa-09 it gets one.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — every ring contains exactly one copy of the integers or of a $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$)

Let $R$ be a ring. For $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ write $n\cdot 1_R$ for $1_R + \cdots + 1_R$ ($n$ times) when $n > 0$, for $0_R$ when $n = 0$, and for $-((-n)\cdot 1_R)$ when $n < 0$.

(a) Prove that $(m\cdot 1_R) + (n\cdot 1_R) = (m+n)\cdot 1_R$ and $(m\cdot 1_R)(n\cdot 1_R) = (mn)\cdot 1_R$ for all $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

(b) Deduce that $R_0 = \{n\cdot 1_R : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subring of $R$, and that it is contained in every subring of $R$.

(c) Let $N = \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : n\cdot 1_R = 0_R\}$. Show $N = c\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique integer $c \ge 0$. (This $c$ is the **characteristic** of $R$.)

(d) Compute the characteristic of $\mathbb{Z}$, of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, of $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})$, and of $C[0,1]$.

(e) Prove that if $R$ is an integral domain then its characteristic is $0$ or a prime.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is induction on $n$ for $n \ge 0$, then a sign argument. (c) is the argument of aa-03 Problem 6, with $N$ in place of the set of things related to $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(e) Suppose $c = ab$ with $1 < a, b < c$ and apply (a) to get $(a\cdot 1)(b\cdot 1) = c\cdot 1 = 0$; then use minimality of $c$ to see that neither factor is $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For $m, n \ge 0$ the additive statement is just regrouping a sum of $m+n$ copies of $1_R$, formally by induction on $n$. The multiplicative one follows from distributivity: $(m\cdot 1)(n\cdot 1)$ expands into $mn$ products $1_R\cdot 1_R = 1_R$.

(b) $R_0$ contains $0 = 0\cdot 1_R$ and $1_R = 1\cdot 1_R$, and (a) makes it closed under $+$ and $\cdot$; $-(n\cdot 1_R) = (-n)\cdot 1_R$ gives negation. Any subring contains $1_R$ and is closed under $+$ and negation, hence contains every $n\cdot 1_R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) By (a), $N$ is closed under addition and negation and contains $0$. If $N = \{0\}$ take $c = 0$. Otherwise $N$ contains a positive element; let $c$ be the least, which exists by well-ordering. For $n \in N$ write $n = cq + r$ with $0 \le r < c$ (Theorem 1.3, p. 6); then $r\cdot 1_R = n\cdot 1_R - q(c\cdot 1_R) = 0$, so $r \in N$ and minimality forces $r = 0$. Hence $N = c\mathbb{Z}$, and $c$ is unique among nonnegative integers because $c\mathbb{Z} = c'\mathbb{Z}$ forces $c \mid c'$ and $c' \mid c$.

(d) $\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $0$; $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ has $12$; $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})$ has $6$, since $n(1,1) = (0,0)$ needs $2 \mid n$ and $3 \mid n$ — and note this equals the characteristic of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, consistent with Problem 1(c). $C[0,1]$ has characteristic $0$, the constant function $n$ being nonzero for $n \ne 0$.

(e) Let $c$ be the characteristic and suppose $c > 0$ is composite, $c = ab$ with $1 < a,b < c$. Then $(a\cdot 1_R)(b\cdot 1_R) = c\cdot 1_R = 0_R$. Since $0 < a < c$ and $c$ is the *least* positive element of $N$, $a\cdot 1_R \ne 0$; likewise $b\cdot 1_R \ne 0$. So $R$ has zero-divisors, contradicting Definition 3.23 (p. 53). Also $c \ne 1$, since $1\cdot 1_R = 1_R \ne 0_R$ in a domain. Hence $c$ is $0$ or prime.
</details>
