## Problem 1 (easy — proof, functionals have nowhere to go) [interleaves la-06]

Explain why every linear functional is either surjective or the zero map.

*(Axler §3.F, Exercise 1, p. 113)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A linear functional maps into $\mathbb{F}$, and $\mathbb{F}$ regarded as a vector space over itself is very small. What are the possible ranges?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$\operatorname{range}\varphi$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{F}$ (la-06, Theorem 3.19), and $\dim\mathbb{F}=1$, so by la-04's Theorem 2.38 its dimension is $0$ or $1$. Deal with the two cases: dimension $0$ means the range is $\{0\}$, dimension $1$ means the range is all of $\mathbb{F}$. Each needs one sentence converting a dimension into a statement about the set.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $\dim\operatorname{range}\varphi=0$ then $\operatorname{range}\varphi=\{0\}$, so $\varphi v=0$ for every $v$, i.e. $\varphi=0$. If $\dim\operatorname{range}\varphi=1$ then $\operatorname{range}\varphi$ is a $1$-dimensional subspace of the $1$-dimensional space $\mathbb{F}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
A subspace whose dimension equals that of the whole space *is* the whole space (la-04's guided-proof result), so $\operatorname{range}\varphi=\mathbb{F}$ and $\varphi$ is surjective. Note that no finite-dimensionality hypothesis on $V$ is needed anywhere — everything happens in the codomain. That is worth saying, since the instinct after la-06 is to reach for the Fundamental Theorem, which would require one.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — computing a dual map)

Define $T:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2$ by $T(x,y,z)=(4x+5y+6z,\ 7x+8y+9z)$. Let $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$ be the dual basis of the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3$ the dual basis of the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

(a) Describe the linear functionals $T'(\varphi_1)$ and $T'(\varphi_2)$.

(b) Write $T'(\varphi_1)$ and $T'(\varphi_2)$ as linear combinations of $\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3$.

Then check your answer against $\mathcal{M}(T)$ and its transpose, and say which of the two matrices your coefficients form the columns of.

*(Axler §3.F, Exercise 13, p. 114)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$T'(\varphi)=\varphi\circ T$ by Definition 3.99 — so apply $T$ first and then read off a coordinate. $\varphi_1$ reads the first coordinate of a vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (Example 3.97).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Evaluate $(T'(\varphi_1))(x,y,z)=\varphi_1(T(x,y,z))$ directly; the answer is one of the two coordinate expressions defining $T$. For (b), recall $\psi_j$ reads the $j$-th coordinate of a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so a functional of the form $ax+by+cz$ is $a\psi_1+b\psi_2+c\psi_3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(T'(\varphi_1))(x,y,z)=4x+5y+6z$ and $(T'(\varphi_2))(x,y,z)=7x+8y+9z$. So $T'(\varphi_1)=4\psi_1+5\psi_2+6\psi_3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Likewise $T'(\varphi_2)=7\psi_1+8\psi_2+9\psi_3$. For the check: $\mathcal{M}(T)$ has rows $(4,5,6)$ and $(7,8,9)$, so its transpose has columns $(4,5,6)$ and $(7,8,9)$ — and by Definition 3.32 the columns of $\mathcal{M}(T')$ are the coefficient lists you just computed. So your coefficients are the columns of $\mathcal{M}(T')=(\mathcal{M}(T))^{t}$, which is Theorem 3.114 verified in one instance.

State the direction of $T'$ explicitly in your answer: $T'$ maps $(\mathbb{R}^2)'$ to $(\mathbb{R}^3)'$, so it is legitimate for its outputs to be functionals on $\mathbb{R}^3$ even though $T$ maps *out* of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Getting that backwards is the standard way to lose marks here.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, a proper subspace is detected by a functional)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $U$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U\ne V$. Prove that there exists $\varphi\in V'$ such that $\varphi(u)=0$ for every $u\in U$ but $\varphi\ne0$.

*(Axler §3.F, Exercise 4, p. 113)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The set of functionals vanishing on $U$ has a name and a known dimension. You do not need to construct $\varphi$ at all — you need to show the space it lives in is not $\{0\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The functionals vanishing on $U$ are exactly $U^0$ (Definition 3.102), and Theorem 3.106 gives $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$. So the task reduces to showing $\dim U<\dim V$, which follows from $U\ne V$ by la-04's Theorem 2.38 together with the guided-proof result from la-04.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\dim U\le\dim V$ by Theorem 2.38, and $\dim U=\dim V$ would force $U=V$, which is excluded. So $\dim U<\dim V$ and $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U\ge1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
A space of dimension at least $1$ contains a nonzero vector, so some $\varphi\in U^0$ is nonzero — and by definition of $U^0$ it vanishes on every $u\in U$. That is the required functional.

Two remarks worth making. The proof is entirely a dimension count and produces no formula; if you want an explicit $\varphi$, take a basis of $U$, extend it to a basis of $V$ (la-04, Theorem 2.33), and use the dual functional belonging to any added vector. And note the shape of the statement — it says the dual space is rich enough to *see* every proper subspace, which is the same separating property as the lesson's guided proof, one level up.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, annihilators reverse inclusion)

(a) Suppose $U$ and $W$ are subsets of $V$ with $U\subseteq W$. Prove that $W^0\subseteq U^0$.

(b) Suppose now that $V$ is finite-dimensional and $U,W$ are **subspaces** of $V$ with $W^0\subseteq U^0$. Prove that $U\subseteq W$.

Say why part (b) needs hypotheses that part (a) does not.

*(Axler §3.F, Exercises 20 and 21, p. 114)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is one line from Definition 3.102. For (b), argue by contradiction: assume some $u\in U$ lies outside $W$, and manufacture a functional that kills $W$ but not $u$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Suppose $u\in U$ with $u\notin W$. Take a basis of $W$, adjoin $u$ — the extended list is independent precisely because $u\notin\operatorname{span}(W)=W$ (la-03, Exercise 11's criterion) — and extend to a basis of $V$. The dual functional belonging to $u$ then vanishes on $W$ and equals $1$ at $u$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $\varphi\in W^0$ then $\varphi$ vanishes on every element of $W$, hence on every element of $U\subseteq W$, so $\varphi\in U^0$.
(b) With $\varphi$ as above, $\varphi\in W^0\subseteq U^0$, so $\varphi(u)=0$ — contradicting $\varphi(u)=1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Hence no such $u$ exists and $U\subseteq W$. For the final part of the question: (a) needs nothing at all — $U$ and $W$ may be arbitrary subsets and $V$ may be infinite-dimensional — because it only unwinds a definition. (b) needs $W$ to be a **subspace**, so that $u\notin W$ really does mean $u\notin\operatorname{span}$ of a basis of $W$ and the extended list is independent; and it needs finite-dimensionality to extend that list to a basis of $V$ by Theorem 2.33.

Together (a) and (b) say that on subspaces of a finite-dimensional space, $U\mapsto U^0$ is inclusion-reversing *and* injective — the same order-reversal as the dual map itself, seen on subspaces instead of on maps.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the annihilator of a sum)

Suppose $U,W$ are subspaces of $V$. Show that
$$(U+W)^0 = U^0\cap W^0.$$

*(Axler §3.F, Exercise 22, p. 114)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Set equality, two inclusions. One of them is Problem 4(a) applied twice; the other is a direct computation on a general element $u+w$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\subseteq$) $U\subseteq U+W$ and $W\subseteq U+W$, so Problem 4(a) gives $(U+W)^0\subseteq U^0$ and $(U+W)^0\subseteq W^0$, hence $(U+W)^0$ is contained in the intersection. ($\supseteq$) Take $\varphi$ vanishing on both $U$ and $W$, and evaluate it at an arbitrary element of $U+W$, which has the form $u+w$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\varphi(u+w)=\varphi(u)+\varphi(w)=0+0=0$, using additivity of $\varphi$. So $\varphi$ annihilates every element of $U+W$, i.e. $\varphi\in(U+W)^0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The two inclusions give equality. Three things earn the marks. Both inclusions must be written — the ($\subseteq$) half is genuinely a corollary of Problem 4(a) and should cite it rather than being redone. The ($\supseteq$) half is where additivity of $\varphi$ is used, and it is the only place. And no finite-dimensionality is needed anywhere, which is worth stating: this is an identity about definitions, not a dimension count, unlike most of §3.F.

If you want a sanity check, take $V=\mathbb{R}^3$ with $U$ and $W$ two distinct lines through the origin and confirm both sides have dimension $1$ by Theorem 3.106.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — dualising is itself an isomorphism)

Suppose $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional. Prove that the map that takes $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ to $T'\in\mathcal{L}(W',V')$ is an isomorphism of $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ onto $\mathcal{L}(W',V')$.

*(Axler §3.F, Exercise 16, p. 114)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Three things: linear, injective, surjective. Two of them are quotable; the third is best obtained by counting rather than by constructing a preimage.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Call the map $\Lambda(T)=T'$. Linearity is the first two bullet points of Theorem 3.101. For injectivity, show $T'=0$ forces $T=0$, using the lesson's guided-proof result to produce a functional that detects a nonzero $Tv$. For surjectivity, compute both dimensions with la-08's Theorem 3.61 and Theorem 3.95, then use la-06's Fundamental Theorem — do not try to build a preimage by hand.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Injectivity: suppose $T\ne0$, so $Tv\ne0$ for some $v$. By the lesson's guided-proof result there is $\varphi\in W'$ with $\varphi(Tv)=1$. Then $(\Lambda(T)(\varphi))(v)=(\varphi\circ T)(v)=1\ne0$, so $\Lambda(T)\ne0$. Contrapositively $\Lambda(T)=0$ forces $T=0$, i.e. $\operatorname{null}\Lambda=\{0\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For surjectivity, $\dim\mathcal{L}(V,W)=(\dim V)(\dim W)$ and $\dim\mathcal{L}(W',V')=(\dim W')(\dim V')=(\dim W)(\dim V)$, using Theorem 3.61 twice and Theorem 3.95 twice. The two are equal. Since $\Lambda$ is injective, la-06's Fundamental Theorem gives $\dim\operatorname{range}\Lambda=\dim\mathcal{L}(V,W)=\dim\mathcal{L}(W',V')$, and a subspace of full dimension is the whole space — so $\Lambda$ is surjective. With injectivity, la-08's Theorem 3.56 makes $\Lambda$ an isomorphism.

Two points for the write-up. Note that la-08's Theorem 3.69 does **not** apply: $\Lambda$ is not an operator, its domain and codomain being different spaces, so the equality of dimensions has to be established and used explicitly rather than assumed away. And observe what the result says — dualising is not merely a construction that happens to exist, but a perfect dictionary between $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ and $\mathcal{L}(W',V')$, which is the precise sense in which no information is lost when a computation is run on the dual side.
</details>
