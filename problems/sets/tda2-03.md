## Problem 1 (easy — decomposition and what Krull–Schmidt gives) [interleaves tda2-02]

(a) State Definition 11.10, including what it means for $M$ to be
*indecomposable*.
(b) State what the Krull–Schmidt theorem supplies for a finitely generated
graded module, and what it does **not** supply.
(c) Contrast this with the one-parameter case of tda1-04: there, what plays the
role of the indecomposables, and what extra thing is true that fails here?
(d) §11.6 opens by saying that for multiparameter persistence modules "no such
complete invariants exist that are finite and hence computable". Explain how
this is consistent with Krull–Schmidt guaranteeing an essentially unique
decomposition.

*(Dey §11.2.1, Definition 11.10, p. 329; §11.6 opening, pp. 356–357)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Existence and uniqueness of a decomposition is a different claim from having a
finite list of what the pieces can look like.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Keep three notions apart: a decomposition exists; it is unique; the summands
are classifiable by a finite computable invariant. Krull–Schmidt gives the first
two only.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
In one parameter the indecomposables are exactly the interval modules, and the
multiset of intervals is the barcode.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 11.10: for a finitely generated graded module $M$, a
*decomposition* is an isomorphism $M \cong \bigoplus M^i$ for some collection of
modules $\{M^i\}$; $M$ is *indecomposable* if $M \cong M^1 \oplus M^2$ forces
$M^1 = \mathbf{0}$ or $M^2 = \mathbf{0}$.
(b) Krull–Schmidt supplies an essentially unique decomposition into
indecomposables — unique up to permutation and isomorphism. It supplies no
description of what those indecomposables are, and no finite invariant that
distinguishes them.
(c) In one parameter the indecomposables are exactly the interval modules, and
the multiset of intervals — the barcode — is a complete, finite, computable
invariant. Here existence and uniqueness survive; classification does not.
(d) The two statements are about different things. Krull–Schmidt says the
decomposition exists and is essentially unique; the non-existence of a complete
finite invariant says there is no finite computable summary that is equal for
two modules exactly when they are isomorphic. One can have a unique
decomposition into pieces one cannot enumerate — which is precisely the
situation for $d \geq 2$, and precisely why the rest of §11.6 settles for
incomplete invariants.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — presentations)

(a) State Definition 11.13 and say what exactness of
$F^1 \xrightarrow{f} F^0 \xrightarrow{g} H \to \mathbf{0}$ tells you about $g$
and about $\operatorname{im} f$.
(b) Explain why a presentation is called a description by "generators and
relations", identifying which free module supplies which.
(c) State Remark 11.1: in what sense is a presentation non-unique, and in what
sense is there a unique one?
(d) State Definition 11.14 and say what has to be chosen before a presentation
map has a matrix, and what ring the entries live in.

*(Dey §11.2.1, Definition 11.13, Remark 11.1 and Definition 11.14, pp. 330–331)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Exactness at $H$ says $g$ is surjective; exactness at $F^0$ says
$\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Generators are basis elements of $F^0$; relations are basis elements of $F^1$,
and $f$ records which combination of generators each relation kills.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H \cong \operatorname{coker} f = F^0/\operatorname{im} f$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 11.13: a presentation of a graded module $H$ is an exact sequence
$F^1 \xrightarrow{f} F^0 \xrightarrow{g} H \to \mathbf{0}$ with $F^1, F^0$ free;
$f$ is the presentation map, and $H$ is finitely presented when both are
finitely generated. Exactness at $H$ makes $g$ surjective; exactness at $F^0$
gives $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$. Together, $H \cong \operatorname{coker} f$.
(b) A basis of $F^0$ maps onto a generating set of $H$ — the generators. A basis
of $F^1$ maps under $f$ into $\ker g$ — each such element is a combination of
generators that is zero in $H$, that is, a relation.
(c) Remark 11.1: presentations are not unique. However there is an essentially
unique presentation $f$ — the *minimal* presentation — such that any presentation
$f'$ can be written $f' \cong f \oplus f''$ with $\operatorname{coker} f'' = \mathbf{0}$.
So every presentation is the minimal one plus redundant baggage that contributes
nothing to the cokernel.
(d) Definition 11.14: fixing bases of $F^1$ (relations) and $F^0$ (generators)
gives the presentation map a matrix form $[f]$, the presentation matrix of $H$,
with entries in $R$. If $H$ is free, so that $F^1$ is the zero module, the
presentation matrix is defined to be a null column matrix.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the three-way correspondence)

(a) State Theorem 11.1 in full, listing all three structures it puts in
one-to-one correspondence.
(b) Explain why the theorem is stated for a *minimal* presentation map, and what
Proposition 11.2 provides when the presentation is not minimal.
(c) State Remark 11.3 and describe the shape of the matrix
$[f] \cong [f^*] \oplus [g] \oplus [h]$: what does each of the three pieces look
like, and which one carries the module?
(d) Given Theorem 11.1, explain in two sentences why "decompose the module" and
"diagonalise a matrix over $\mathbf{k}[x_1,\dots,x_d]$" are the same problem, and
say what that buys computationally.

*(Dey §11.2.1, Theorem 11.1, Remark 11.2, Proposition 11.2 and Remark 11.3,
pp. 331–332; Definition 11.15, pp. 332–333)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The three structures are a decomposition of $H$, a decomposition of $f$, and a
diagonalisation of $[f]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), note that Proposition 11.2 deliberately drops minimality and still gets
one direction of the correspondence.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$[g]$ is an identity submatrix and $[h]$ is an empty matrix in Remark 11.3.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Theorem 11.1: for a minimal presentation map $f : F^1 \to F^0$ of a graded
module $H$ with presentation matrix $[f]$, there are one-to-one correspondences
between (a) a decomposition $H \cong \bigoplus H^i$; (b) a decomposition
$f \cong \bigoplus f_i$; and (c) a diagonalisation $[f] \cong \bigoplus [f]_i$.
(b) Minimality is what makes the correspondence a bijection: a non-minimal
presentation carries extra summands with zero cokernel, which decompose without
corresponding to any piece of $H$. Proposition 11.2 recovers what survives
without minimality — for any decomposition $H \cong \bigoplus H^i$ there is a
decomposition $f \cong \bigoplus f_i$ with $\operatorname{coker} f_i = H^i$, and
the total decomposition of $H$ follows from the total decomposition of $f$.
(c) Remark 11.3: any presentation splits as $f \cong f^* \oplus f'$ with $f^*$
minimal and $\operatorname{coker} f' = \mathbf{0}$, and further $f' \cong g \oplus h$
with $g$ an identity map and $h$ a zero map. In matrix form
$[f] \cong [f^*] \oplus [g] \oplus [h]$, where $[g]$ is an identity submatrix and
$[h]$ is an empty matrix. Only $[f^*]$ carries the module.
(d) By Theorem 11.1 the two operations are literally the same operation viewed
through different structures, so an algorithm that block-diagonalises the
presentation matrix over $R$ simultaneously produces the module's decomposition.
Computationally this converts an abstract algebra question into linear algebra
over a polynomial ring — which is what Dey and Wang's TOTDIAGONALIZE of §11.4
performs, and it is why the presentation matrix, not the module, is the object
an implementation stores.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the rank invariant and its limits) [interleaves tda1-05]

(a) State Definition 11.17 and Definition 11.18.
(b) State Fact 11.3, and say which one-parameter object of tda1-05 the rank
invariant reduces to.
(c) Dey and Wang say that for multiparameter modules the rank invariant "cannot
provide information about the birth and death of generators". State
Definition 11.19 and Fact 11.4, and explain precisely how the multirank invariant
recovers that information.
(d) State Fact 11.5 and explain why it is weaker than a completeness statement.
Then say, in one sentence, what a practitioner may and may not conclude from two
modules having equal multirank invariants.

*(Dey §11.6.1, Definition 11.17, Fact 11.2, Definition 11.18, Fact 11.3,
Definition 11.19, Fact 11.4 and Fact 11.5, pp. 357–359)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Fact 11.5 is conditional on the modules being interval decomposable.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Fact 11.4 counts births by a cokernel over the immediate predecessors and deaths
by a kernel into the immediate successors.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$r_{\mathbf{uv}}(M) = \operatorname{rank}(M_{\mathbf{u}} \to M_{\mathbf{v}})$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 11.17: the support of a $\mathbb{Z}^d$-graded module $M$ is the
graph $\operatorname{supp}(M) = (V,E)$ with $\mathbf{v} \in V$ iff
$M_{\mathbf{v}} \neq \mathbf{0}$, and $(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) \in E$ iff
$\mathbf{u} < \mathbf{v}$ with nothing strictly between them and the map between
them non-zero in the relevant sense. Definition 11.18: with
$r_{\mathbf{uv}}(M) = \operatorname{rank}(M_{\mathbf{u}} \to M_{\mathbf{v}})$,
the collection $\{r_{\mathbf{uv}}(M)\}$ over pairs in the support is the rank
invariant.
(b) Fact 11.3: the rank invariant of a one-parameter module is a complete
invariant, and for $\mathsf{H}_p$ it is given by the persistent Betti numbers
$\beta_p^{i,j}$ — equivalently, it is the barcode of tda1-05 in another
notation.
(c) Definition 11.19: the multirank invariant is the collection
$\{r_{\mathbf{UV}}(M)\}$ over *subsets* $\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}$ of the support,
where $r_{\mathbf{UV}}(M) = \operatorname{rank}(\bigoplus_{\mathbf{u} \in \mathbf{U}} M_{\mathbf{u}} \to \bigoplus_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbf{V}} M_{\mathbf{v}})$.
Fact 11.4 then says $m$ generators are born at grade $\mathbf{u}$ iff the
cokernel of $\bigoplus_{\mathbf{u}' \in P_{\mathbf{u}}} M_{\mathbf{u}'} \to M_{\mathbf{u}}$
has dimension $m$, and $m$ generators die leaving $\mathbf{u}$ iff the kernel of
$M_{\mathbf{u}} \to \bigoplus_{\mathbf{u}' \in S_{\mathbf{u}}} M_{\mathbf{u}'}$
has dimension $m$, where $P_{\mathbf{u}}$ and $S_{\mathbf{u}}$ are the immediate
predecessors and successors. Taking whole sets of grades at once is what makes
births and deaths visible; a single pair cannot see them.
(d) Fact 11.5: two *interval decomposable* modules are isomorphic if and only if
they have the same multirank invariants. This is weaker than completeness
because the hypothesis restricts the modules — outside the interval decomposable
class the multirank invariant is still not complete, as Dey and Wang state
explicitly. A practitioner may conclude that two modules with different
multirank invariants are non-isomorphic; equal multirank invariants prove
isomorphism only if both modules are already known to be interval decomposable.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — graded Betti numbers and blockcodes)

(a) State Definition 11.20 and explain how a free resolution extends a
presentation.
(b) State Definition 11.21 and Definition 11.22, and explain the difference
between the graded Betti numbers of $M$ and the *persistent* graded Betti
numbers.
(c) State Fact 11.6 and use it to explain in what sense the graded Betti numbers
determine the dimension function. Verify the alternating-sum formula on the free
module $R_{\to(1,1)}$ of tda2-02, whose minimal free resolution has
$F^0 = R_{\to(1,1)}$ and $F^j = 0$ for $j \geq 1$.
(d) State Definition 11.23. Then argue, using Problem 1(d), why the blockcode is
worth computing even though it is not a complete invariant — and state exactly
what a mismatch of blockcodes does and does not prove.

*(Dey §11.6.2, Definition 11.20 through Definition 11.23 and Fact 11.6,
pp. 359–361)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A presentation is the tail $F^1 \to F^0 \to M \to \mathbf{0}$ of a resolution;
continue by resolving $\ker f^1$, and so on.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the free module has a single generator at grade $(1,1)$ and no
relations, so only $\beta_{0,(1,1)} = 1$ is non-zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathrm{dm}M(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{\mathbf{v} \leq \mathbf{u}} \sum_j (-1)^j \beta_{j,\mathbf{v}}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 11.20: a free resolution of $M$ is an exact sequence
$\cdots \to F^2 \xrightarrow{f^2} F^1 \xrightarrow{f^1} F^0 \xrightarrow{f^0} M \to \mathbf{0}$
with every $F^i$ free. It extends a presentation because a presentation is
exactly the truncation $F^1 \to F^0 \to M \to \mathbf{0}$; one continues by
choosing a free module surjecting onto $\ker f^1$, and repeating.
(b) Definition 11.21: for $F^j$ in the *minimal* free resolution of $M$, let
$\beta_{j,\mathbf{u}}^M$ be the multiplicity of the grade $\mathbf{u}$ among the
grades of homogeneous basis elements of $F^j$; the map
$\beta_{(-,-)}^M$ is the graded Betti numbers. Definition 11.22: taking a total
decomposition $M \cong \bigoplus M^i$ and recording the graded Betti numbers
$\beta^{M^i}$ of each indecomposable separately gives the *persistent* graded
Betti numbers $\mathcal{PB}(M) = \{\beta^{M^i}\}$. The difference is that the
plain version aggregates over the whole module and so loses which generator
belongs with which relation; the persistent version keeps the summands apart,
which is what makes it barcode-like.
(c) Fact 11.6: for all $\mathbf{u}$,
$\mathrm{dm}M(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{\mathbf{v} \leq \mathbf{u}} \sum_j (-1)^j \beta_{j,\mathbf{v}}$,
so the dimension at each grade is an alternating count of generators, relations,
relations-among-relations and so on, accumulated over the down-set of
$\mathbf{u}$. For $M = R_{\to(1,1)}$ the minimal resolution is
$0 \to F^0 = R_{\to(1,1)} \to M \to \mathbf{0}$, so the only non-zero graded Betti
number is $\beta_{0,(1,1)} = 1$. The formula then gives
$\mathrm{dm}M(\mathbf{u}) = 1$ when $(1,1) \leq \mathbf{u}$ and $0$ otherwise —
which is exactly the shifted quadrant with apex $(1,1)$ computed in tda2-02.
(d) Definition 11.23: the set of dimension functions
$\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{dm}}(M) := \{\mathrm{dm}M^i\}$ over the indecomposable
summands is the blockcode of $M$. By Problem 1(d) no finite computable complete
invariant exists for $d \geq 2$, so the choice is not between an incomplete
invariant and a complete one — it is between an incomplete invariant and none.
The blockcode is computable, is equal for isomorphic modules, and refines the
plain graded Betti numbers by keeping the summands separate. A mismatch of
blockcodes therefore *proves* the two modules are non-isomorphic; a match proves
nothing, since non-isomorphic modules may share a blockcode.
</details>
