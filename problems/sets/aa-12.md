## Problem 1 (easy–medium — a valuation makes every ideal principal)

(a) Check that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain in the sense of Definition 6.24 (pp. 112–113), with $v(n) = |n|$, quoting Theorem 1.3 (p. 6).

(b) Prove Theorem 6.26 (p. 113): every Euclidean domain is a PID (Definition 6.25, p. 113).

(c) You proved "every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$" by hand in aa-09 Problem 6(a). Compare that proof with (b) specialised to $\mathbb{Z}$, and say what (b) has abstracted.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Given a nonzero ideal $I$, choose $b \in I\setminus\{0\}$ with $v(b)$ least — possible because $v$ takes values in the non-negative integers, so well-ordering applies.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Then divide an arbitrary $a \in I$ by $b$ and show the remainder must be $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Theorem 1.3 (p. 6) gives, for $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $b\ne0$, a unique $q$ and $r$ with $a = bq+r$ and $0\le r<|b|$. So either $r=0$ or $|r| = r < |b|$, which is Definition 6.24's requirement with $v = |\cdot|$. Aluffi's definition asks only for existence, not uniqueness, so division with remainder supplies more than is needed here. Example 6.27 (p. 114) is this observation.

(b) Let $I$ be an ideal of the Euclidean domain $R$. If $I=\{0\}$ then $I = (0)$. Otherwise the set $\{v(b) : b\in I, b\ne 0\}$ is a nonempty set of non-negative integers, so by well-ordering it has a least element; pick $b\in I$ attaining it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) Certainly $(b)\subseteq I$. Conversely let $a\in I$ and write $a = bq+r$ with $r=0$ or $v(r)<v(b)$. Then $r = a - bq\in I$, and if $r\ne 0$ its valuation would beat the minimum. So $r=0$, $a = bq\in(b)$, and $I=(b)$.

(c) The two proofs have the same three moves: pick the least element, divide, kill the remainder by minimality. What (b) abstracts is *what is being minimised*. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the natural order on the elements themselves does the job, and it is tempting to think the argument is about the ordering of $\mathbb{Z}$. It is not: it is about the existence of a function to $\mathbb{Z}^{\ge0}$ that division decreases. The ring need not be ordered at all — $k[x]$ uses degree, and the Gaussian integers use the norm.

This is the fourth appearance of the argument (aa-03 Problem 6, aa-07 Problem 6, aa-09 Problem 6, here), and the last: from now on it is a corollary of Theorem 6.26 rather than a proof to repeat.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — in a PID, irreducible and prime agree)

(a) Prove Lemma 6.22 (p. 112): for $q\ne 0$ a non-unit in a domain, $q$ is irreducible if and only if $(q)$ is maximal among the **proper principal** ideals.

(b) Prove Proposition 6.33 (p. 117): in a PID, if $q$ is irreducible then $(q)$ is a maximal ideal, and hence $q$ is a prime element.

(c) Where exactly does (b) use that $R$ is a PID and not merely a domain?

(d) Combine (b) with Theorem 6.23 (p. 112) into a single statement about PIDs, and say which theorem of chapter 1 it generalises.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $(q)\subseteq(a)$ says $a \mid q$, i.e. $q = ab$ for some $b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) In a PID, "maximal among principal ideals" and "maximal among ideals" are the same condition, because every ideal is principal. Then use Proposition 6.3 (p. 106).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) Let $q$ be irreducible and $(q)\subseteq(a)$. Then $q = ab$, so $a$ or $b$ is a unit. If $a$ is a unit, $(a)=(1)$. If $b$ is a unit, $a = qb^{-1}$, so $(a)=(q)$. ($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, suppose $(q)$ is maximal among proper principal ideals and $q = ab$. Then $(q)\subseteq(a)$, so $(a)=(1)$ — making $a$ a unit — or $(a)=(q)$, in which case $a = qc$ for some $c$, so $q = qcb$ and cancelling gives $cb=1$, making $b$ a unit.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) In a PID every ideal is principal, so "maximal among proper principal ideals" is "maximal among proper ideals", and (a) makes $(q)$ maximal. Then $(q)$ is prime by Proposition 6.3 (p. 106), and $q$ is a prime element by Definition 6.18 (p. 111).

(c) In exactly one place: the step that upgrades "maximal among principal ideals" to "maximal among ideals". Lemma 6.22 is a statement about domains and is *not* the same as maximality — in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal $(x)$ is maximal among proper principal ideals and is not a maximal ideal, since $(x)\subsetneq(2,x)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $(2,x)$ is not principal (aa-11 Problem 6(c)). So $x$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ without $(x)$ being maximal — and $x$ *is* prime there, which shows (b)'s conclusion can hold for other reasons even when its proof does not apply.

(d) **In a PID, a nonzero element is prime if and only if it is irreducible.** Theorem 6.23 gives one direction in any domain; (b) gives the other, and needs the PID hypothesis. This is Theorem 1.21 (p. 14) — Aluffi's theorem that in $\mathbb{Z}$ prime and irreducible agree — with $\mathbb{Z}$ replaced by an arbitrary PID, and it explains what aa-11 Problem 5(d) found: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ has an irreducible that is not prime because it is not a PID.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — ascending chains stop)

(a) Prove that the union of an ascending chain of ideals $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$ is an ideal.

(b) Prove Proposition 6.32 (p. 116): in a PID every ascending chain of ideals stabilises — there is an $N$ with $I_n = I_N$ for all $n\ge N$.

(c) Show (a) genuinely needs the chain condition: give two ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ whose union is not an ideal.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) The union is an ideal by (a), so it is $(d)$ for some $d$; and $d$ lies in the union, so in some $I_N$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Take $2\mathbb{Z}$ and $3\mathbb{Z}$ and add two elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $J = \bigcup_n I_n$. Given $a, b\in J$, say $a\in I_i$ and $b\in I_j$ with $i\le j$; then both lie in $I_j$, so $a+b\in I_j\subseteq J$. And for $r\in R$ and $a\in I_i$, absorption in $I_i$ gives $ra, ar\in I_i\subseteq J$. Nonempty because $0\in I_1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) By (a), $J = \bigcup_n I_n$ is an ideal, so $J = (d)$ for some $d$ since $R$ is a PID. Now $d\in J$, so $d\in I_N$ for some $N$. Then for every $n\ge N$,
$$(d)\subseteq I_N\subseteq I_n\subseteq J = (d),$$
so all the containments are equalities and $I_n = I_N$.

(c) $2\mathbb{Z}\cup3\mathbb{Z}$ contains $2$ and $3$ but not $5$, so it is not closed under addition. The union of *two* ideals is almost never an ideal; what makes (a) work is that any two elements of a chain lie in a common member, so the closure conditions never have to compare incomparable ideals. That is the whole content of the chain hypothesis, and it is why (b) is a statement about chains rather than about arbitrary families.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — factorisation, existence and uniqueness)

(a) Prove the existence half of Theorem 6.35 (p. 117): in a PID, every nonzero non-unit is a product of finitely many irreducibles. Use Proposition 6.32.

(b) Prove the uniqueness half, up to order and units. Use Proposition 6.33 (p. 117).

(c) Definition 6.37 (p. 119) calls a domain in which the conclusion of Theorem 6.35 holds a **UFD**. State the containment Theorem 6.35 establishes, and give a UFD that is not a PID — Remark 6.38 (p. 119).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Suppose $a$ has no such factorisation. Then $a$ is not irreducible, so $a = bc$ with both non-units, and one of $b, c$ has no factorisation either. Iterate, and watch the chain of ideals.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Induct on the number of factors, using that an irreducible in a PID is prime to move a factor from one side to the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Suppose some nonzero non-unit $a_1$ has no factorisation into irreducibles. It is not itself irreducible, so $a_1 = a_2b_2$ with both non-units, and at least one factor — say $a_2$ — has no factorisation. Note $(a_1)\subsetneq(a_2)$: the containment holds since $a_2\mid a_1$, and it is strict because $(a_1)=(a_2)$ would make $b_2$ a unit. Repeating gives a strictly ascending chain $(a_1)\subsetneq(a_2)\subsetneq\cdots$ that never stabilises, contradicting Proposition 6.32.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Suppose $q_1\cdots q_r = p_1\cdots p_s$ with all factors irreducible, and induct on $r$. Since $q_1$ is prime by Proposition 6.33 and divides the right-hand side, it divides some $p_j$; after reordering, $q_1 \mid p_1$, so $p_1 = q_1u$. As $p_1$ is irreducible and $q_1$ is not a unit, $u$ is a unit. Cancel $q_1$ — legitimate in a domain — to get $q_2\cdots q_r = (up_2)p_3\cdots p_s$, and apply the inductive hypothesis. The base case $r=1$ says an irreducible that is a product of irreducibles is one of them, up to a unit.

Note where each hypothesis went: **existence used the chain condition, uniqueness used irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime.** These are two different consequences of being a PID, and aa-02 Problem 6 already made exactly this split for $\mathbb{Z}$ — where existence survived in the multiplicative set $\{1,5,9,\dots\}$ and uniqueness did not.

(c) **Every PID is a UFD.** The converse fails: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD — its irreducibles are the primes of $\mathbb{Z}$ together with the primitive irreducible polynomials, and aa-14 makes this precise — and it is not a PID, since $(2,x)$ is not principal by aa-11 Problem 6(c). So the two conditions are genuinely different, and Remark 6.38 records that the gap is large rather than exceptional.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — a hierarchy, and what this set can and cannot separate)

Consider the containments
$$\text{fields}\ \subseteq\ \text{Euclidean domains}\ \subseteq\ \text{PIDs}\ \subseteq\ \text{UFDs}\ \subseteq\ \text{integral domains}.$$

(a) Prove the first containment: every field is a Euclidean domain.

(b) Give a Euclidean domain that is not a field.

(c) Give a UFD that is not a PID, and a domain that is not a UFD. Prove both.

(d) One of the four containments is not separated by anything in this unit. Say which, and state honestly what would be needed.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) In a field, division with remainder always has remainder $0$. Any $v$ at all will do.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) For the second, use aa-11 Problem 5(c) and exhibit two genuinely different factorisations of one element.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $k$ be a field and set $v(a) = 0$ for all $a\ne 0$. Given $a$ and $b\ne 0$, take $q = ab^{-1}$ and $r = 0$. So Definition 6.24 holds vacuously in its second alternative, and every field is Euclidean, hence a PID and a UFD.

(b) $\mathbb{Z}$, by Problem 1(a); it is not a field since $2$ has no inverse.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) *UFD not a PID:* $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, by Problem 4(c).

*Domain not a UFD:* $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$. It is a domain, being a subring of $\mathbb{C}$. In it,
$$6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}),$$
and all four factors are irreducible: $N(2)=4$, $N(3)=9$, $N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})=6$, and a proper factorisation of any of them would need an element of norm $2$ or $3$, while $a^2+5b^2$ takes neither value. The two factorisations are not rearrangements of each other up to units, because the units are $\pm1$ (aa-11 Problem 5(c)) and $\pm2 \ne 1\pm\sqrt{-5}$. So uniqueness fails.

(d) **Euclidean $\subseteq$ PID is not separated here.** The containment is strict — there are PIDs carrying no Euclidean valuation whatever — but exhibiting one is a different kind of work from everything above: it requires showing that *no* function $v$ can exist, not that one particular candidate fails, and the standard witness, $\mathbb{Z}\big[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}\big]$, needs machinery this unit does not have. It is recorded here as unproven rather than asserted, because "the containment is strict" and "here is a witness I have checked" are different claims and only the second is a solution.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — making every nonzero element invertible)

Let $R$ be an integral domain and let $\hat F = \{(a,b) : a,b\in R,\ b\ne 0\}$, with $(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ meaning $ad = bc$.

(a) Prove Lemma 6.42 (p. 122): $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. Identify the one step that uses $R$ being a domain, and show that step fails in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.

(b) Prove Claim 6.44 (p. 122): the operations
$$\tfrac ab + \tfrac cd = \tfrac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad \tfrac ab\cdot\tfrac cd = \tfrac{ac}{bd}$$
are well defined on $F = \hat F/\!\sim$, and check $bd \ne 0$.

(c) Show $F$ is a field (Definition 6.43, p. 122) and that $\iota : R\to F$, $a\mapsto a/1$, is an injective ring homomorphism.

(d) Prove the universal property, Theorem 6.45 (p. 124): every injective ring homomorphism $i : R\to k$ into a field factors uniquely as $i = j\circ\iota$ for a ring homomorphism $j : F\to k$.

(e) Identify the field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}$, of a field $k$, and of $k[x]$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) For transitivity, from $ad = bc$ and $cf = de$ derive $d(af) = d(be)$ — and then you need to cancel $d$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) There is only one possible formula for $j$, forced by $j(a/1) = i(a)$ and $j$ respecting products.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Reflexive: $ab = ba$. Symmetric: $ad = bc$ gives $cb = da$. Transitive: from $ad=bc$ and $cf=de$,
$$d(af) = (ad)f = (bc)f = b(cf) = b(de) = d(be),$$
and since $d\ne 0$ and $R$ is a domain, cancellation (Proposition 3.26, p. 54) gives $af = be$.

In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ that step fails, and the relation really is not transitive. Take
$$([0],[1])\ \sim\ ([0],[2])\ \sim\ ([3],[1]).$$
The first holds because $[0][2] = [0] = [1][0]$; the second because $[0][1] = [0] = [2][3] = [6]$. But $([0],[1])\sim([3],[1])$ would need $[0][1] = [1][3]$, i.e. $[0]=[3]$, which is false. The middle term has a zero-divisor as its denominator, $[2]$, and that is exactly the $d$ the transitivity argument wanted to cancel.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $bd\ne0$ because $R$ is a domain — this is where the hypothesis appears a second time, and without it the proposed denominators would not even be legal. Well-definedness: replace $(a,b)$ by $(a',b')$ with $ab'=a'b$ and check $(ad+bc)b'd = (a'd+b'c)bd$; expanding, both sides are $ab'd^2 + bb'cd$ after using $ab'=a'b$. The product is the shorter computation: $(ac)(b'd') = (a'c')(bd)$ follows by multiplying $ab'=a'b$ and $cd'=c'd$.

(c) The ring axioms are inherited computation by computation. $0_F = 0/1$ and $1_F = 1/1$, and these differ because $R$ is nonzero. If $a/b\ne 0_F$ then $a\ne 0$, so $b/a$ is a legal fraction and $(a/b)(b/a) = ab/ab = 1_F$. Injectivity of $\iota$: $a/1 = 0/1$ means $a\cdot 1 = 0$, so $a=0$.

(d) *Uniqueness first.* If $i = j\circ\iota$ then $j(a/1) = i(a)$, and since $a/b = (a/1)(b/1)^{-1}$ in $F$, $j(a/b) = i(a)i(b)^{-1}$ — which is legal in $k$ because $b\ne0$ and $i$ is injective, so $i(b)\ne0$. So there is at most one $j$.

*Existence.* Define $j(a/b) = i(a)i(b)^{-1}$. Well defined: if $ad=bc$ then $i(a)i(d)=i(b)i(c)$, so $i(a)i(b)^{-1} = i(c)i(d)^{-1}$. The homomorphism clauses are the usual fraction arithmetic in $k$, and $j(1/1) = 1_k$. Finally $j(\iota(a)) = i(a)i(1)^{-1} = i(a)$.

(e) $\mathbb{Z}$ gives $\mathbb{Q}$ — Remark 6.46 (p. 126) is the summary. A field $k$ gives $k$ itself, since $\iota$ is already surjective: $a/b = (ab^{-1})/1$. And $k[x]$ gives the field of rational functions, the quotients $f(x)/g(x)$ with $g\ne0$.

The universal property says the field of fractions is the *smallest* field containing $R$, in the precise sense that it maps uniquely into any other. That is the same shape of statement as Proposition 5.39 (p. 91) and aa-08 Problem 3(a): an object characterised not by what it is made of but by the maps out of it.
</details>
