## Problem 1 (easy–medium — Lagrange)

(a) Prove Lemma 12.2 (p. 267) and deduce Theorem 12.3 (p. 267): for finite $G$ and $H\le G$, $|G| = [G:H]\,|H|$, so $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

(b) Deduce Proposition 12.4 (p. 268): $|g|$ divides $|G|$, so $g^{|G|}=e$.

(c) Deduce Proposition 12.5 (p. 269): a group of prime order is cyclic.

(d) Show the converse of Lagrange fails: $\mathcal{A}_4$ has order $12$ and no subgroup of order $6$ — Example 12.6 (p. 269).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(d) A subgroup of index $2$ is normal, so it would be a union of conjugacy classes containing $e$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The class sizes of $\mathcal{A}_4$ are $1,3,4,4$; look for a union summing to $6$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $h\mapsto gh$ is a bijection $H\to gH$ with inverse $z\mapsto g^{-1}z$, so every coset has $|H|$ elements. The cosets partition $G$ (aa-26 Problem 1(a)), so $|G|$ is the number of cosets times $|H|$.

(b) $|g| = |\langle g\rangle|$, which divides $|G|$ by (a). Then $g^{|G|} = (g^{|g|})^{|G|/|g|} = e$.

(c) If $|G| = p$ and $g\ne e$, then $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $p$ and exceeds $1$, so equals $p$, and $G = \langle g\rangle$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(d) Suppose $H\le\mathcal{A}_4$ with $|H| = 6$. Then $[\mathcal{A}_4:H] = 2$, so $H$ is normal (aa-26 Problem 2(c)), hence a union of conjugacy classes containing $e$ (aa-26 Problem 3(a)). The class sizes of $\mathcal{A}_4$ are $1,3,4,4$ — Example 12.18 (p. 272) — and the subsets containing the class of size $1$ sum to $1$, $4$, $5$, $8$, $9$, $12$ and so on; **no union gives $6$**. So no such $H$ exists.

Lagrange says a subgroup's order divides the group's order; it does **not** say that every divisor occurs. $6$ divides $12$ and there is no subgroup of that order, and the class equation is what proves it. (For cyclic groups the converse does hold — aa-21 Problem 3(a) produced one subgroup per divisor — which is why the failure is easy to overlook.)
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — index is multiplicative)

(a) State Definition 12.1 (p. 267) and prove Proposition 12.7 (p. 269): for $H\subseteq K\subseteq G$ subgroups of a finite group, $[G:H] = [G:K]\,[K:H]$.

(b) Give a proof that does not assume $G$ finite.

(c) Try to use (a) to redo Problem 1(d) — the nonexistence of a subgroup of order $6$ in $\mathcal{A}_4$ — without conjugacy classes, and explain precisely why it cannot be done. Which relation does index multiplicativity give for a hypothetical $H$ of order $6$, and why is that relation perfectly consistent? Name the extra ingredient Problem 1(d) uses that no index arithmetic can supply.

(d) Deduce that if $[G:H]$ is prime then there is no subgroup strictly between $H$ and $G$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) With $G$ finite it is arithmetic: divide $|G| = [G:H]|H|$ by $|K| = [K:H]|H|$.

(c) Every index the hypothetical $H$ would produce is an integer, and integrality is all Lagrange and (a) can test.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Each coset of $K$ in $G$ splits into $[K:H]$ cosets of $H$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By Theorem 12.3, $|G| = [G:K]|K|$ and $|K| = [K:H]|H|$, so $|G| = [G:K][K:H]|H|$; comparing with $|G| = [G:H]|H|$ and cancelling $|H|$ gives the claim.

(b) Choose coset representatives $g_i$ for $K$ in $G$ and $k_j$ for $H$ in $K$. Then every element of $G$ lies in exactly one $g_iK$, and within $g_iK$ in exactly one $g_ik_jH$. So the $g_ik_jH$ are distinct and exhaust $G$, giving $[G:H] = [G:K][K:H]$ as cardinals — no finiteness used.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The attempt, and why it stops. A subgroup $H$ of order $6$ would have $[\mathcal{A}_4:H] = |\mathcal{A}_4|/|H| = 12/6 = 2$, and $[\mathcal{A}_4:H]\,|H| = 2\cdot6 = 12$ ✓. Every index in sight is a positive integer and every product checks out: the arithmetic is entirely consistent with a subgroup of order $6$ existing, and (a) adds nothing, since chaining through an intermediate subgroup only ever factors that same $2$.

That is the answer. Index multiplicativity, like Lagrange itself, only tests **divisibility**, and $6$ divides $12$; no amount of index arithmetic can exclude a subgroup whose order is a legal divisor. The extra ingredient in Problem 1(d) is structural and has no arithmetic proxy: a subgroup of index $2$ is *normal*, a normal subgroup is a *union of conjugacy classes* (Proposition 11.57), and the class sizes of $\mathcal{A}_4$ are $1,3,4,4$, from which no sub-collection containing the identity's class sums to $6$. Divisibility permits the subgroup; conjugacy forbids it.

This is exactly why the converse of Lagrange is false, and $\mathcal{A}_4$ at order $6$ is the standard witness — see aa-29's Example 12.6.

(d) If $H\subseteq K\subseteq G$ then $[G:H] = [G:K][K:H]$, a factorisation of a prime, so one factor is $1$ — meaning $K = G$ or $K = H$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — orbit and stabiliser, formally)

(a) State Definition 12.8 (p. 270) and prove that $\operatorname{Stab}(a)$ is a subgroup.

(b) Prove Lemma 12.9 (p. 270): there is a bijection between the orbit of $a$ and the set $G/\operatorname{Stab}(a)$ of left cosets.

(c) Deduce Theorem 12.10 (p. 270): $|O_a| = [G:\operatorname{Stab}(a)]$, and hence $|O_a|$ divides $|G|$ for finite $G$.

(d) Apply to the rotation group of the cube acting on faces, edges and vertices, and check the three counts agree.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
This is aa-24 Problem 5 with Aluffi's numbering attached; if you proved it there, check your proof against Lemma 12.9.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $ga = ha$ if and only if $h^{-1}g\in\operatorname{Stab}(a)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $e$ fixes $a$. If $g,h$ fix $a$ then $gh^{-1}a = g(h^{-1}a) = ga = a$, using $h^{-1}a = a$, so Proposition 11.7 (p. 232) applies.

(b) Send $g\operatorname{Stab}(a)\mapsto ga$. The chain
$$ga = ha\iff h^{-1}ga = a\iff h^{-1}g\in\operatorname{Stab}(a)\iff g\operatorname{Stab}(a) = h\operatorname{Stab}(a)$$
runs both ways, giving well-definedness and injectivity at once; surjectivity is the definition of the orbit.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) By (b) the orbit and the coset set have the same size, and that size is $[G:\operatorname{Stab}(a)]$ by Definition 12.1. For finite $G$, Theorem 12.3 makes it a divisor of $|G|$.

(d) The rotation group $G$ acts transitively on the $6$ faces with stabiliser of order $4$; on the $12$ edges with stabiliser of order $2$; on the $8$ vertices with stabiliser of order $3$. So
$$|G| = 6\cdot4 = 12\cdot2 = 8\cdot3 = 24 .$$
Three independent computations of the same number, which is the standard way to be confident about a symmetry count: pick a different orbit and see whether the answer moves.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — p-groups and fixed points)

(a) State Definition 12.14 (p. 271) and prove Corollary 12.15 (p. 271): if a $p$-group acts on a finite set $A$ with fixed-point set $F$, then $|F|\equiv|A|\bmod p$.

(b) Deduce Example 12.13 (p. 271): every action of $D_8$ on a set of odd size has a fixed point.

(c) Deduce Example 12.12 (p. 271): an action of $C_{35}$ on a $4$-element set fixes every point.

(d) Where does (a) use that $|G|$ is a prime power, and what is the statement for general finite $G$?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Split $A$ into orbits; the non-fixed orbits have size divisible by $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $C_{35}$ is not a $p$-group; argue from orbit sizes directly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The orbits partition $A$ (aa-24 Problem 2(b)), so $|A| = \sum|O_i|$. Each $|O_i|$ divides $|G| = p^r$ by Theorem 12.10, so each is a power of $p$; and $|O_i| = 1$ exactly when the point is fixed. So modulo $p$ every orbit of size $>1$ contributes $0$, leaving $|A|\equiv|F|$.

(b) $|D_8| = 8 = 2^3$, a $2$-group. If $|A|$ is odd then $|F|\equiv|A|\not\equiv0\bmod2$, so $|F|\ne0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $|C_{35}| = 35 = 5\cdot7$, which is *not* a prime power, so (a) does not apply. Argue directly: each orbit size divides $35$, so is $1$, $5$, $7$ or $35$. The orbits partition a set of size $4$, so no orbit can have size $5$ or more. Hence every orbit has size $1$ and every point is fixed.

(d) (a) uses the prime power exactly once, to know that every orbit size is either $1$ or divisible by $p$. For general finite $G$ the correct statement is the one used in (c): **orbit sizes divide $|G|$**, and any conclusion has to come from arithmetic on the divisors of $|G|$ against $|A|$. The $p$-group case is special because "divides $p^r$ and exceeds $1$" already implies "divisible by $p$" — a coincidence of prime powers that gives Corollary 12.15 its clean congruence.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the class equation)

(a) State Definition 12.19 (p. 272) and Definition 12.20 (p. 272), and prove Corollary 12.17 (p. 272): the number of conjugates of $a$ divides $|G|$, and equals $[G:Z_G(a)]$.

(b) Prove Theorem 12.21 (p. 272), the class equation: $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G:Z_G(a_i)]$, the sum over one representative $a_i$ of each conjugacy class of size $>1$.

(c) Write the class equation for $\mathcal{A}_4$ and for $D_8$.

(d) Which term of the class equation collects the classes of size $1$, and why is that the centre?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Apply Theorem 12.10 to the conjugation action; the stabiliser of $a$ is $Z_G(a)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) The classes partition $G$; separate those of size 1 from the rest.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $G$ acts on itself by conjugation (aa-24 Problem 6). The orbit of $a$ is its conjugacy class and the stabiliser is $\{g : gag^{-1}=a\} = \{g : ga=ag\} = Z_G(a)$. Theorem 12.10 gives $|{\rm class}(a)| = [G:Z_G(a)]$, which divides $|G|$ by Theorem 12.3.

(b) The conjugacy classes partition $G$. A class has size $1$ exactly when $gag^{-1}=a$ for every $g$, i.e. $a\in Z(G)$. Summing sizes:
$$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i|{\rm class}(a_i)| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i[G:Z_G(a_i)],$$
over representatives of the classes with more than one element.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\mathcal{A}_4$: class sizes $1,3,4,4$ — Example 12.18 (p. 272) — so
$$12 = 1 + 3 + 4 + 4,$$
with $|Z(\mathcal{A}_4)| = 1$: the centre is trivial.

$D_8$: class sizes $1,1,2,2,2$ from aa-24 Problem 6(d), so
$$8 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 2,$$
with $|Z(D_8)| = 2$ collecting the two singleton classes.

(d) The term $|Z(G)|$. A class has one element precisely when conjugation fixes it, i.e. when the element commutes with everything — so the singleton classes are exactly the central elements, and there are $|Z(G)|$ of them. That identification is the whole reason the class equation says anything: it converts a statement about class sizes, which are indices, into a statement about the centre, which is a subgroup.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — p-groups have a centre, and order p² is abelian)

(a) Prove Corollary 12.22 (p. 273): a nontrivial $p$-group has nontrivial centre.

(b) Prove Corollary 12.23 (p. 273): a group of order $p^2$ is abelian.

(c) Classify the groups of order $p^2$ up to isomorphism.

(d) Show (a) fails without the prime-power hypothesis, and (b) fails for order $p^3$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) In the class equation, $|G|$ and every $[G:Z_G(a_i)] > 1$ are divisible by $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Combine (a) with aa-27 Problem 6.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $|G| = p^r$ with $r\ge1$. In the class equation each $[G:Z_G(a_i)]$ divides $p^r$ and exceeds $1$, so is divisible by $p$; and $|G|$ is divisible by $p$. Rearranging,
$$|Z(G)| = |G| - \sum_i[G:Z_G(a_i)],$$
a difference of multiples of $p$, so $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$. Since $|Z(G)|\ge1$, it is at least $p$ — in particular not $1$.

(b) $|G| = p^2$ and $Z(G)\ne\{e\}$ by (a), so $|Z(G)|\in\{p,p^2\}$ by Lagrange. If $|Z(G)| = p$ then $[G:Z(G)] = p$ is prime, so $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic (Proposition 12.5, p. 269) and $G$ is abelian by aa-27 Problem 6(a) — whence $Z(G) = G$, contradicting $|Z(G)|=p$. So $|Z(G)| = p^2$ and $G$ is abelian.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
This closes the gap left open in aa-27 Problem 6(c), which assumed exactly (a) and named it as the missing step. It is supplied here by the class equation, and by nothing else in the module.

(c) By (b), $G$ is abelian of order $p^2$, so aa-22 Theorem 10.18 applies: the invariant-factor chains with product $p^2$ are $(p^2)$ and $(p,p)$. So
$$G\cong C_{p^2}\qquad\text{or}\qquad G\cong C_p\times C_p,$$
**two groups**, matching $p(2) = 2$ from aa-22 Problem 2(b). At $p=2$ these are $C_4$ and $C_2\times C_2$, the two groups of order $4$ found by hand in aa-23 Problem 6(c).

(d) *(a) without prime powers:* $S_3$ has order $6$ and trivial centre — its class sizes are $1,3,2$ and only the identity is central. The class-equation argument breaks because $3$ and $2$ are not both divisible by any single prime dividing $6$.

*(b) at order $p^3$:* $D_8$ has order $2^3$ and is not abelian. Consistently with (a), its centre is nontrivial — of order $2$ — and $D_8/Z(D_8)\cong C_2\times C_2$ is not cyclic, so aa-27 Problem 6(a) does not apply. The argument in (b) used $p^2$ in exactly one place, to force $[G:Z(G)]$ to be $1$ or $p$; at $p^3$ the index can be $p^2$, and $D_8$ is what that looks like.
</details>
