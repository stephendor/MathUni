## Problem 1 (easy — sorting one ring into three kinds of element)

Work in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.

(a) List every unit, using Proposition 3.28 (p. 55) for the criterion, and give each one's inverse.

(b) List every zero-divisor in the sense of Definition 3.22 (p. 53), exhibiting for each a nonzero partner killing it.

(c) Check that $0$, the units and the zero-divisors together account for all twelve elements, with no element counted twice.

(d) Is $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ an integral domain (Definition 3.23, p. 53)? Answer with Example 3.24 (p. 53) and independently from your own list.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Proposition 3.28 says the units are the classes $[a]_{12}$ with $\gcd(a,12) = 1$. That leaves eight classes to sort by hand.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For each non-unit $[a] \ne [0]$, the partner to try is $[12/\gcd(a,12)]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\gcd(a,12) = 1$ for $a = 1, 5, 7, 11$. Each of these is its own inverse: $5^2 = 25 = 24+1$, $7^2 = 49 = 48+1$, $11^2 = 121 = 120+1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $[2][6] = [12] = [0]$; $[3][4] = [0]$; $[4][3] = [0]$; $[6][2] = [0]$; $[8][3] = [24] = [0]$; $[9][4] = [36] = [0]$; $[10][6] = [60] = [0]$. So the zero-divisors are $[2],[3],[4],[6],[8],[9],[10]$ — seven of them.

(c) $1 + 4 + 7 = 12$. ✓ No overlap: a unit cannot be a zero-divisor, since $ab = 0$ with $a$ invertible gives $b = a^{-1}ab = 0$ — which is Remark 3.32 (p. 56), and worth proving once rather than checking case by case.

(d) Not a domain: Example 3.24 says $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain exactly when $n$ is prime, and $12$ is not. Independently, $[3][4] = [0]$ with neither factor $[0]$, which is the definition failing directly.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — cancellation is the same thing as not being a zero-divisor)

Let $R$ be a ring and $a \in R$.

(a) Prove Proposition 3.26 (p. 54): if $a$ is not a zero-divisor, then $ab = ac$ implies $b = c$.

(b) Prove the converse: if cancellation by $a$ holds, then $a$ is not a zero-divisor.

(c) Prove Remark 3.32 (p. 56): every unit (Definition 3.27, p. 54) is a non-zero-divisor.

(d) Show the converse of (c) is false, using $\mathbb{Z}$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $ab = ac$ means $a(b-c) = 0$. (b) Take $c = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
All four parts are one or two lines. The content is in seeing that (a) and (b) make "non-zero-divisor" and "cancellable" two names for one property.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) From $ab = ac$, distributivity gives $a(b-c) = ab - ac = 0$. Since $a$ is not a zero-divisor, $b - c = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Suppose cancellation by $a$ holds and $ab = 0$ for some $b$. Then $ab = a\cdot 0$, so $b = 0$. Hence no nonzero $b$ kills $a$ from the right; the left-hand version is identical.

(c) If $a$ is a unit and $ab = 0$, multiply on the left by $a^{-1}$: $b = (a^{-1}a)b = a^{-1}(ab) = 0$.

(d) In $\mathbb{Z}$, the element $2$ is not a zero-divisor — $2b = 0$ forces $b = 0$ — but it is not a unit either, since $2b = 1$ has no integer solution. So (c) is a one-way implication, and the gap between the two is exactly what makes $\mathbb{Z}$ a domain and not a field.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — fields are domains, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is why the converse fails)

(a) Prove Proposition 3.31 (p. 55): every field (Definition 3.29, p. 55) is an integral domain.

(b) Where does the nontriviality clause of Definition 3.29 get used?

(c) Give the standard counterexample to the converse, and one more that is not $\mathbb{Z}$.

(d) Deduce from Theorem 2.15 (p. 32) that for $n > 1$ the ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain if and only if it is a field. Why does that not contradict (c)?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) If $ab = 0$ and $a \ne 0$, invert $a$. (d) is a restatement of the equivalence of Theorem 2.15's (ii) and (iii).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), any integral domain with a non-invertible nonzero element will do; polynomial rings are the other familiar source, though they arrive formally in aa-13.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $R$ be a field and $ab = 0$. If $a = 0$ we are done; otherwise $a^{-1}$ exists and $b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) A field is required to be nontrivial, i.e. $1 \ne 0$. Without it the zero ring would count as a field, and it is *not* an integral domain — Definition 3.23 asks for $1 \ne 0$ as well, so the implication would fail on that one object. The clause is not there to avoid an ugly example; it is there to make this proposition true.

(c) $\mathbb{Z}$ is the standard one: an integral domain in which $2$ has no inverse. A second is $\mathbb{Z}\times\{0\}$… no — that is not a domain. Take instead the ring of polynomials with real coefficients: it is a domain, and $x$ has no inverse, since a product of nonzero polynomials has degree the sum of the degrees and $1$ has degree $0$.

(d) Theorem 2.15 says that for $n > 1$, "no zero-divisors" and "every nonzero element invertible" are *both* equivalent to $n$ prime, hence to each other. This does not contradict (c) because it is a statement about the rings $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ only, and every one of them is finite. Problem 4 identifies finiteness as the whole reason.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — finiteness turns a domain into a field)

(a) Prove Proposition 3.33 (p. 56): a finite integral domain is a field.

(b) Identify the exact step that fails for $\mathbb{Z}$.

(c) Show that the same argument proves more: in a **finite** ring, every non-zero-divisor is a unit. (This is the observation of Remark 3.32, p. 56, run in the other direction.)

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Fix $a \ne 0$ and consider the map $x \mapsto ax$ from $R$ to itself. Cancellation makes it injective.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
An injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective. Then $1$ is in the image.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $R = \{r_1, \dots, r_m\}$ be a finite integral domain and $a \ne 0$. By Proposition 3.26 (p. 54), $ar_i = ar_j$ implies $r_i = r_j$, so the list $ar_1, \dots, ar_m$ has no repetitions and therefore exhausts $R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a, cont.) In particular $1 \in \{ar_1, \dots, ar_m\}$, so $ar_i = 1$ for some $i$; commutativity makes $r_i$ a two-sided inverse. Hence every nonzero $a$ is invertible and $R$ is a field.

(b) Injectivity holds in $\mathbb{Z}$ perfectly well — the map $x \mapsto 2x$ is injective. What fails is "injective implies surjective", which is a property of finite sets and of nothing else. The image $2\mathbb{Z}$ misses $1$, and the argument stops there. This is the one place where finiteness enters, and it enters as a fact about sets rather than about rings.

(c) Nothing in the argument used that *every* nonzero element is cancellable — only that the particular $a$ is. So let $R$ be any finite ring and $a$ a non-zero-divisor; the map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective by Problem 2(a), hence surjective, hence $ax = 1$ for some $x$. Together with Remark 3.32 this says: **in a finite ring, unit and non-zero-divisor mean the same thing**, and every element is either $0$, a unit, or a zero-divisor — which is what Problem 1(c) observed for $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ without knowing why.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — which classes of ring survive which constructions)

(a) Let $R, S$ be nonzero rings. Show $R\times S$ is never an integral domain, however good $R$ and $S$ are.

(b) Characterise the zero-divisors of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ completely: show that for $n > 1$, the class $[a]_n$ is a zero-divisor if and only if $[a]_n \ne [0]_n$ and $\gcd(a,n) > 1$. Deduce Example 3.24 (p. 53).

(c) Count the units of $\mathbb{Z}/36\mathbb{Z}$ and list them.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is Problem 5(b) of aa-05 again, now with a name for what it shows. (b) One direction is Proposition 3.28 (p. 55) plus Problem 4(c); for the other, produce the partner explicitly.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) If $d = \gcd(a,n) > 1$, the partner to try is $[n/d]_n$, which is nonzero precisely because $d > 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(1_R, 0_S)$ and $(0_R, 1_S)$ are both nonzero — each coordinate ring being nonzero means $1 \ne 0$ there — and their product is $(0,0)$.

(b, $\Leftarrow$) Let $d = \gcd(a,n) > 1$ and put $b = n/d$. Then $1 \le b < n$, so $[b]_n \ne [0]_n$, and $ab = a(n/d) = n(a/d)$ is a multiple of $n$ since $d \mid a$. Hence $[a][b] = [0]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, $\Rightarrow$) Suppose $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. Then $[a]_n$ is a unit by Proposition 3.28 (p. 55). A unit is not a zero-divisor, by Remark 3.32 (p. 56). So a nonzero zero-divisor must have $\gcd(a,n) > 1$.

Example 3.24 follows: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero-divisors exactly when no $a$ with $0 < a < n$ shares a factor with $n$, i.e. exactly when $n$ is prime.

(c) $36 = 2^2\cdot 3^2$, so the units are the classes of integers divisible by neither $2$ nor $3$: $1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31, 35$ — twelve of them, which is $36(1 - \tfrac12)(1 - \tfrac13) = 12$. The remaining $24$ elements are $[0]$ and $23$ zero-divisors, and by Problem 4(c) that trichotomy is forced, not observed.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a ring where "not a unit" and "zero-divisor" come apart)

Let $R = C[0,1]$ be the set of continuous functions $[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$, with pointwise addition and multiplication.

(a) Check that $R$ is a commutative ring (Definition 3.20, p. 52), and identify $0_R$ and $1_R$.

(b) Show that $f \in R$ is a unit if and only if $f(x) \ne 0$ for every $x \in [0,1]$.

(c) Exhibit two nonzero elements of $R$ whose product is $0$, so $R$ is not an integral domain.

(d) Show that $f(x) = x$ is **neither** a unit **nor** a zero-divisor. Conclude that the trichotomy of Problem 1(c) fails here, and say which hypothesis of Problem 4(c) $R$ lacks.

(e) Show that $R$ has no nonzero **nilpotents**: $f^n = 0$ for some $n \ge 1$ forces $f = 0$. So $R$ has zero-divisors but nothing that is its own kind of degenerate twice over.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) If $f$ never vanishes, is $1/f$ continuous? (c) Build two "bumps" living on the two halves of the interval. (d) If $fg = 0$ and $f(x) = x$, what must $g$ be on $(0,1]$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c) take $f(x) = \max(0, \tfrac12 - x)$ and $g(x) = \max(0, x - \tfrac12)$. Both are continuous, neither is the zero function, and at every $x$ at least one factor is $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Each clause of Definition 3.1 is checked at one point at a time, and sums and products of continuous functions are continuous. $0_R$ is the constant function $0$ and $1_R$ the constant function $1$.

(b) If $fg = 1$ then $f(x)g(x) = 1$ for every $x$, so $f$ vanishes nowhere. Conversely if $f$ vanishes nowhere then $x \mapsto 1/f(x)$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and is an inverse.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) With $f, g$ as in the Strategy: $f(0) = \tfrac12 \ne 0$ and $g(1) = \tfrac12 \ne 0$, so neither is $0_R$; and for $x \le \tfrac12$ we have $g(x) = 0$ while for $x \ge \tfrac12$ we have $f(x) = 0$, so $fg$ is the zero function.

(d) $f(x) = x$ is not a unit, by (b), since $f(0) = 0$. Nor is it a zero-divisor: if $fg = 0$ then $xg(x) = 0$ for all $x$, so $g(x) = 0$ for every $x \in (0,1]$, and continuity at $0$ forces $g(0) = \lim_{x\to 0^+} g(x) = 0$ too. So $g = 0_R$.

Hence $R$ has an element that is nonzero, non-invertible and non-zero-dividing all at once — the trichotomy of Problem 1(c) is not a theorem about rings. What Problem 4(c) required was **finiteness**, and $R$ is very far from finite. Continuity is what does the work in (d): the same function on the ring of *all* functions $[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ **is** a zero-divisor, killed by the indicator of $\{0\}$.

(e) If $f^n = 0$ then $f(x)^n = 0$ for every $x$, and a real number with $t^n = 0$ is $0$. So $f = 0_R$. Zero-divisors and nilpotents are different failures: a nilpotent is an element that kills *itself* eventually, and $R$ has plenty of the first kind and none of the second — whereas $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has $[2]$, which is both.
</details>
