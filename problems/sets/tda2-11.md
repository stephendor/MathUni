## Problem 1 (easy — the dual data structure) [interleaves tda2-10]

(a) Define a cosheaf $\hat{\mathcal{F}}$ on a cell complex $X$: what is assigned
to cells, what is assigned to face pairs, in which direction does it go, and what
composition rule must hold? What is the data over a cell called?
(b) Write down Eq. (9.14), the value of a cosheaf over all of $X$. Say precisely
how it differs in *kind* from the sheaf-theoretic Eq. (9.2) — one is a subobject
of a product, the other is something else.
(c) Describe the skyscraper cosheaf $\hat{\mathbf{G}}_\sigma$ and compute its
value over $X$. State the contrast Ghrist draws with the skyscraper *sheaf*, in
terms of the face poset.
(d) Summarise Example 9.17 in two sentences: which of sensing and inference is a
sheaf, which is a cosheaf, and what are the stalks and maps in each case?

*(Ghrist §9.10, the cosheaf definition and the costalk and Eq. (9.14) p. 198; the
skyscraper cosheaf and Example 9.17 p. 199)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
An extension map runs $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\tau) \to \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)$ for
$\sigma \triangleleft \tau$ — from the bigger cell to its face.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), a sheaf's global sections are a *sub*object cut out by equations; a
cosheaf's are a *quotient* cut out by an equivalence.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For the skyscraper sheaf only minima of the face poset contribute globally; for
the skyscraper cosheaf, only maxima.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For a cell complex $X$, a cosheaf $\hat{\mathcal{F}}$ assigns to each cell
$\sigma$ an abelian group $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)$, and to each face pair
$\sigma \triangleleft \tau$ an **extension map** — a homomorphism
$\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma \triangleleft \tau) : \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\tau) \to \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma)$,
running from the coface to the face — composing properly: for
$\rho \triangleleft \sigma \triangleleft \tau$,
$\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\rho \triangleleft \tau) = \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\rho \triangleleft \sigma) \circ \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma \triangleleft \tau)$.
The data over a cell is the **costalk**.
(b) Eq. (9.14):
$$\hat{\mathcal{F}}(X) := \bigoplus_\tau \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\tau) \Big/ \sim \ , \quad s_\rho \sim \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\rho \triangleleft \sigma)(s_\sigma).$$
The difference in kind: Eq. (9.2) presents $\mathcal{F}(X)$ as a *subgroup* of the
product $\prod_\tau \mathcal{F}(\tau)$ — the tuples satisfying the compatibility
equations — whereas Eq. (9.14) presents $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(X)$ as a *quotient* of
the direct sum, by the equivalence identifying a datum on a cell with its
extension down to each face. Ghrist notes this is much harder to parse than the
global-sections intuition: a cosheaf over $X$ is an equivalence class of data
assignments, the equivalence being up to compatible extensions falling from
higher- to lower-dimensional cells.
(c) The skyscraper cosheaf $\hat{\mathbf{G}}_\sigma$ assigns $\mathbf{G}$ to
$\sigma$ and $0$ to all other cells. Its value over all of $X$ is zero unless
$\sigma$ has no cofaces — that is, unless $\sigma$ is locally top-dimensional — in
which case $\hat{\mathbf{G}}_\sigma(X) \cong \mathbf{G}$. The contrast: nonzero
global sections of a skyscraper *sheaf* exist only if the cell is minimal in the
face poset, that is a vertex; for a skyscraper cosheaf this is turned upside down
and only maxima in the face poset contribute globally.
(d) Sensing is a sheaf: to each sensor-vertex $x_i$ assign the vector space with
basis the sensed attributes $S(x_i)$, with restriction maps the inclusions into
the unions over the vertices of a simplex, so that the global sections
$\mathcal{F}(X)$ give the full sensorium. Inference is a cosheaf: to each vertex
assign the vector space whose basis is all objects consistent with $S(x_i)$, with
extension maps the projections onto the subbasis generated by intersection, so
that the global sections return the constraint satisfactions consistent with the
sensing.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — cosheaf homology)

(a) Write down the cellular cosheaf chain complex (9.15) and the boundary map
(9.16). In which direction does the grading move, and why is that the right
direction for this data structure?
(b) State what the cosheaf homology of the constant cosheaf is, and what the
cosheaf homology of a skyscraper cosheaf is. Compare the second with the
corresponding statement for skyscraper *sheaves* from tda2-10, and say which
earlier duality Ghrist says this is reminiscent of.
(c) State the exactness condition (9.17) that a precosheaf must satisfy to be a
cosheaf, and say what it amounts to in terms of $H_0$ of a nerve. What warning
does Ghrist attach to it?
(d) Give the two examples of cosheaves arising from functions in Example 9.18, and
the cosheaf of Example 9.19. In each case say what the extension map is.

*(Ghrist §9.10, the chain complex (9.15) and the boundary (9.16) and the constant
and skyscraper cosheaf homology p. 199; the duality remark and Example 9.18 with
Eq. (9.17) and Example 9.19 p. 200)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\partial(\tau) = \sum_{\sigma \leqslant \tau} [\sigma : \tau]\,\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma \leqslant \tau)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), the hint Ghrist gives is Poincaré duality: pass from functions to
functions *of compact support*.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The precosheaf condition says $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(U)$ agrees with
$H_0(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}); \hat{\mathcal{F}})$ for any cover $\mathcal{U}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) The complex is
$$\cdots \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } \bigoplus_{\dim\sigma=2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } \bigoplus_{\dim\sigma=1}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } \bigoplus_{\dim\sigma=0}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma) \longrightarrow 0$$
with $\partial(\tau) = \sum_{\sigma \leqslant \tau}[\sigma:\tau]\,\hat{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma \leqslant \tau)$.
The grading *decreases* by one, and $\partial^2 = 0$ as a boundary operator must.
That is the right direction because the extension maps themselves run downwards,
from a cell to its faces: the algebra has to follow the data. The result is the
cosheaf homology $H_\bullet(X; \hat{\mathcal{F}})$.
(b) For the constant cosheaf $\mathbf{G}_X$ the cosheaf homology agrees with the
cellular homology with $\mathbf{G}$ coefficients. For a skyscraper cosheaf
$\hat{\mathcal{F}}_\sigma$ the cosheaf homology vanishes unless $\sigma$ is a
maximal cell in the face poset — a cell of locally top dimension — in which case
it is $\mathbf{G}$ in grading $\dim\sigma$. The sheaf statement of tda2-10 was
that $H^\bullet(X; \mathbf{G}_\sigma)$ is $\mathbf{G}$ in grading $\dim\sigma$ and
zero elsewhere, with no maximality condition; the cosheaf version acquires one,
which is exactly the poset flip of Problem 1(c). Ghrist says this duality between
dimensions and sheaf cohomology / cosheaf homology on skyscrapers is reminiscent
of the delicacies of duality between homology and compactly supported cohomology
from Chapter 6.
(c) In the topological setting a **precosheaf** assigns data to open sets, with
extension maps $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(V) \to \hat{\mathcal{F}}(U)$ for $V \subset U$
composing appropriately. To be a cosheaf it must behave well under limits of
covers: the sequence
$$\bigoplus_{i,j}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(U_{ij}) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } \bigoplus_k \hat{\mathcal{F}}(U_k) \longrightarrow \hat{\mathcal{F}}(U) \longrightarrow 0$$
must be exact for every finite open cover $\mathcal{U} = \{U_i\}$ of $U$. In other
words $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(U)$ agrees with the cellular cosheaf homology
$H_0(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}); \hat{\mathcal{F}})$ for any cover $\mathcal{U}$.
Ghrist's warning: in practice this agreement *cannot* be assumed and can be
challenging to confirm.
(d) Taking the hint from Poincaré duality, the dual notions come from restricting
to functions of compact support. On a manifold $M$, the precosheaf of compactly
supported continuous functions $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(U) = C_c(U, \mathbb{R})$ with
extension-by-zero from $V$ to $U \supset V$ is a cosheaf; and the sheaf of
differential $p$-forms $\Omega^p$ is complemented by the cosheaf of $p$-currents
$\Omega_p$. Example 9.19: for $f : X \to Y$ with $Y$ locally connected, the
precosheaf assigning $H_k(f^{-1}(U))$ to $U \subset Y$ is a cosheaf, the extension
maps arising from the induced homomorphism on $H_k$; the cellular case is
analogous to the fibre homology sheaf of tda2-10's Example 9.3.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — categories) [interleaves cat-03]

(a) State the definition of a category: the three pieces of data and the two
axioms.
(b) Describe the arrow-diagram visualisation of a small category. What does
composition look like, and what does associativity look like? Which limitation
does Ghrist note for large categories?
(c) Give five of the eleven "most common" categories Ghrist lists, then the four
less sweeping ones: $\mathsf{Op}_X$, $\mathsf{Face}_X$, poset categories, and a
group as a category. In each of the last four, say what the objects and morphisms
are.
(d) Define a groupoid and a monoid in Ghrist's terms, and locate a group inside
each definition. Then state Example 10.1: what is the categorical $n$-simplex
$[n]$, and where does it get its morphisms?

*(Ghrist §10.1, the definition of a category and the remark on the word p. 208;
the arrow diagrams, the list of common categories, and $\mathsf{Op}_X$,
$\mathsf{Face}_X$, posets, groups, groupoids and monoids p. 209; Example 10.1
p. 209 continuing to p. 210)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A regular cell complex is a category $\mathsf{Face}_X$ whose morphisms are the
face relations.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), think about which simplex has a commutative triangle as its boundary,
and which has an associativity diagram as its wireframe.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
A group is a category with one object all of whose morphisms are invertible.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) A category $\mathsf{C}$ consists of (1) a collection of objects $\mathcal{O}$;
(2) for each ordered pair $(a,b)$ in $\mathcal{O}$, a set of morphisms
$\mathcal{M}(a,b)$; and (3) for each ordered triple $(a,b,c)$ a composition
$\circ : \mathcal{M}(a,b) \times \mathcal{M}(b,c) \to \mathcal{M}(a,c)$. These
satisfy: **associativity**, composition of morphisms is associative; and
**identity**, for each $a$ there is $\mathrm{Id} \in \mathcal{M}(a,a)$ with
$f \circ \mathrm{Id} = f$ for all $f \in \mathcal{M}(a,b)$ and
$\mathrm{Id} \circ g = g$ for all $g \in \mathcal{M}(b,a)$. Ghrist adds that the
word "category" is so generic as to be uninformative, is unrelated to the LS
category of Chapter 7, and was chosen to evoke an Aristotelian organisation.
(b) A small category is a directed graph: vertices are objects, and for each
ordered pair $a, b$ there is a set of arrows $\mathcal{M}(a,b)$ from $a$ to $b$,
with a loop-like identity arrow at each vertex. Composition is visualised by a
2-simplex whose boundary is the commutative triangle; associativity has an arrow
diagram best represented as the wireframe of a 3-simplex. The limitation: very
small categories are easily visualised, but categories with large sets of objects
and morphisms — singular chain complexes, say — demand too much of the Apollonian
seer. Ghrist observes that seeing composition and associativity in their
simplicial guise is the subject's first hint at its relevance to topology.
(c) Five of the eleven: vector spaces over $\mathbb{F}$ and linear transformations
$\mathsf{Vect}$; groups and homomorphisms $\mathsf{Grp}$; topological spaces and
continuous maps $\mathsf{Top}$; chain complexes and chain maps $\mathsf{ChCo}$;
posets and order-preserving functions $\mathsf{Pos}$. (The others are
$\mathsf{Ab}$, $\mathsf{GrAb}$, $\mathsf{hTop}$, $\mathsf{Man}$, $\mathsf{Set}$,
$\mathsf{FinSet}$.) The four less sweeping ones: $\mathsf{Op}_X$ has as objects
the open sets of a space $X$, including the empty set, with morphisms $V \to U$
the inclusions $V \subset U$. $\mathsf{Face}_X$, for a regular cell complex $X$,
has cells as objects and a morphism $\sigma \to \tau$ iff $\sigma \leqslant \tau$.
Any poset $(P, \leqslant)$ is a category with objects the elements of $P$ and a
morphism $a \to b$ iff $a \leqslant b$ — and the previous two are special cases. A
group $\mathbf{G}$ is a category with one object $\star$ whose morphisms
$\star \to \star$ are the elements of $\mathbf{G}$, composition being the group
operation, the group identity being the identity morphism, and every morphism
invertible.
(d) A **groupoid** is a category with invertible morphisms; a **monoid** is a
category with a single object. A group is a single-object groupoid, and equally a
monoid-with-inverses — so it sits in the intersection. Example 10.1: the
categorical $n$-simplex is the category
$[n] := 0 \to 1 \to 2 \to \cdots \to n-1 \to n$, whose objects are the ordinals
through $n$ and whose morphisms are induced by the total order; there are more
morphisms than the displayed generators, since composition must be applied.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — natural transformations) [interleaves cat-04]

(a) State the definition of a natural transformation $\eta : \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \mathcal{F}'$
between functors $\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}' : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}'$, and draw
the commutative square it requires. Then state Ghrist's slogan about what a
natural transformation "really" is.
(b) State Example 10.10. What are the objects and morphisms of $(\mathbb{R}, \leq)$
as a category, what is the translation functor $T_\varepsilon$, and what is the
natural transformation $\tau_\varepsilon$? What does naturality mean here in
words?
(c) State Example 10.11 and Example 10.12. In the first, which map is being
asserted to be natural, and between which functors? In the second, what is a
*natural isomorphism*, and what is the example?
(d) State Example 10.14. Why is $V \cong V^\vee$ not a natural isomorphism while
$V \cong (V^\vee)^\vee$ is? Then define isomorphic categories and equivalent
categories, and say which is rarer and what the analogy with topology is.

*(Ghrist §10.5, the definition and the Category-of-categories slogan and
composability and Example 10.10 p. 217; Example 10.11 and Example 10.12 p. 218;
Example 10.14 and isomorphic versus equivalent categories p. 219)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A natural transformation gives one morphism $\eta(a) : \mathcal{F}(a) \to \mathcal{F}'(a)$
per object, not per morphism.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), "not natural" is a precise statement: there is no natural isomorphism,
not merely that the obvious choice depends on a basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Equivalent categories have natural transformations $\mathcal{G}\cdot\mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}$
rather than equalities.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) A natural transformation $\eta : \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \mathcal{F}'$ connects
a pair of functors $\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}' : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}'$ by
sending each object $a \in \mathbb{C}$ to a morphism
$\eta(a) : \mathcal{F}(a) \to \mathcal{F}'(a)$ such that for each morphism
$h : a \to b$ in $\mathbb{C}$ the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc}\mathcal{F}(a) & \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{F}(h)\ } & \mathcal{F}(b)\\ \downarrow \eta(a) & & \downarrow \eta(b)\\ \mathcal{F}'(a) & \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{F}'(h)\ } & \mathcal{F}'(b)\end{array}$$
commutes. Ghrist's slogan: said better, a natural transformation is a functor in
the Category of categories — categories are the Objects of a Category whose
Morphisms are functors, and the Functors of that Category relate a pair of
Morphisms, which in the original category is precisely a natural transformation.
Natural transformations are composable, and one may also compose a natural
transformation with a functor on the left or the right.
(b) Example 10.10: regard $(\mathbb{R}, \leq)$ as a category with the reals as
objects and one morphism $a \to b$ whenever $a \leq b$. Translation by
$\varepsilon$ is a functor $T_\varepsilon : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ sending
$a \mapsto a + \varepsilon$ and preserving the order, hence morphisms. There is a
natural transformation $\tau_\varepsilon : \mathrm{Id} \Rightarrow T_\varepsilon$
from the identity, taking $(a \leq b)$ to $(a + \varepsilon \leq b + \varepsilon)$.
Naturality here is the indifference to whether one $\leq$-compares objects before
or after the translation.
(c) Example 10.11: the Snake Lemma's connecting homomorphism
$\delta : H_\bullet(\mathcal{C}) \to H_{\bullet-1}(\mathcal{A})$ is asserted to be
natural. Precisely, on the category $\mathsf{ChCoSES}$ of short exact sequences of
chain complexes, $\delta$ is a natural transformation between the two homology
functors to $\mathsf{GrAb}$ sending a short exact sequence to
$H_\bullet(\mathcal{C})$ and to $H_{\bullet-1}(\mathcal{A})$ respectively; this
naturality is what makes the long exact sequence powerful. Example 10.12: the
several homology theories of Chapter 4 are not merely isomorphic but *naturally*
so — there are natural isomorphisms between the cellular, singular, Morse and Čech
homology functors on the subcategory where both are defined, a **natural
isomorphism** being an inverse pair of natural transformations whose composites
both ways are the identity natural transformation. The content is that maps
between cell complexes induce the same homomorphism whichever theory is used.
(d) Example 10.14: on $\mathsf{FinVect}$ there is the dual-space functor
$\mathcal{D} : V \mapsto V^\vee$. Although $V$ and $V^\vee$ are isomorphic as
vector spaces, the isomorphism is not natural: there is no natural isomorphism
$\mathcal{D} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}$. It is true, however, that every $V$ is
naturally isomorphic to its double dual — there is a natural isomorphism
$\eta : \mathcal{D}^2 \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}$. Categories are *isomorphic*,
$\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{D}$, when there are functors
$\mathcal{F} : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{D}$ and $\mathcal{G} : \mathbb{D} \to \mathbb{C}$
that are inverses, $\mathcal{G}\cdot\mathcal{F} = \mathrm{Id}_\mathbb{C}$ and
$\mathcal{F}\cdot\mathcal{G} = \mathrm{Id}_\mathbb{D}$; this is rarely satisfied,
even for closely related categories. More common and more useful is the case of
*equivalent* categories $\mathbb{C} \approx \mathbb{D}$, where there are not
equalities but natural transformations
$\mathcal{G}\cdot\mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}_\mathbb{C}$ and
$\mathcal{F}\cdot\mathcal{G} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}_\mathbb{D}$. Ghrist's analogy:
this is like the way homotopic spaces are topologically equivalent though not
necessarily homeomorphic.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — interleaving, twice) [interleaves tda1-06 and tda1-07]

(a) Set up §10.6's situation: the family $\{X_t\}$, what the maps are, and what
$X_t$ is thought of as. State the definition of an $\varepsilon$-interleaving of
two $\mathbb{R}$-indexed homology sequences, including the condition on the
composites.
(b) Define the interleaving pseudometric. Ghrist gives two distinct reasons it is
not a metric — state both. Which two properties does he say do hold?
(c) State Theorem 10.15. Then give the categorical reformulation: define the
excursion-set functor $\mathcal{E}_h$, and state what an $\varepsilon$-interleaving
of two functors $\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}' : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ is,
including both composite identities.
(d) Reconstruct Ghrist's proof of Theorem 10.15. Then write a paragraph a reader
could use: exactly how far does the categorical definition generalise as §10.6

have to change to reach a multiparameter module indexed by $\mathbb{R}^d$?

*(Ghrist §10.6, the $\mathbb{R}$-indexed setting and the $\varepsilon$-interleaving
of homology sequences and the pseudometric p. 219; Theorem 10.15, the excursion-set
functor, the categorical $\varepsilon$-interleaving with
$(\eta'\cdot T_\varepsilon)\circ\eta = \mathcal{F}\cdot\tau_{2\varepsilon}$, the
interleaving distance on functors, and the proof p. 220)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The composites of the two interleaving maps must be the maps induced by the shift
$+2\varepsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), the proof is two set inclusions plus one sentence about functoriality.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathcal{E}_h(t) = \{h \leq t\} \subset \{h' \leq t + \varepsilon\} = \mathcal{E}_{h'}(t+\varepsilon)$
when $\varepsilon = \|h - h'\|_\infty$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Consider persistence over $\mathbb{R}$: a family $\{X_t : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$
of spaces with inclusion maps $X_a \subset X_b$ for $a \leq b$, thought of as the
lower excursion sets $X_t = \{h \leq t\}$ of a height function $h : X \to \mathbb{R}$.
Given two $\mathbb{R}$-indexed homology sequences $H_k(X_t)$ and $H_k(X'_t)$, they
are **$\varepsilon$-interleaved** if there exist homomorphisms
$\phi_t : H_k(X_t) \to H_k(X'_{t+\varepsilon})$ and
$\phi'_t : H_k(X'_t) \to H_k(X_{t+\varepsilon})$ such that
$\phi'_t \circ \phi_t$ and $\phi_t \circ \phi'_t$ are each the inclusion maps on
homology induced by the shift $+2\varepsilon$.
(b) An $\varepsilon$-interleaving implies the two sequences can differ
substantially only over short parameter intervals, which motivates defining
$\mathsf{d}(H_k(X_t), H_k(X'_t))$ to be the infimum of $\varepsilon$ over all
$\varepsilon$-interleavings. It is not a metric for two reasons: it may take the
value $+\infty$, if no interleaving exists at all; and two persistence diagrams at
interleaving distance zero need not be identical, since the infimum may be $0$
without a $0$-interleaving existing. Reflexivity and the triangle inequality do
hold, so it is a pseudometric.
(c) Theorem 10.15: given $h, h' : X \to \mathbb{R}$, the interleaving distance
between the sublevel set persistent homology sequences is bounded by the
$L_\infty$ norm of the difference of the height functions,
$\mathsf{d}(H_k(X_t), H_k(X'_t)) \leq \|h - h'\|_\infty$. Categorically: for
$a \leq b$ the inclusion $X_a \to X_b$ of sublevel sets defines an **excursion-set
functor** $\mathcal{E}_h : (\mathbb{R}, \leq) \to \mathsf{Top}$, and the sublevel
set persistent homology is the composition of this functor with $H_\bullet$. An
$\varepsilon$-interleaving of two functors
$\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}' : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ is a pair of natural
transformations $\eta : \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \mathcal{F}'\cdot T_\varepsilon$
and $\eta' : \mathcal{F}' \Rightarrow \mathcal{F}\cdot T_\varepsilon$ such that
$$(\eta'\cdot T_\varepsilon)\circ\eta = \mathcal{F}\cdot\tau_{2\varepsilon} \quad\text{and}\quad (\eta\cdot T_\varepsilon)\circ\eta' = \mathcal{F}'\cdot\tau_{2\varepsilon},$$
and the interleaving distance on functors $\mathsf{d}(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}')$
is the infimal such $\varepsilon$ — again a pseudometric that may take the value
$\infty$ for non-interleavable functors.
(d) Proof. Given $h, h' : X \to \mathbb{R}$, they define excursion-set functors
$\mathcal{E}_h, \mathcal{E}_{h'} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathsf{Top}$. Put
$\varepsilon = \|h - h'\|_\infty = \sup_x |h(x) - h'(x)|$. Then for every $t$,
$$\mathcal{E}_h(t) = \{h \leq t\} \subset \{h' \leq t + \varepsilon\} = \mathcal{E}_{h'}(t+\varepsilon)$$
— since $h(x) \leq t$ forces $h'(x) \leq h(x) + \varepsilon \leq t + \varepsilon$ —
and symmetrically
$\mathcal{E}_{h'}(t) \subset \mathcal{E}_h(t+\varepsilon)$. These inclusions are
the components of natural transformations $\eta, \eta'$ and give an
$\varepsilon$-interleaving of $\mathcal{E}_h$ and $\mathcal{E}_{h'}$. Finally, if
$\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{F}'$ are $\varepsilon$-interleaved then so are
$\mathcal{G}\cdot\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}\cdot\mathcal{F}'$ for any functor
$\mathcal{G}$, by functoriality; applying $H_k$ therefore yields an
$\varepsilon$-interleaving on homology with
$\varepsilon = \|h - h'\|_\infty$. $\square$ Paragraph: what §10.6 makes free is
the *target*. The definition of an $\varepsilon$-interleaving is stated for
functors $\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F}' : (\mathbb{R}, \leq) \to \mathbb{C}$ with
$\mathbb{C}$ an arbitrary category, so persistence modules ($\mathbb{C} =
\mathsf{Vect}$), excursion sets ($\mathbb{C} = \mathsf{Top}$) and anything else
one cares to index over the line are covered by one definition and one distance —
that is what makes tda1-06's interleaving distance a definition rather than a
construction. What §10.6 does *not* make free is the *index*: the source category
is $(\mathbb{R}, \leq)$ throughout, and the shift is the specific functor
$T_\varepsilon$ of Example 10.10 with its natural transformation
$\tau_\varepsilon$. To reach a multiparameter module indexed by $\mathbb{R}^d$ one
would have to replace the index category by $(\mathbb{R}^d, \leq)$ and the
one-parameter shift by translation along a vector, and then re-examine which
$\varepsilon$ the infimum ranges over; §10.6 does not do this, and tda2-04's
Definition 12.8 is where that version is actually set up. The right summary is
that §10.6 generalises the interleaving distance across all target categories, not
across all index categories.
</details>
