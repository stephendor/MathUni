## Problem 1 (easy — four candidates, one impostor)

Decide which of the following are metrics on $X = \mathbb{R}^2$, writing $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$.

(a) $d(x,y) = \sqrt{(x_1-y_1)^2 + (x_2-y_2)^2}$
(b) $d(x,y) = 1$ if $x \ne y$, and $d(x,x) = 0$
(c) $d(x,y) = \max\{|x_1-y_1|, |x_2-y_2|\}$
(d) $d(x,y) = |x_1x_2 + y_1y_2|$

*(Abbott §8.2, Exercise 8.2.1, p. 222)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Check the cheapest axiom first on (d): what is the distance from a point to itself?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For each candidate check all three conditions of Definition 8.2.1 in order. For (c), the triangle inequality follows from the one-dimensional one applied inside the maximum.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) fails at once: $d(x,x) = |2x_1x_2|$, which is nonzero whenever both coordinates are nonzero, so a point is at positive distance from itself. Nothing else needs checking.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Positivity and symmetry are immediate. For the triangle inequality, fix $i \in \{1,2\}$; then
$$|x_i - y_i| \le |x_i - z_i| + |z_i - y_i| \le \max_j|x_j-z_j| + \max_j|z_j-y_j| = d(x,z) + d(z,y).$$
Taking the maximum over $i$ on the left gives $d(x,y) \le d(x,z)+d(z,y)$.

(a) is the Euclidean metric and (b) is the discrete metric, both standard; verify (b) by the case analysis in the lesson's faded example.

The habit to build: check the axioms in the order positivity, symmetry, triangle, and stop at the first failure. The counterexample in (d) is one line, and looking for it before attempting a proof saves the whole exercise. Note also that "maximum over a finite set" is doing the work in (c) — the same step as an-07's Theorem 3.2.3, and it would fail for a maximum over infinitely many coordinates.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — metrics on a space of functions)

Let $C[0,1]$ be the continuous functions on $[0,1]$. Decide which of these are metrics on it.

(a) $d(f,g) = \sup\{|f(x)-g(x)| : x \in [0,1]\}$
(b) $d(f,g) = |f(1) - g(1)|$
(c) $d(f,g) = \int_0^1 |f - g|$

*(Abbott §8.2, Exercise 8.2.2, p. 222)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The condition that usually fails is "$d(f,g) = 0$ only if $f = g$". Two of these are fine; one is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), note first that the supremum is finite: an-11's Extreme Value Theorem makes $|f-g|$ attain a maximum on the compact $[0,1]$, so the "distance" is a real number rather than $\infty$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) is not a metric: take $f(x) = x$ and $g(x) = x^2$, distinct functions with $f(1) = g(1) = 1$, so $d(f,g) = 0$ with $f \ne g$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) is a metric. Finiteness is the point above; symmetry is clear; and if $d(f,g)=0$ then $|f(x)-g(x)| \le 0$ for every $x$, so $f = g$. The triangle inequality comes from taking suprema in $|f(x)-h(x)| \le |f(x)-g(x)| + |g(x)-h(x)|$, using that a supremum of a sum is at most the sum of the suprema.

(c) is a metric too, and the only delicate axiom is again the second half of positivity: if $\int_0^1|f-g| = 0$ with $f - g$ continuous and non-negative, then $f = g$. Prove it by contraposition — if $f(c) \ne g(c)$ for some $c$, continuity gives an interval around $c$ on which $|f-g| > |f(c)-g(c)|/2$, and the integral over that interval alone is positive.

Then observe what the exercise is really about. The metric in (a) is an-13's uniform convergence; the metric in (c) gives a genuinely different notion of two functions being close, and Lindström's Definition 3.4.3 remark applies — the same set of functions is complete under one of these metrics and not under another. The distance is part of the data, not a property of the set.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — neighbourhoods, and a surprise)

(a) Describe the $\epsilon$-neighbourhoods in $\mathbb{R}^2$ for each metric of Problem 1, and for the discrete metric on $\mathbb{R}$.
(b) Verify that an $\epsilon$-neighbourhood $V_\epsilon(x)$ is always an open set, and decide whether $C_\epsilon(x) = \{y : d(x,y) \le \epsilon\}$ is always closed.
(c) Find a metric space in which $\overline{V_\epsilon(x)} \ne \{y : d(x,y) \le \epsilon\}$.

*(Abbott §8.2, Exercises 8.2.7, 8.2.8 and 8.2.11, pp. 224, 225)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), the discrete metric with $\epsilon = 1$ is the whole answer.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), given $y \in V_\epsilon(x)$, the triangle inequality shows the ball of radius $\epsilon - d(x,y)$ around $y$ stays inside. That is the standard argument and it uses nothing but the axioms.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Euclidean: open discs. Maximum metric: open squares with sides parallel to the axes. Discrete metric: $V_\epsilon(x) = \{x\}$ when $\epsilon \le 1$, and the whole space when $\epsilon > 1$ — only two possible neighbourhoods, which is why so much collapses in that space.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Let $y \in V_\epsilon(x)$ and put $r = \epsilon - d(x,y) > 0$. If $d(y,z) < r$ then $d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z) < d(x,y) + r = \epsilon$, so $V_r(y) \subseteq V_\epsilon(x)$ and $V_\epsilon(x)$ is open by the definition copied from an-07. The set $C_\epsilon(x)$ is closed by a similar argument on the complement.

(c) In the discrete metric on an infinite $X$, take $\epsilon = 1$. Then $V_1(x) = \{x\}$, which is already closed, so $\overline{V_1(x)} = \{x\}$. But $\{y : d(x,y) \le 1\} = X$, since every distance is at most 1. The two sets differ by everything.

Say why this cannot happen in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and does happen here: in $\mathbb{R}^n$ every point at distance exactly $\epsilon$ is approached from inside the ball, and in the discrete metric there is no "inside" to approach from. The notation $\overline{V_\epsilon(x)}$ for "closed ball" is a habit imported from Euclidean space, and this exercise is where it should be unlearned.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — closure and interior are dual)

Let $E$ be a subset of a metric space $(X,d)$.

(a) Show $E$ is closed if and only if $\overline{E} = E$, and open if and only if $E^\circ = E$.
(b) Show that the complement of the closure is the interior of the complement, and the complement of the interior is the closure of the complement.

*(Abbott §8.2, Exercise 8.2.10, p. 225)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Everything is Definition 8.2.8 plus the definitions of open, closed and limit point copied from an-07. Do not import any picture of $\mathbb{R}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Prove the two statements in (b) by taking an arbitrary point and unwinding the definitions in both directions. Each is a chain of equivalences, so a single argument read forwards and backwards suffices.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) forward: if $E$ is closed it contains its limit points, so $\overline{E} = E \cup L(E) = E$. Backwards: if $\overline{E} = E$ then $L(E) \subseteq E$, which is the definition of closed.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $x \notin \overline{E}$ means $x \notin E$ and $x$ is not a limit point of $E$, which means some $V_\epsilon(x)$ meets $E$ in no point other than possibly $x$ — and since $x \notin E$, in no point at all. That says $V_\epsilon(x) \subseteq E^c$, i.e. $x \in (E^c)^\circ$. Every step reverses, so $(\overline{E})^c = (E^c)^\circ$. Replacing $E$ by $E^c$ and taking complements gives the other identity.

Then say what has been shown at the structural level, since it is the point of the exercise. Closure and interior are exchanged by complementation, exactly as an-07's Theorem 3.2.13 exchanged closed and open — and the proof here is an-07's proof with $|x-y|$ replaced by $d(x,y)$ and nothing else changed. Confirming that a proof transfers by inspecting it, rather than by hoping, is the skill this unit is testing.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — Cauchy in one metric, not in another)

(a) In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the discrete metric of Problem 1(b), describe the Cauchy sequences and decide whether the space is complete.
(b) Show that $C[0,1]$ under the metric $d(f,g) = \int_0^1|f-g|$ is *not* complete, by exhibiting a Cauchy sequence of continuous functions whose only plausible limit is discontinuous.

*(Abbott §8.2, Exercise 8.2.5, p. 223)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), an-13 already supplied a sequence of continuous functions with a discontinuous pointwise limit. Compute the integral distance between its terms.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), apply the definition with $\epsilon = 1/2$ and use that the only distance below $1/2$ is $0$. For (b), take piecewise-linear ramps steepening around a fixed point and show the areas between successive terms tend to zero. Ruling out a continuous limit is the half that needs care: integral-metric convergence says nothing about the value at any single point, so the argument must go through continuity, and continuity turns one bad point into a whole interval of area.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A sequence is Cauchy exactly when it is eventually constant, and such a sequence converges to that constant value. So the discrete space is complete — and it is also bounded and closed in itself and not compact, which is the lesson's counterexample to Heine-Borel.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Let $f_n$ be $0$ on $[0, 1/2]$, $1$ on $[1/2 + 1/n, 1]$, and linear in between. Each $f_n$ is continuous. For $m > n$, the two graphs differ only on an interval of length at most $1/n$ and by at most 1, so $d(f_n, f_m) = \int_0^1|f_n - f_m| \le 1/n$, and the sequence is Cauchy in this metric.

Now suppose some continuous $f$ had $d(f_n, f) \to 0$. The conclusion to reach is that $f$ agrees with $0$ on $[0,1/2]$ and with $1$ on each $[1/2+\delta, 1]$ — which is impossible for a function continuous at $1/2$. But do **not** get there by saying that $\int_0^1 |f_n - f| \to 0$ makes $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ at each $x$. It does not. The integral can be driven to zero while the values at a point head somewhere else entirely: let $g_n$ be $1$ at $0$, $0$ on $[1/n, 1]$ and linear between, so that $d(g_n, 0) = 1/(2n) \to 0$ while $g_n(0) = 1$ for every $n$. Here $(g_n)$ converges to the zero function in the integral metric, and $g_n(0) \to 1 \ne 0$ — so integral convergence to a limit does not force pointwise convergence *to that limit*. Convergence in this metric constrains an *area*, and an area is blind to any one point.

What rescues the argument is that $f$ is assumed continuous, so disagreeing at a point costs area on a whole interval.

**Left half.** Suppose $f(c) \ne 0$ for some $c \in [0,1/2]$, and put $\epsilon = |f(c)|/2 > 0$. Continuity at $c$ gives an interval $I \subseteq [0,1/2]$ of some positive length $L$, containing $c$, on which $|f| \ge \epsilon$. Every $f_n$ is identically $0$ on $[0,1/2] \supseteq I$, so
$$d(f_n, f) = \int_0^1 |f_n - f| \ \ge\ \int_I |f| \ \ge\ \epsilon L > 0$$
for **every** $n$ — the distance is bounded away from zero, contradicting $d(f_n, f) \to 0$. So $f \equiv 0$ on $[0,1/2]$.

**Right half.** Fix $\delta > 0$ and suppose $f(c) \ne 1$ for some $c \in [1/2+\delta, 1]$, with $\epsilon = |f(c) - 1|/2 > 0$. Continuity gives an interval $I \subseteq [1/2+\delta, 1]$ of positive length $L$ on which $|f - 1| \ge \epsilon$. Here the bound needs large $n$: once $n > 1/\delta$ we have $f_n \equiv 1$ on $[1/2 + 1/n, 1] \supseteq I$, and for those $n$
$$d(f_n, f) \ \ge\ \int_I |1 - f| \ \ge\ \epsilon L > 0 .$$
Again the distance cannot tend to $0$. So $f \equiv 1$ on $[1/2+\delta, 1]$ for every $\delta > 0$, hence on all of $(1/2, 1]$.

**The contradiction.** $f(1/2) = 0$ from the left half, while $f(x) = 1$ for every $x > 1/2$ from the right, so $f(x) \to 1 \ne 0 = f(1/2)$ as $x \to 1/2^+$. That is exactly a failure of continuity at $1/2$, and $f$ was assumed continuous. No continuous limit exists, and the space is not complete.

The pair of results is the exercise's real content: the same set $C[0,1]$ is complete under the sup metric of Problem 2(a) and incomplete under the integral metric here. Completeness belongs to the metric, not to the set. State that explicitly, and connect it to Lindström's Definition 3.4.3, which makes the same point with three metrics on this very set.

Get the sup-metric half right, though, because it takes two theorems and not one. A sup-metric Cauchy sequence in $C[0,1]$ satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem 6.2.5, which returns a function $f$ that $(f_n)$ converges to *uniformly*. That is not yet completeness. Completeness requires the limit to land back inside $C[0,1]$, and Theorem 6.2.5 says nothing whatever about continuity. Theorem 6.2.6 supplies precisely the missing step — a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous — so $f \in C[0,1]$, and only now is the space complete. Quoting 6.2.5 alone proves the sequence has a limit somewhere, which is not the claim. *(Abbott §6.2, Theorem 6.2.5, Theorem 6.2.6, p. 160)*

That also sharpens the contrast with (b), and the difference is not that one argument is harder. In the integral metric the analogue of Theorem 6.2.6 is simply false: the limit exists as a function and fails to be continuous, so it is not in the space. Both halves turn on the same question — does the limit come home? — and the metric is what decides.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — compactness without Heine-Borel)

In a metric space $(X,d)$, a set $K$ has the *open covering property* if every open covering of $K$ has a finite subcovering.

(a) Show directly from the definitions that a compact set is closed and bounded.
(b) Give an example of a closed, bounded, complete metric space that is not compact.
(c) Show that a set with the open covering property is bounded, and explain why this makes the covering property a strictly better notion of compactness to generalise than "closed and bounded".

*(Abbott §8.2, Definition 8.2.7, p. 224; Lindström §3.6, Definition 3.6.1, printed 69, and Theorem 3.6.4, printed 70)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), cover $K$ by the balls of radius 1 around each of its points, and see what a finite subcover forces.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) is the lesson's guided proof. (b) is the discrete metric on an infinite set. For (c), a finite union of balls of radius 1 is bounded by the triangle inequality, since the finitely many centres are within a bounded distance of each other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The collection $\{V_1(x) : x \in K\}$ is an open covering of $K$, since each $x$ lies in its own ball. A finite subcovering $V_1(x_1), \dots, V_1(x_n)$ gives, for any $a, b \in K$, indices $i, j$ with $d(a, x_i) < 1$ and $d(b, x_j) < 1$, whence
$$d(a,b) \le d(a,x_i) + d(x_i,x_j) + d(x_j,b) < 2 + \max_{i,j} d(x_i,x_j),$$
a bound independent of $a$ and $b$ because the maximum is over finitely many pairs.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For the final part of (c), the comparison to make is this. "Closed and bounded" is equivalent to compactness in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and fails in general, as (b) shows — so it is a coincidence of Euclidean space rather than a description of the phenomenon. The covering property is equivalent to sequential compactness in every metric space, by Lindström's Theorem 3.6.4, and it continues to make sense when the metric is discarded entirely and only the open sets are kept, which is what Semester 2 does.

Say also which fact of $\mathbb{R}$ the failed equivalence actually rested on. an-08 proved closed and bounded implies compact via Bolzano-Weierstrass, which came from the Axiom of Completeness, which is a statement about the order of $\mathbb{R}$. A metric space has no order, so the implication had no chance — and locating the exact hypothesis that a generalisation loses is more valuable than memorising which theorems survive.

Finally, note where this leaves Semester 1. Every definition of the last eleven units has been rewritten with $d(x,y)$ in place of $|x-y|$, and the mission's three spaces — point clouds, spaces of filtration functions, and spaces of persistence diagrams under the bottleneck distance — are all instances. Nothing in Semester 2 will require the real line again.
</details>
