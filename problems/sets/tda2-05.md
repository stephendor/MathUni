## Problem 1 (easy — Morse functions and matchings)

(a) State Definition 10.1, both conditions, and say in plain words what each one
forbids.
(b) Dey and Wang cite a result of Chari to the effect that the two sets in
Definition 10.1 are disjoint. Why does that matter — what would go wrong if a
simplex could be in both?
(c) State Definition 10.2 and explain how a discrete Morse function induces a
matching.
(d) A simplicial complex is given the function $f(\sigma) = \dim\sigma$. Verify
that this is a discrete Morse function and describe the matching it induces.

*(Dey §10.1, Definition 10.1 and Definition 10.2, pp. 290–291)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A facet of $\sigma^p$ is a $(p-1)$-face; a cofacet is a $(p+1)$-simplex
adjoining it.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), check that $f(\sigma^{p-1}) = p-1 < p = f(\sigma^p)$ always, so both
counted sets are empty.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Both conditions cap a count at $1$: at most one facet with value not less, and
at most one cofacet with value not greater.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 10.1: $f : K \to \mathbb{R}$ is a discrete Morse function if for
every $p$-simplex $\sigma^p$, at most one facet $\sigma^{p-1}$ has
$f(\sigma^{p-1}) \geq f(\sigma^p)$, and at most one cofacet $\sigma^{p+1}$ has
$f(\sigma^{p+1}) \leq f(\sigma^p)$. In words: $f$ may fail to increase with
dimension in at most one direction downwards and at most one direction upwards
at each simplex.
(b) Disjointness — Chari's result — is what makes the induced pairing a
*matching*. If a simplex could simultaneously be the low end of one exceptional
pair and the high end of another, it would appear in two pairs, violating
Definition 10.2's second condition, and there would be no well-defined
$\mu$.
(c) Definition 10.2: a set of ordered pairs $M = \{(\sigma,\tau)\}$ is a
matching if $\sigma$ is a facet of $\tau$ in each pair, and no simplex appears in
more than one pair. A Morse function induces one by pairing $\sigma^p$ with the
unique exceptional facet or cofacet when it exists; disjointness makes the
result well defined.
(d) With $f(\sigma) = \dim\sigma$, a facet has value $p-1 < p$ and a cofacet
$p+1 > p$, so both counted sets are empty and both conditions hold vacuously.
The induced matching is empty: every simplex is unpaired, hence critical. This
is the trivial DMVF that §10.2's cancellation procedure starts from.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — V-paths and acyclicity)

(a) State Definition 10.3, including what makes a $V$-path cyclic and what makes
a matching a *Morse matching*.
(b) State Fact 10.1 and Fact 10.2 and explain what the pair of them establishes.
(c) Dey and Wang observe that along a sequence as in (10.1) "the function values
on facets of the matched pairs strictly decrease". Use this to explain why
Fact 10.1 holds.
(d) State Definition 10.4. Which three sets partition $K$, and what is the
vector-field picture?

*(Dey §10.1, Definition 10.3, Facts 10.1 and 10.2, Definition 10.4, pp. 291–294)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A strictly decreasing quantity cannot return to its starting value.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Fact 10.1 and Fact 10.2 together say the two notions — Morse function and Morse
matching — determine each other, so one may work with whichever is convenient.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$V = C \sqcup L \sqcup U$ with $\mu(L) = U$ and $C$ the unpaired simplices.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 10.3: given a matching $M$, a $V$-path is a sequence
$\sigma_0 \prec \sigma_1 \prec \cdots \prec \sigma_k$ where for $0 < i < k$,
$\sigma_i \neq \mu(\sigma_{i-1})$ implies $\sigma_{i+1} = \mu(\sigma_i)$ — an
alternating sequence of facets and cofacets in which consecutive pairs alternate
between matched and unmatched. It is cyclic if $\sigma_0$ is a facet of
$\sigma_k$ or $\sigma_0 = \mu(\sigma_k)$; $M$ is cyclic if it contains such a
path, and acyclic otherwise. An acyclic matching is a *Morse matching*.
(b) Fact 10.1: the matching induced by a Morse function is acyclic, hence a
Morse matching. Fact 10.2: a Morse matching defines a Morse function. Together
they say the two notions determine each other, so one may define, compute and
reason with whichever is convenient — and in practice it is the matching, being
combinatorial.
(c) Along a $V$-path the function values on the facets of the matched pairs
strictly decrease. A cyclic path would return to its starting simplex, forcing
that value to equal itself after a strict decrease — impossible. Hence no
cyclic path exists and the matching is acyclic.
(d) Definition 10.4: a discrete Morse vector field is a partition
$V = C \sqcup L \sqcup U$ of $K$, where $L$ is the set of facets paired with a
unique cofacet in $U$ under a Morse matching with $\mu(L) = U$, and $C$ is the
set of unpaired simplices, called *critical*. Each pair $(\sigma, \mu(\sigma))$
is drawn as an arrow with tail in $\sigma$ and head in $\mu(\sigma)$, and the
critical simplices play the role of critical points of the field.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — what the critical cells compute) [interleaves at1-08]

(a) State Proposition 10.1, both the weak and the strong Morse inequalities.
(b) Deduce from the weak inequality that a DMVF with few critical simplices
bounds the Betti numbers, and explain why part (a)(ii) is an equality rather
than an inequality — what invariant is it computing?
(c) State Ghrist's Theorem 7.23 and explain what it adds to Proposition 10.1.
(d) Describe Ghrist's discrete flow $\Phi = \mathrm{Id} + \partial V + V\partial$
and state the property that distinguishes it from a continuous-time flow. Why is
that property what makes the theory computational?

*(Dey §10.1, Proposition 10.1, p. 293; Ghrist §7.8, the flow Φ and Theorem 7.23, pp. 149–150)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Part (a)(ii) is an alternating sum on both sides.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
An alternating sum of Betti numbers is the Euler characteristic, and an
alternating sum of cell counts computes the same thing.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\Phi^\infty = \lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi^n$ is constant for $n$ sufficiently large.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Proposition 10.1, with $c_i$ the number of critical $i$-simplices and
$\beta_i$ the Betti numbers: (a) weak — (i) $c_i \geq \beta_i$ for all
$i \geq 0$, and (ii)
$c_p - c_{p-1} + \cdots \pm c_0 = \beta_p - \beta_{p-1} + \cdots \pm \beta_0$
for $K$ of dimension $p$; (b) strong —
$c_i - c_{i-1} + \cdots \pm c_0 \geq \beta_i - \beta_{i-1} + \cdots \pm \beta_0$
for all $i \geq 0$.
(b) $c_i \geq \beta_i$ says the critical simplices are at least as numerous as
the homology they must generate, so a DMVF with few critical cells forces small
Betti numbers — and conversely a complex with large Betti numbers admits no
small DMVF. Part (a)(ii) is an equality because both alternating sums compute
the Euler characteristic, which is a topological invariant and therefore cannot
change when cells are cancelled in pairs of adjacent dimension.
(c) Ghrist's Theorem 7.23: for any discrete gradient field $V$,
$MH_\bullet(V) \cong H^{\mathrm{cell}}_\bullet(X)$ — the Morse homology built on
the critical cells is isomorphic to the cellular homology of the space. This is
strictly stronger than Proposition 10.1: the inequalities bound the counts,
while Theorem 7.23 says the smaller complex computes the homology exactly.
(d) $\Phi := \mathrm{Id} + \partial V + V\partial$ is a degree-zero chain map
whose iterations describe how cells descend along $V$. Unlike a continuous-time
flow, the discrete flow terminates: $\Phi^\infty = \lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi^n$ is
constant for $n$ sufficiently large. Termination in finitely many steps is
exactly what lets the limit be computed rather than approximated.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — cancellation, guided by persistence) [interleaves tda1-09]

(a) State Definition 10.5 and Definition 10.6. What is cancelled, and how is the
matching modified?
(b) State Proposition 10.2 and explain the role of the uniqueness hypothesis —
what goes wrong if two critical $V$-paths connect the same pair?
(c) State Proposition 10.3, and explain the sense in which persistence pairs are
a natural cancellation order.
(d) State Theorem 10.5 and Theorem 10.6, and say precisely what class of complex
each applies to and what each guarantees.

*(Dey §10.1.1, Definition 10.5 and Definition 10.6, pp. 293–294; §10.2.1,
Proposition 10.2 and Proposition 10.3, pp. 295–296; Theorem 10.5 p. 297;
§10.2.2, Theorem 10.6 p. 300)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Cancelling a pair of critical simplices reduces $c_i$ and $c_{i-1}$ each by one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), a matching must stay acyclic; reversing a path can create a cycle if
another path already runs the other way.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Theorem 10.6(a) gives $O(n\alpha(n))$ time, and (b) gives $O(n\log n)$ when the
filtration comes from a PL-function.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 10.5: given a DMVF $V = C \sqcup L \sqcup U$ induced by $M$, a
$V$-path $\pi : \sigma_0 \prec \cdots \prec \sigma_k$ is *critical* if both
$\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_k$ are critical. Definition 10.6: for such a pair
$(\sigma_0,\sigma_k)$ with a critical $V$-path $\pi$, the pair is *cancelled* by
modifying the matching along $\pi$ — shifting the matched pairs along the path
so that $\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_k$ become paired and the interior simplices
re-pair accordingly. The effect is to remove two critical simplices of adjacent
dimension.
(b) Proposition 10.2: after cancelling $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ via a critical
$V$-path, the new matching $M'$ remains a Morse matching if and only if that
$V$-path is the only critical $V$-path connecting them. If two such paths exist,
reversing one leaves the other running the opposite way, and the two together
close a cycle — so $M'$ is cyclic and no longer a Morse matching.
(c) Proposition 10.3: for a filtration of a 1-complex, taking the non-essential
vertex–edge persistence pairs in increasing order of the edges' appearance, each
cancellation in turn is legitimate. Persistence pairs are a natural order
because they pair exactly a creator with its destroyer, which is precisely the
relation a critical $V$-path realises — the persistence pairing is a ready-made
list of cancellable pairs, ordered by significance.
(d) Theorem 10.5: for a finite simplicial 2-complex whose underlying space is a
2-manifold without boundary, and a simplex-wise filtration $\mathcal{F}$,
starting from the trivial DMVF one obtains a DMVF by cancelling the vertex–edge
and edge–triangle persistence pairs given by $\mathcal{F}$. Theorem 10.6: for a
simplicial 1-complex or a simplicial 2-manifold $K$ with $n$ simplices, one can
compute (a) a DMVF by cancelling all persistence pairs from a given filtration
in $O(n\alpha(n))$ time, and (b) the same when the filtration is induced by a
given PL-function on $K$ in $O(n\log n)$ time.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — reconstructing a graph, and what is guaranteed)

(a) State Definition 10.7, both conditions (C-1) and (C-2), and explain what
each says about the density function. Why is $\beta > 2\nu$ required?
(b) State Theorem 10.9 and say what the complexity is measured against.
(c) State Theorem 10.12 and Proposition 10.13. Between them, what has been
proved about the output graph $\hat{G}$ — as a subset, and up to homotopy?
(d) State Theorem 10.14. Then write a short paragraph a practitioner could use:
given a noisy density field over a region, what must be checked before the
output of MORSERECON may be reported as a reconstruction of the underlying
network, and what exactly would be claimed?

*(Dey §10.4, Theorem 10.9 p. 307, Definition 10.7 p. 308,
Theorem 10.12, Proposition 10.13 and Theorem 10.14, pp. 310–312)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(C-2) requires the density inside $G^\omega$ to be at least $\beta$ and outside
at most $\nu$, with $\beta > 2\nu$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), the guarantees are conditional on the input assumption; the checkable
part is the noise model, and the conclusion is homotopy equivalence plus
containment, not equality.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Theorem 10.12: under the input assumption, $\hat{G} \subseteq G^\omega$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 10.7: a density $\rho : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is a
$(\beta,\nu,\omega)$-approximation of a connected graph $G$ if (C-1) there is an
$\omega$-neighbourhood $G^\omega$ of $G$ that deformation retracts to $G$; and
(C-2) $\rho(x) \in [\beta, \beta+\nu]$ for $x \in G^\omega$ and
$\rho(x) \in [0,\nu]$ otherwise, with $\beta > 2\nu$. (C-1) says the
neighbourhood has the same topology as the hidden graph; (C-2) says the density
concentrates around $G$, being significantly higher inside $G^\omega$ than
outside with only small fluctuation on either side. The condition $\beta > 2\nu$
is the separation that makes "significantly higher" precise: it guarantees the
lowest density inside exceeds the highest outside by more than the fluctuation,
so no thresholding decision is ambiguous.
(b) Theorem 10.9: the time complexity of MORSERECON is $O(\mathrm{Pert}(K))$,
where $\mathrm{Pert}(K)$ is the time to compute persistence pairings for $K$.
The complexity is measured against persistence computation itself — the
reconstruction is free relative to the pairing it consumes.
(c) Theorem 10.12: under the input assumption, the output satisfies
$\hat{G} \subseteq G^\omega$. Proposition 10.13: under the same assumption,
$\hat{G}$ is homotopy equivalent to $G$. Together: the reconstruction lies
inside the $\omega$-neighbourhood of the truth — so it is geometrically close —
and it has the right topology. Neither says $\hat{G} = G$.
(d) Theorem 10.14: for any deformation retraction
$H : G^\omega \times [0,1] \to G^\omega$ with $H(G^\omega,1) = G$, the
restriction $H|_{\hat{G}}$ is a homotopy from the embedded $\hat{G}$ to some
$G' \subseteq G$, where $G$ and $G'$ have the same first Betti number. For a
practitioner: before reporting, one must check the noise model — that the
density really does concentrate on an $\omega$-neighbourhood which deformation
retracts to the hidden graph, and that the separation $\beta > 2\nu$ holds — and
one must fix the persistence threshold $\delta$ that decides which critical
edges are kept. What may then be claimed is that the output lies within
$\omega$ of the true network and is homotopy equivalent to it, with the same
first Betti number; what may not be claimed is metric accuracy beyond $\omega$,
nor that individual edges of the output correspond to individual edges of the
truth. Dey and Wang's §10.5 applies exactly this to road networks and neuron
networks, which is where those caveats bite.
</details>
