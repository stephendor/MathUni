proves it in the body of §3.7. The same sentence is an exercise in one section and a proved lemma
in another: Lemma 3.7.11. If you did Exercise 8, you have already proved it; check
your argument against his.

**A warning about the word "complete".** It is the one word in this unit that
does *not* mean what its neighbours mean. "Closed" is relative — a set is closed
*in* a space, and the same set can be closed in one space and not in another.
"Complete" is absolute: it is a property of the metric space (A, d_A) considered
on its own, with no ambient space in sight. Proposition 3.4.4 is the bridge
between the relative notion and the absolute one, and it only builds that bridge
when the ambient space is complete to begin with. Problem 2 is about exactly
this.

(d) Lindström's Exercise 1 to §3.4: show that the discrete metric is always

(e) Lindström's Exercise 2 to §3.4: if $(X,d_X)$ and $(Y,d_Y)$ are complete, show

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — closed is relative, complete is absolute)

(a) Prove **Proposition 3.4.4**: if $(X,d)$ is complete and $A \subseteq X$, then
$(A,d_A)$ is complete if and only if $A$ is closed. Lindström proves both
directions at p. 60; reproduce them, and name the result from an2-01 that the
forward direction uses to place the limit inside $A$.

(b) One direction of Proposition 3.4.4 does not need $X$ complete and the other
does. Determine which, and prove the one that survives without the hypothesis:
*if $(A,d_A)$ is complete then $A$ is closed in $X$*, for an arbitrary metric
space $X$. Then give a two-line example showing the other direction genuinely
fails without completeness of $X$ — a closed subset of an incomplete space that
is not complete.

(c) Sort these into complete and incomplete, with a one-line reason each, all
with the metric inherited from $\mathbb{R}$: $[0,1]$; $(0,1)$; $\mathbb{Z}$;
$\mathbb{Q}$; $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$; $\{1/n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$;
$\{0\} \cup \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. For each incomplete one, exhibit the
Cauchy sequence and say which point is missing.

(d) Lindström's Exercise 3 to §3.4. For $A \subseteq X$ put
$\mathrm{diam}(A) = \sup\{d(x,y) : x,y \in A\}$. Let $A_{n+1} \subseteq A_n$ with

complete then $\{a_n\}$ converges. Then say what extra hypothesis you would need
to conclude that the limit lies in $\bigcap A_n$, and give a counterexample
without it.

(e) Lindström's Example 2 to §3.4 (p. 60) says that of the three metrics

state precisely what Example 2 shows about the relationship between a *set*, a
*metric on that set*, and completeness, and say which of the two an2-01 taught

## Problem 3 (medium — completeness is not a topological property)

This is the problem the unit exists for. an2-01 Problem 5(c) showed that the
Manhattan and Euclidean metrics on $\mathbb{R}^2$ are different functions with
exactly the same open sets. The obvious next question is which properties
survive that.

(a) Lindström's Exercise 4 to §3.4 calls $d_1$ and $d_2$ on a set $X$
*equivalent* if there are constants $K, M$ with $d_1(x,y) \leq K d_2(x,y)$ and
$d_2(x,y) \leq M d_1(x,y)$ for all $x,y$. Prove that if $d_1$ and $d_2$ are
equivalent in this sense and one of $(X,d_1), (X,d_2)$ is complete, so is the
other. Do it in two steps: show the two metrics have the same Cauchy sequences,
then show they have the same convergent sequences with the same limits.

(b) Check that the Manhattan and Euclidean metrics on $\mathbb{R}^2$ *are*
equivalent in that sense, with explicit $K$ and $M$ from your inequality chain
in an2-01 Problem 2(a). Conclude that both are complete.

(c) Now the question (a) does not answer. Equivalence in Lindström's sense is a
statement about the two *distance functions* — a pair of inequalities between
numbers. Generating the same open sets is weaker: it says only that each ball of
one kind contains a ball of the other **around the same centre**, with no uniform
constant. Decide, with proof or counterexample: *if $d_1$ and $d_2$ are two
metrics on a set $X$ with exactly the same open sets, and $(X,d_1)$ is complete,
must $(X,d_2)$ be complete?*

Do not decide this by how the sentence sounds. Test it on the smallest thing you
can, and if you look for a counterexample, look for a *bounded* distortion of an
unbounded space — the failure has to happen out at infinity, because on any
bounded region the two notions of smallness are hard to prise apart.

(d) If you found the counterexample, do this with it. Let $\rho$ be your second
metric on $\mathbb{R}$. Exhibit the explicit $\rho$-Cauchy sequence, prove it has
no $\rho$-limit, prove $\rho$ and the usual metric have the same open sets, and
then answer: which of Lindström's two inequalities, $d \leq K\rho$ or
$\rho \leq M d$, holds, and which fails? Locate the failure at a specific pair of
points as a function of the constant $M$.

(e) Draw the moral for the vocabulary. an2-01 concluded that "a property of a
metric space need not be a property of the metric" (three metrics, one topology).
State the converse moral this problem establishes, in one sentence, and then say
which of the following are topological and which are metric, justifying each from
what you have proved so far: *open*, *closed*, *convergent*, *continuous*,
*Cauchy*, *complete*, *bounded*.

*(Lindström pp. 62–63 for Exercise 4, which is set and not worked; p. 57 for
Example 1, from which the function in the intended counterexample is taken —
the counterexample itself is not in Lindström and is constructed here.)*

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) The two inequalities let you convert an $\epsilon$ for one metric into an
$\epsilon/K$ or $\epsilon/M$ for the other, uniformly in $x$ and $y$. Because the
constants do not depend on the points, the conversion works inside the
double quantifier of Definition 3.4.1 — which is exactly what a merely
topological equivalence cannot do.
(c)–(d) Put $\rho(x,y) = |\arctan x - \arctan y|$ on $\mathbb{R}$. That it is a
metric is immediate from injectivity of $\arctan$: it is the usual metric
transported along an injection. That it has the same open sets follows from
$\arctan$ and $\tan$ both being continuous. That it is incomplete is
$x_n = n$.
(e) The dividing line is whether the definition can be stated using only the
collection of open sets. "Cauchy" cannot: it compares $d(x_n,x_m)$ across two
running indices, and no open set knows the difference between "these two points
are within $\epsilon$" and "these two points are within $\epsilon'$".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c)–(d) **No.** Take $X = \mathbb{R}$, $d(x,y) = |x-y|$ and
$\rho(x,y) = |\arctan x - \arctan y|$.

*$\rho$ is a metric.* Positivity: $\rho(x,y) = 0$ iff $\arctan x = \arctan y$
iff $x = y$, since $\arctan$ is injective. Symmetry and the Triangle Inequality
are inherited from $|\cdot|$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

*Same open sets.* $\rho(x,y) < r$ iff $|\arctan x - \arctan y| < r$, so every
$\rho$-ball is the $\arctan$-preimage of an interval, which is an open interval
of $\mathbb{R}$ containing the centre; and conversely every open interval about
$a$ contains a $\rho$-ball about $a$, because $\arctan$ is continuous at $a$ with
a continuous inverse. So a set is $\rho$-open iff it is $d$-open.

*Not $\rho$-complete.* $x_n = n$. Since $\arctan n \to \pi/2$, the real sequence
$\{\arctan n\}$ is Cauchy, so $\rho(x_n,x_m) = |\arctan n - \arctan m|$ can be
made as small as we like, and $\{x_n\}$ is $\rho$-Cauchy. But for any
$a \in \mathbb{R}$, $\rho(n,a) \to \pi/2 - \arctan a > 0$, so $\{x_n\}$ has no
$\rho$-limit at all.

*Which inequality survives.* $\rho \leq M d$ holds with $M = 1$, since $\arctan$
has derivative $1/(1+x^2) \leq 1$ and the Mean Value Theorem applies. The other,
$d \leq K \rho$, fails for every $K$: take $x = n$, $y = n+1$, so $d = 1$ while
$\rho(n,n+1) \to 0$; any proposed $K$ is beaten once $\rho(n,n+1) < 1/K$.

(e) The converse moral: **a property of a metric need not be visible in the
topology it generates.** Topological — open, closed, convergent, continuous.
Metric — Cauchy, complete, bounded. (Boundedness is metric for the same reason:
$\rho$ is bounded by $\pi$ and $d$ is not, on the same topology.)
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — building the limits you were missing)

§3.7 constructs, for any metric space, a complete space containing it as a dense
subset. The whole construction is one idea: **if there is nothing to converge to,
make the approximating sequences themselves be the points.**

(a) State **Definition 3.7.1** (dense) and prove **Proposition 3.7.2**: $D$ is
dense in $X$ iff for each $x \in X$ and each $\delta > 0$ there is $y \in D$ with
$d(x,y) \leq \delta$. Lindström leaves this as Problem 1 to §3.7. Note the
$\leq$, and say whether replacing it with $<$ changes the statement.

(b) State **Definition 3.7.3** (completion) and prove **Proposition 3.7.4**: any
two completions of $(X,d_X)$ are isometric. Lindström's proof at pp. 72–73
constructs $i : Y \to Z$ by sending $y$ to the limit in $Z$ of any sequence from
$X$ converging to $y$. Three things must be checked and he checks all three —
list them, and say which one is the *well-definedness* check as opposed to a
property of the map.

(c) The three lemmas that do the real work. Prove **Lemma 3.7.5** (for Cauchy
$\{x_n\}, \{y_n\}$, the limit $\lim d(x_n,y_n)$ exists), **Lemma 3.7.6** ($\sim$
is an equivalence relation), and **Lemma 3.7.7** (the limit is unchanged by
replacing either sequence with an equivalent one). Lindström's proof of 3.7.5
uses **Proposition 3.1.4**, the Inverse Triangle Inequality from an2-01 — say
where, and why the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ is what closes the argument.

Then answer the structural question: Lemmas 3.7.5–3.7.7 occupy more than a page,
and the proof of **Lemma 3.7.8** — that $(\bar X, \bar d)$ is a metric space —
takes six lines. Explain why, in terms of which of the three conditions of
Definition 3.1.1 would be hard if the earlier lemmas had not been proved.

(d) Prove **Lemma 3.7.9** ($D = \{\bar x\}$ is dense in $\bar X$),
**Lemma 3.7.10** (every Cauchy sequence in $D$ converges in $\bar X$) and
**Lemma 3.7.11** (if $D$ is dense in $Y$ and every Cauchy sequence *from $D$*
converges in $Y$, then $Y$ is complete), and assemble **Theorem 3.7.12**. In
3.7.9, Lindström chooses $x = x_N$ for an $N$ from the Cauchy condition. Say why
the natural first guess — "choose $x$ to be the limit of $\{x_n\}$" — is
unavailable, and note that this is the same obstacle the whole section exists to
remove.

(e) Lindström notes at p. 75 that $x \mapsto \bar x$ is an *embedding* in the
sense of **Definition 3.1.3**, and on the same page that the final step is to
"replace
the elements $\bar x$ in $D$ by the original elements $x$ in $X$". State what
Definition 3.7.3(i) demands that makes this last replacement necessary rather
than cosmetic, and say why **Proposition 3.7.4** is what licenses calling the
result *the* completion.
