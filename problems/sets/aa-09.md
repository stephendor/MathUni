## Problem 1 (easy — four kernels)

Compute the kernel, in the sense of Definition 5.3 (p. 79), of each of the following ring homomorphisms, and say in each case what the answer is as a subset.

(a) $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $a \mapsto [a]_{12}$.

(b) $e_0 : R[x]\to R$, $f(x)\mapsto f(0)$.

(c) $\pi : R\times S\to R$, $(r,s)\mapsto r$.

(d) the unique homomorphism $\sigma : \mathbb{Z}\to R$ of aa-08 Problem 3(a), for an arbitrary ring $R$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The kernel is $\{a \in R : f(a) = 0_S\}$ — everything sent to zero, not everything sent to itself.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) $\sigma(n) = n\cdot 1_R$, so its kernel is the set of $n$ with $n\cdot 1_R = 0$. That set has a name from aa-07.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $[a]_{12} = [0]_{12}$ exactly when $12 \mid a$, so $\ker\varphi = 12\mathbb{Z}$. Aluffi's Example 5.4 (p. 79) is the general $n$.

(b) $f(0) = a_0$, the constant term, so $\ker e_0$ is the set of polynomials with zero constant term — equivalently the multiples of $x$. Example 5.5 (p. 79).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\ker\pi = \{(0_R, s) : s \in S\} = \{0_R\}\times S$. This is the subset aa-07 Problem 5(b) showed absorbs multiplication from both sides while missing $1$ — and Problem 2 below finally says what it is.

(d) $\ker\sigma = \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : n\cdot 1_R = 0_R\}$, which is precisely the set called $N$ in aa-07 Problem 6(c) and shown there to be $c\mathbb{Z}$, where $c$ is the characteristic of $R$. So the characteristic is not a separate invention: it is the kernel of the only homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to R$ there is.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the object four earlier problems were circling)

(a) Prove Proposition 5.9 (p. 81): a **nonempty** subset $I \subseteq R$ is an ideal if and only if it is closed under addition and satisfies absorption, $ra \in I$ and $ar \in I$ for all $a \in I$, $r \in R$.

(b) Where does closure under negation come from? It is one of the axioms Definition 5.8 (p. 80) demands, and Proposition 5.9 does not ask for it.

(c) Show that each of the following is an ideal, using (a): $2\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$; $\{(a,0) : a \in R\}\subseteq R\times R$; $\{f \in C[0,1] : f(0)=0\}\subseteq C[0,1]$; $\{0_R\}\times S\subseteq R\times S$.

(d) Show that $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ is **not** an ideal (Example 5.13, p. 81), and say which of the two conditions in (a) it fails.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Absorption with $r = -1_R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) All four appeared in aa-05, aa-06 or aa-07 as subsets that were closed under everything except containing $1$. Absorption is the extra property to check, and in each case it is one line.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) An ideal satisfies both conditions by Definition 5.8 and is nonempty since it contains $0$. Conversely, given the two conditions, take any $a \in I$ (possible as $I \ne \varnothing$); then $-a = (-1_R)a \in I$ by absorption, so $0 = a + (-a) \in I$, and the remaining additive axioms are inherited from $R$.

(b) From absorption itself, exactly as in (a). This is why Proposition 5.9 is a genuine simplification and not just a restatement: it removes a condition by deriving it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $2\mathbb{Z}$: closed under $+$, and $r(2k) = 2(rk)$. — $\{(a,0)\}$: closed under $+$, and $(r,s)(a,0) = (ra,0)$, $(a,0)(r,s) = (ar,0)$. — $\{f : f(0)=0\}$: a sum of two functions vanishing at $0$ vanishes at $0$, and $(gf)(0) = g(0)f(0) = 0$. — $\{0\}\times S$: aa-07 Problem 5(b), where both absorption directions were checked.

So all four subsets that kept turning up — closed under addition and negation, absorbing multiplication by anything, missing $1_R$ — are the same kind of object, and it is the kernel of a homomorphism. Aluffi's Example 5.10 (p. 81) previews the converse, and Proposition 5.35 (p. 89) proves it: **every** ideal is a kernel.

(d) $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under addition and fails absorption: $\tfrac12\cdot 3 = \tfrac32 \notin\mathbb{Z}$, with $3 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\tfrac12\in\mathbb{Q}$. Note that $\mathbb{Z}$ *is* a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ — subring and ideal are different conditions, and apart from $R$ itself no ideal of a ring with $1$ is ever a subring, since an ideal containing $1$ is everything (Problem 3).
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — an ideal that is too big is everything)

(a) Prove: if an ideal $I$ of $R$ contains a unit, then $I = R$.

(b) Deduce that a field has exactly two ideals, $\{0\}$ and itself.

(c) Prove the converse for commutative rings: if $R$ is a nonzero commutative ring whose only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $R$, then $R$ is a field.

(d) Where in (c) is commutativity used, and where is $1_R \ne 0_R$ used?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Absorption applied to $u^{-1}$. (c) Given $a \ne 0$, look at the principal ideal $(a)$ of Definition 5.17 (p. 82).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Proposition 5.16 (p. 82) says $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ is an ideal. It is not $\{0\}$, so it is $R$, so $1 \in (a)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $u \in I$ be a unit. For any $r \in R$, absorption gives $r = (ru^{-1})u \in I$. So $I = R$.

(b) In a field every nonzero element is a unit, so any ideal other than $\{0\}$ contains one and is all of $R$ by (a). And $\{0\}$ and $R$ are distinct because $1 \ne 0$ in a field.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $a \in R$ be nonzero. By Proposition 5.16 the set $(a) = \{ra : r\in R\}$ is an ideal, and $a = 1_R a \in (a)$, so $(a) \ne \{0\}$. By hypothesis $(a) = R$, so $1_R \in (a)$, i.e. $1_R = ba$ for some $b$. Hence $a$ is invertible, and $R$ is a field.

(d) Commutativity twice: Proposition 5.16 is stated for commutative rings, because in a noncommutative ring $\{ra\}$ absorbs on the left only and is not an ideal; and $ba = 1$ gives a two-sided inverse only when $ab = ba$. Without it the conclusion is weaker than you might guess: it is only that $R$ is a *simple ring*, one whose sole two-sided ideals are $\{0\}$ and $R$. Simple does **not** mean division ring — $M_n(k)$ for $n>1$ is simple and is full of nonzero non-units. Replacing $(a)$ by the two-sided ideal generated by $a$ writes $1$ as a sum $\sum_i r_i a s_i$, which is not a two-sided inverse for $a$.

$1_R \ne 0_R$ is used to know $\{0\}$ and $R$ are two ideals and not one — in the zero ring they coincide, so the zero ring vacuously has "only these two" and is not a field. Definition 3.29 (p. 55) excludes it by hand for exactly this reason, and here is the theorem that would otherwise be false.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — which axiom does which job)

Let $I$ be an ideal of a ring $R$, and let $a \sim_I b$ mean $b - a \in I$ (Definition 5.19, p. 83).

(a) Prove Proposition 5.20 (p. 83): $\sim_I$ is an equivalence relation.

(b) Prove Lemma 5.25 (p. 85): if $a \sim_I a'$ and $b \sim_I b'$ then $a + b \sim_I a'+b'$ and $ab \sim_I a'b'$.

(c) Conclude Theorem 5.26 (p. 85): the operations of Definition 5.24 (p. 84) make $R/I$ a ring.

(d) Go back through (a) and (b) and say, for each use, which property of $I$ was needed: closure under addition, closure under negation, or absorption. Then explain why a mere subring will not do.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) For the product, write $a'b' - ab = a'(b'-b) + (a'-a)b$ and look at where each factor lives.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The additive half of the relation never needs absorption; the multiplicative half needs nothing else.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Reflexive: $a - a = 0 \in I$. Symmetric: $b - a \in I$ gives $a - b = -(b-a) \in I$. Transitive: $(c-b) + (b-a) = c - a \in I$. This is aa-03 Problem 6 again with $I$ in place of $N$ — and there the conclusion was that on $\mathbb{Z}$ the only such relations are congruence mod $n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Additive: $(a'+b') - (a+b) = (a'-a) + (b'-b)$, a sum of two elements of $I$. Multiplicative: with $a' - a \in I$ and $b' - b \in I$,
$$a'b' - ab = a'b' - a'b + a'b - ab = a'(b'-b) + (a'-a)b,$$
and both terms are in $I$ by absorption — the first by absorption on the left, the second on the right. Their sum is in $I$ by closure.

(c) The operations of Definition 5.24 are therefore well defined, and every ring axiom for $R/I$ is the corresponding axiom for $R$ read on representatives, with $0_{R/I} = 0 + I$ and $1_{R/I} = 1 + I$.

(d) Reflexivity uses $0 \in I$; symmetry uses closure under negation; transitivity uses closure under addition. The additive half of (b) uses closure under addition. **The multiplicative half of (b) is the only place absorption appears, and it appears twice — once on each side.**

That is why a subring will not do. $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$, and $\sim_{\mathbb{Z}}$ is a perfectly good equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Q}$ — parts (a) and the additive half of (b) go through unchanged. What fails is exactly the product: $\tfrac12 \sim \tfrac32$ and $\tfrac13\sim\tfrac13$, but $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac16$ and $\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac12$, which differ by $\tfrac13 \notin\mathbb{Z}$. A quotient *group* exists; a quotient *ring* does not. Absorption is precisely the price of multiplying cosets.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — four quotients identified)

Identify each of the following quotient rings as a ring you already know, and give the isomorphism explicitly.

(a) $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, obtained from Theorem 5.26 (p. 85) with $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $I = 6\mathbb{Z}$. Compare Example 5.27 (p. 85).

(b) $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)$ — Example 5.32 (p. 86).

(c) $R[x]/(x-r)$ for a commutative ring $R$ and $r \in R$ — Example 5.33 (p. 87).

(d) $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$ — Example 5.34 (p. 87).

Then: (e) Example 5.31 (p. 86) observes that a quotient of an integral domain need not be one. Give the smallest witness you can, and say which of (a)–(d) are domains.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) and (c): a coset $f(x) + (x-r)$ is determined by one element of $R$. Which one? (d) $x^2 + 1 \in (x^2+1)$, so in the quotient the class of $x$ squares to the class of $-1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b)–(d), define a homomorphism *out of the polynomial ring* whose kernel is the ideal, then read off the quotient. Evaluation is the map in (b) and (c).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The cosets are $a + 6\mathbb{Z}$, which are exactly the congruence classes $[a]_6$, and Definition 5.24's operations are Definition 2.10's. So the general construction *reproduces* $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ rather than resembling it — this is why the notation was chosen.

(b) $e_0 : \mathbb{Z}[x]\to\mathbb{Z}$ is surjective with kernel $(x)$, by Problem 1(b). So $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, via $f(x) + (x) \mapsto f(0)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Evaluation at $r$, $e_r : R[x]\to R$, is a surjective homomorphism (it fixes constants) and $f(x) \in \ker e_r$ exactly when $f(r) = 0$, which for a commutative $R$ happens exactly when $(x-r) \mid f(x)$. So $R[x]/(x-r)\cong R$ via $f(x) + (x-r)\mapsto f(r)$, and (b) is the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $r = 0$.

(d) Send $f(x) + (x^2+1)$ to $f(i) \in \mathbb{C}$. This is well defined because $i^2 + 1 = 0$ kills the ideal, it respects both operations, and it is onto because $a + bx \mapsto a+bi$. It is injective because every coset has a representative of degree $\le 1$ (divide by $x^2+1$ with remainder) and $a + bi = 0$ forces $a = b = 0$. So $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$: the complex numbers are what you get by *decreeing* that $x^2 = -1$.

(e) The smallest witness is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$: $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain and $[2][2] = [0]$ — or $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ as in (a), with $[2][3] = [0]$. Among (a)–(d): (a) is not a domain, (b) is, (c) is a domain exactly when $R$ is, and (d) is a field. So the construction can destroy a property, preserve it, or improve it, and which of the three happens is a fact about $I$ rather than about $R$ — which is what aa-11's prime and maximal ideals are for.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the ideals of ℤ, and of a product)

(a) Prove that every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \ge 0$.

(b) You have now run this argument three times: aa-03 Problem 6, aa-07 Problem 6(c), and here. Explain why the three are the same argument — specifically, why for $\mathbb{Z}$ the ideal condition and closure under addition and negation are the same condition.

(c) Let $R, S$ be rings. Prove that every ideal of $R\times S$ has the form $I\times J$ with $I$ an ideal of $R$ and $J$ an ideal of $S$.

(d) Use (c) to list all ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, and all ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Compare the two lists.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) Given an ideal $K$ and $(r,s) \in K$, multiply by $(1_R, 0_S)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Set $I = \{r : (r,0)\in K\}$ and $J = \{s : (0,s)\in K\}$, then show $K \subseteq I\times J$ and $I\times J\subseteq K$ separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $I$ be an ideal. If $I = \{0\}$ take $n = 0$. Otherwise $I$ contains a nonzero element and hence, being closed under negation, a positive one; let $n$ be the least, by well-ordering. Certainly $n\mathbb{Z}\subseteq I$ by absorption. Conversely, for $a \in I$ write $a = nq + r$ with $0\le r<n$ (Theorem 1.3, p. 6); then $r = a - nq \in I$, and minimality forces $r=0$. Uniqueness: $n\mathbb{Z} = n'\mathbb{Z}$ with both $\ge 0$ gives $n \mid n'$ and $n'\mid n$.

(b) Absorption in $\mathbb{Z}$ asks that $ma \in I$ for every integer $m$ — and $ma$ is $a$ added to itself $m$ times, or its negative. So absorption in $\mathbb{Z}$ is *implied by* closure under addition and negation, and the ideal condition collapses onto the additive one. That is special to $\mathbb{Z}$ (and to any ring generated by $1$ under addition); in $\mathbb{Q}$ it fails, which is exactly Problem 2(d).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $K$ be an ideal of $R\times S$ and set $I = \{r \in R : (r,0_S)\in K\}$, $J = \{s\in S : (0_R,s)\in K\}$. Both are ideals: closure under addition is inherited, and for $r' \in R$ we have $(r',0)(r,0) = (r'r,0) \in K$ by absorption, giving $r'r \in I$, and symmetrically on the other side and for $J$.

$K \subseteq I\times J$: if $(r,s)\in K$ then $(1_R,0_S)(r,s) = (r,0_S)\in K$, so $r\in I$, and $(0_R,1_S)(r,s) = (0_R,s)\in K$, so $s\in J$.

$I\times J\subseteq K$: if $r\in I$ and $s\in J$ then $(r,0)\in K$ and $(0,s)\in K$, so their sum $(r,s)\in K$.

The two idempotents $(1_R,0_S)$ and $(0_R,1_S)$ do all the work, and they exist because the ring is a product. No such elements exist in $\mathbb{Z}$ — its only idempotents are $0$ and $1$ — which is why (a) has a completely different flavour.

(d) $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ are fields, so each has exactly two ideals by Problem 3(b). By (c) the product has exactly four: $0\times 0$, $0\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times 0$, and the whole ring.

$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: by (a) and the correspondence between ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ containing $6\mathbb{Z}$, these come from $n \mid 6$ — namely $n = 1, 2, 3, 6$ — giving four ideals: the whole ring, $\{[0],[2],[4]\}$, $\{[0],[3]\}$, and $\{[0]\}$.

Four and four, and they match up — check the sizes, which settle the pairing on their own. Under $[a]_6 \mapsto ([a]_2,[a]_3)$ the two-element ideal $\{[0],[3]\}$ goes to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times 0$, and the three-element ideal $\{[0],[2],[4]\}$ goes to $0\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. That is not a coincidence, and aa-10 will name it: the two rings are isomorphic, so nothing distinguishable in the language of rings can tell their ideals apart either.
</details>
