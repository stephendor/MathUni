## Problem 1 (easy — proof, a polynomial constrains the eigenvalues)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $(T-2I)(T-3I)(T-4I)=0$. Suppose $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$. Prove that $\lambda=2$ or $\lambda=3$ or $\lambda=4$.

*(Axler §5.B, Exercise 2, p. 153)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Let $v$ be an eigenvector for $\lambda$ and apply the given operator to it. Before you can finish, you need to know what $p(T)$ does to an eigenvector — work that out for $T^k$ first.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
If $Tv=\lambda v$ then $T^kv=\lambda^kv$ for every $k\ge0$, by induction. Hence for any polynomial $p$, Definition 5.17 gives $p(T)v=p(\lambda)v$ — an identity worth stating as a lemma of its own, since it is what turns a polynomial equation about operators into one about scalars. Apply it with $p(z)=(z-2)(z-3)(z-4)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$0=p(T)v=p(\lambda)v$. Since $v\ne0$ and $p(\lambda)$ is a scalar, $p(\lambda)=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So $(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)(\lambda-4)=0$, and a product of scalars in $\mathbb{F}$ is zero only when one factor is, giving $\lambda\in\{2,3,4\}$. Two points for the write-up. Prove the lemma $p(T)v=p(\lambda)v$ rather than asserting it — it is the whole content, and the induction for $T^kv=\lambda^kv$ is two lines. And note what the result does *not* say: it does not claim $2$, $3$ and $4$ are eigenvalues, only that nothing else can be. This is the first taste of the minimal polynomial's role, which la-12's lesson flags as Axler §8.C and out of scope.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, eigenvalues of a square)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $9$ is an eigenvalue of $T^2$ if and only if $3$ or $-3$ is an eigenvalue of $T$.

*(Axler §5.B, Exercise 7, p. 153)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction is a one-line computation. For the other, factor $T^2-9I$ and think about what a product of two operators killing a nonzero vector can mean — there are two cases, and both give you something.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Leftarrow$) If $Tv=3v$ with $v\ne0$ then $T^2v=9v$; likewise for $-3$. ($\Rightarrow$) From $T^2v=9v$ with $v\ne0$ we get $(T-3I)(T+3I)v=0$ — check that factorisation against Theorem 5.20(a). Set $w=(T+3I)v$ and split on whether $w$ is zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $w=0$ then $Tv=-3v$ with $v\ne0$, so $-3$ is an eigenvalue. If $w\ne0$ then $(T-3I)w=0$, so $Tw=3w$ with $w\ne0$ and $3$ is an eigenvalue.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Either way one of $3,-3$ is an eigenvalue, completing the proof. The case split is the whole argument and should be signposted as such — a solution that writes "$(T-3I)(T+3I)v=0$, so $Tv=3v$ or $Tv=-3v$" has skipped it, and that inference is not valid for operators, since a product being zero on a vector does not mean either factor is. Note also that the eigenvector produced in the second case is $w$, not $v$: the eigenvalue transfers but the eigenvector need not.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, a geometric series for operators) [interleaves la-08]

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and there exists a positive integer $n$ such that $T^n=0$.

(a) Prove that $I-T$ is invertible and that
$$(I-T)^{-1} = I+T+\dots+T^{n-1}.$$

(b) Explain how you would have guessed the formula.

*(Axler §5.B, Exercise 1, p. 153)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
To prove an operator is invertible with a stated inverse, you do not need to solve anything — multiply the two candidates together, in both orders, and watch the sum telescope.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Expand $(I-T)(I+T+\dots+T^{n-1})$ using distributivity and Definition 5.16's power rules. Almost every term cancels against its neighbour. Then do the same in the other order, and say why both are needed. For (b), recall the scalar identity $\frac{1}{1-x}=1+x+x^2+\dots$ and what $T^n=0$ does to the tail.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(I-T)(I+T+\dots+T^{n-1}) = (I+T+\dots+T^{n-1}) - (T+T^2+\dots+T^{n}) = I-T^n = I$, using $T^n=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The same computation with the factors in the other order gives $I$ as well, since polynomials in one operator commute (Theorem 5.20(b)) — which is the cleanest justification and is worth citing rather than repeating the algebra. Both products equalling the identity is exactly what an inverse must satisfy, so $I-T$ is invertible with the stated inverse.

For (b): the scalar geometric series $1+x+x^2+\dots$ sums to $(1-x)^{-1}$, and the hypothesis $T^n=0$ truncates the operator version to a finite sum, so the guess is the infinite series with everything from $T^n$ onwards deleted. Say explicitly that this is a *heuristic* — the series argument does not transfer to operators without a convergence notion the module has not built — and that the proof in (a) owes it nothing.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, eigenspaces of an inverse) [interleaves la-08]

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is invertible. Prove that
$$E(\lambda,T) = E\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\lambda},T^{-1}\right)$$
for every $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$ with $\lambda\ne0$.

*(Axler §5.C, Exercise 9, p. 161)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Both sides are sets, so prove two inclusions — and by symmetry the second may be obtainable from the first. Unfold Definition 5.36: membership of $E(\lambda,T)$ means $Tv=\lambda v$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $v$ with $Tv=\lambda v$ and apply $T^{-1}$ to both sides. You will obtain $v=\lambda T^{-1}v$, and dividing by $\lambda$ — legal since $\lambda\ne0$ — gives what you want. For the reverse inclusion, note that $T^{-1}$ is invertible with inverse $T$ and that $1/(1/\lambda)=\lambda$, so the argument just given applies verbatim with the roles exchanged.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$Tv=\lambda v\Rightarrow T^{-1}(Tv)=T^{-1}(\lambda v)\Rightarrow v=\lambda T^{-1}v\Rightarrow T^{-1}v=\tfrac1\lambda v$. So $E(\lambda,T)\subseteq E(1/\lambda,T^{-1})$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The reverse inclusion follows by applying the same implication to the invertible operator $T^{-1}$ and the nonzero scalar $1/\lambda$, which yields $E(1/\lambda,T^{-1})\subseteq E(\lambda,T)$. Hence the two sets are equal.

Three details. Say where $\lambda\ne0$ is used — only in the division, and the hypothesis cannot be dropped since $1/\lambda$ would be undefined. Note that $\lambda=0$ is not a real loss: an invertible operator has no zero eigenvalue, so $E(0,T)=\{0\}$ anyway. And do not forget that $0$ belongs to both sides, so the equality is of *subspaces*, not merely of the sets of eigenvectors.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — proof, diagonalisable splits the space)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is diagonalisable. Prove that
$$V = \operatorname{null}T \oplus \operatorname{range}T.$$

*(Axler §5.C, Exercise 1, p. 160)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Diagonalisable gives you a basis of eigenvectors. Sort that basis into the eigenvectors with eigenvalue $0$ and those with nonzero eigenvalue, and identify what each group spans.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use criterion (b) of Theorem 5.41 to get a basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of eigenvectors, with $Tv_j=\lambda_jv_j$. The $v_j$ with $\lambda_j=0$ lie in $\operatorname{null}T$. For the others, $v_j=T(v_j/\lambda_j)$ lies in $\operatorname{range}T$. Then show each of the two subspaces is spanned by its group — the range needs a moment's care, since $\operatorname{range}T$ is spanned by the images $Tv_j$, not by the $v_j$ themselves.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\operatorname{range}T=\operatorname{span}(Tv_1,\dots,Tv_n)=\operatorname{span}(\lambda_1v_1,\dots,\lambda_nv_n)$, and dropping the zero terms leaves the span of those $v_j$ with $\lambda_j\ne0$. Similarly $\operatorname{null}T$ contains the span of those $v_j$ with $\lambda_j=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Together the two groups are the whole basis, so $\operatorname{null}T+\operatorname{range}T=V$. For directness, count: $\dim\operatorname{null}T+\dim\operatorname{range}T=\dim V$ by la-06's Fundamental Theorem, so Theorem 3.78's criterion applies and the sum is direct.

Two things to be careful about. The inclusion $\operatorname{span}\{v_j:\lambda_j=0\}\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$ is easy, but the *equality* needed for the count is best avoided altogether — the dimension argument above gives directness without it. And note that the result fails without diagonalisability: $T(w,z)=(z,0)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ has $\operatorname{null}T=\operatorname{range}T$, so the sum is certainly not direct, and the lesson's Example 5.43 shows that same operator is not diagonalisable.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof, eigenspaces fit inside the range)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_m$ denote the distinct **nonzero** eigenvalues of $T$. Prove that
$$\dim E(\lambda_1,T)+\dots+\dim E(\lambda_m,T) \le \dim\operatorname{range}T.$$

*(Axler §5.C, Exercise 10, p. 161)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Theorem 5.38 gives the same sum bounded by $\dim V$, which is weaker. To sharpen it, show every one of these eigenspaces sits inside $\operatorname{range}T$ — and notice that this is exactly where the word *nonzero* is spent.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For $\lambda\ne0$ and $v\in E(\lambda,T)$, write $v=T(v/\lambda)$, so $E(\lambda,T)\subseteq\operatorname{range}T$. The sum of these eigenspaces is direct by Theorem 5.38, so its dimension is the sum of the dimensions (la-09, Theorem 3.78). That direct sum is a subspace of $\operatorname{range}T$, and la-04's Theorem 2.38 bounds its dimension.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$E(\lambda_1,T)\oplus\dots\oplus E(\lambda_m,T)$ is a subspace of $\operatorname{range}T$, since each summand is and $\operatorname{range}T$ is closed under addition. Its dimension is $\dim E(\lambda_1,T)+\dots+\dim E(\lambda_m,T)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Theorem 2.38 applied to that subspace of $\operatorname{range}T$ gives the stated inequality. Three points earn the marks. Justify $v=T(v/\lambda)$ carefully — it is the only place $\lambda\ne0$ is used, and the statement is false for the zero eigenvalue, since $E(0,T)=\operatorname{null}T$ need not meet $\operatorname{range}T$ at all. Say why the sum of subspaces of $\operatorname{range}T$ is again inside $\operatorname{range}T$, which is closure under addition and not automatic. And compare the strength of the two bounds: this one improves on Theorem 5.38 exactly when $T$ is not injective, and reduces to it when $T$ is invertible, since then $\operatorname{range}T=V$.
</details>
