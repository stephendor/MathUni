## Problem 1 (easy — one function, three domains)

(a) Show that $f(x) = x^3$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
(b) Use Theorem 4.4.6 to argue that $f$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
(c) Show that $f$ is uniformly continuous on any bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

*(Abbott §4.4, Exercise 4.4.1, p. 119)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Factor $x^3 - y^3 = (x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2)$. Everything in all three parts follows from what you can say about the second factor on the domain in question.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), pick sequences that drift to infinity while closing up, as the lesson's gate did for $x^2$. For (c), boundedness of the domain bounds the second factor uniformly, which is exactly what lets $\delta$ stop depending on the point.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Take $x_n = n$ and $y_n = n + 1/n$. Then $|x_n - y_n| = 1/n \to 0$, while
$$|f(y_n) - f(x_n)| = \left(n + \tfrac1n\right)^3 - n^3 = 3n + \tfrac{3}{n} + \tfrac{1}{n^3} \ge 3,$$
so Theorem 4.4.6 applies with $\epsilon_0 = 3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $A$ be bounded, say $|a| \le M$ for all $a \in A$. For $x, y \in A$,
$$|x^3 - y^3| = |x-y|\,|x^2 + xy + y^2| \le 3M^2 |x - y|,$$
so given $\epsilon > 0$ the choice $\delta = \epsilon/(3M^2)$ works for every pair at once, which is Definition 4.4.5.

Part (a) is (c) with $M$ chosen per point: fix $c$, restrict to $|x - c| < 1$, and the same factorisation gives a bound depending on $c$. Say explicitly that this $\delta$ depends on $c$ — the contrast between (a) and (c) is the whole exercise, and writing both proofs with the same algebra makes the single point of difference visible.

Note also that (c) says *bounded*, not *compact*. Uniform continuity on a bounded non-closed set is possible; Theorem 4.4.8 gives a sufficient condition, not a necessary one, and Problem 2 sharpens this.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — where uniform continuity fails)

Show that $f(x) = 1/x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $[1, \infty)$ but not on $(0, 1]$.

*(Abbott §4.4, Exercise 4.4.2, p. 119)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Neither domain is compact, so Theorem 4.4.8 decides nothing here. Both halves must be done by hand.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
On $[1, \infty)$ estimate $|1/x^2 - 1/y^2|$ directly and use that the denominators are at least 1. On $(0,1]$ use Theorem 4.4.6 with two sequences approaching 0.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For $x, y \ge 1$,
$$\left| \frac{1}{x^2} - \frac{1}{y^2} \right| = \frac{|y^2 - x^2|}{x^2 y^2} = \frac{|y-x|(y+x)}{x^2y^2} \le |y - x| \left( \frac{1}{x^2 y} + \frac{1}{x y^2} \right) \le 2|x - y|,$$
so $\delta = \epsilon/2$ serves everywhere on $[1,\infty)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
On $(0,1]$ take $x_n = 1/n$ and $y_n = 1/(n+1)$. Then $|x_n - y_n| \to 0$ while $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| = (n+1)^2 - n^2 = 2n + 1 \ge 3$, so Theorem 4.4.6 applies with $\epsilon_0 = 3$.

The pair of results is worth a closing remark, because together they refute two plausible rules of thumb. An unbounded domain does not prevent uniform continuity, and a bounded domain does not guarantee it. What actually goes wrong on $(0,1]$ is that the function is unbounded near a point missing from the domain — and an-10's Exercise 4.4.13, the Continuous Extension Theorem, makes that observation into a criterion.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — four requests, some impossible)

Give an example of each, or explain which theorem makes it impossible.

(a) A continuous $f : (0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ and a Cauchy sequence $(x_n)$ in $(0,1)$ with $f(x_n)$ not Cauchy.
(b) A continuous $f : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ and a Cauchy sequence $(x_n)$ in $[0,1]$ with $f(x_n)$ not Cauchy.
(c) A continuous $f : [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ and a Cauchy sequence $(x_n)$ in $[0,\infty)$ with $f(x_n)$ not Cauchy.
(d) A continuous bounded $f$ on $(0,1)$ attaining a maximum but not a minimum.

*(Abbott §4.4, Exercise 4.4.6, p. 119)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A Cauchy sequence in $[0,1]$ converges to a point *of* $[0,1]$; a Cauchy sequence in $(0,1)$ need not.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For each domain ask whether the limit of the Cauchy sequence is in the domain. If it is, continuity carries convergence across and the image is convergent, hence Cauchy. If it is not, the function is free to misbehave there.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Possible: $f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0,1)$ with $x_n = 1/n$. The sequence is Cauchy, but $f(x_n) = n$ is unbounded and so not Cauchy by an-05's Lemma 2.6.3.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Impossible. A Cauchy sequence in $[0,1]$ converges by an-05's Theorem 2.6.4, and its limit lies in $[0,1]$ because that set is closed (an-07's Theorem 3.2.8). Continuity then gives $f(x_n) \to f(x)$ by an-10's Theorem 4.3.2(iv), and a convergent sequence is Cauchy by an-05's Theorem 2.6.2.

(c) Impossible, for the same reason: $[0,\infty)$ is closed, so a Cauchy sequence in it still converges inside it. Note that the argument used closedness and *not* boundedness — which is why (c) is impossible even though $[0,\infty)$ is not compact.

(d) Possible. Take $f(x) = x$ for $x \in (0, 1/2]$ and $f(x) = 1 - x$ for $x \in (1/2, 1)$... this is continuous on $(0,1)$, bounded, attains its maximum $1/2$ at $x = 1/2$, and has infimum $0$ which it never attains.

Say which hypothesis of the Extreme Value Theorem (d) violates, and note that it violates only one: the function is continuous and bounded, and the domain is bounded — it is closedness that is missing.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — deriving IVT properly)

Show how the Intermediate Value Theorem follows as a corollary of Theorem 4.5.2, the preservation of connectedness.

*(Abbott §4.5, Exercise 4.5.1, p. 124)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You need an-09's Theorem 3.4.7 twice: once to say $[a,b]$ is connected, once to unpack what connectedness of the image means.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show $[a,b]$ is connected, apply Theorem 4.5.2 to get $f([a,b])$ connected, then read Theorem 3.4.7 in the direction that converts connectedness into "contains every intermediate point".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$[a,b]$ is connected: if $x < c < y$ with $x, y \in [a,b]$ then $a \le x < c < y \le b$, so $c \in [a,b]$, and Theorem 3.4.7 applies. Hence $E = f([a,b])$ is connected by Theorem 4.5.2.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Now suppose $f(a) < L < f(b)$. Both $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ lie in $E$, and $L$ lies strictly between them, so Theorem 3.4.7 applied to the connected set $E$ gives $L \in E$. By definition of the image, $L = f(c)$ for some $c \in [a,b]$. Finally $c \ne a$ and $c \ne b$ because $f(c) = L$ differs from both $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, so $c \in (a,b)$ as the theorem claims. The case $f(a) > L > f(b)$ is identical with the inequalities reversed.

The last step is the one most often dropped: the theorem asserts $c$ is in the *open* interval, and the argument for that is separate from everything before it. Also worth stating is what the proof did not need — no completeness axiom appears anywhere in it, because all of that was spent earlier, in proving Theorem 3.4.7 and in proving that $[a,b]$ behaves as it does.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — three conjectures about images)

Decide the validity of each conjecture.

(a) Continuous functions take bounded open intervals to bounded open intervals.
(b) Continuous functions take bounded open intervals to open sets.
(c) Continuous functions take bounded closed intervals to bounded closed intervals.

*(Abbott §4.5, Exercise 4.5.2, p. 124)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Two of the three are false and one is nearly true. For (c), think about what a constant function does, and then decide whether a single point counts as a closed interval.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The lesson supplies counterexamples for the open cases. For (c), combine Theorem 4.4.2 with Theorem 4.5.2: the image is both compact and connected, and the sets in $\mathbb{R}$ that are both are exactly the closed bounded intervals — allowing degenerate ones.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) False. $f(x) = x^2$ takes $(-1,1)$ to $[0,1)$, which is neither open nor an interval of the same kind. (b) False by the same example, since $[0,1)$ is not open.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) True, with a caveat to state. Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$. The image is compact by Theorem 4.4.2 and connected by Theorem 4.5.2. A connected subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is an interval by an-09's Theorem 3.4.7; a compact one is closed and bounded by an-08's Theorem 3.3.4. So the image is a closed bounded interval $[m, M]$ — where $m$ and $M$ are the minimum and maximum supplied by the Extreme Value Theorem.

The caveat: if $f$ is constant the image is a single point $[k,k]$, which is a closed bounded interval only if degenerate ones are admitted. Say which convention you are using rather than ignoring the case.

Then note what has been assembled. Two preservation theorems, plus the classification of connected sets and the classification of compact sets, combine to say exactly what continuous functions do to closed intervals — and the answer is the Extreme Value Theorem and the Intermediate Value Theorem stated together as one sentence.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a fixed point, and a converse)

(a) Let $f$ be continuous on $[0,1]$ with range contained in $[0,1]$. Prove that $f$ has a fixed point: $f(x) = x$ for at least one $x$.
(b) Show that the Intermediate Value Theorem has a converse if $f$ is assumed increasing on $[a,b]$: if an increasing $f$ has the intermediate value property of Definition 4.5.3, then $f$ is continuous.

*(Abbott §4.5, Exercises 4.5.7 and 4.5.4, p. 124)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), a fixed point of $f$ is a zero of $g(x) = f(x) - x$. Evaluate $g$ at the two endpoints.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) is the Intermediate Value Theorem applied to $g$, with the endpoint signs coming from the range hypothesis. For (b), suppose $f$ is discontinuous at some $c$ and use monotonicity to show the discontinuity forces a whole interval of missed values.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Put $g(x) = f(x) - x$, continuous on $[0,1]$ by an-10's Theorem 4.3.4. Then $g(0) = f(0) - 0 \ge 0$ and $g(1) = f(1) - 1 \le 0$, since the range lies in $[0,1]$. If either is zero the endpoint is a fixed point; otherwise $g(1) < 0 < g(0)$ and Theorem 4.5.1 gives $c \in (0,1)$ with $g(c) = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Suppose $f$ is increasing on $[a,b]$, has the intermediate value property, and is discontinuous at some $c \in (a,b)$. Because $f$ is increasing, the one-sided limits
$$\alpha = \sup\{f(x) : x < c\} \quad\text{and}\quad \beta = \inf\{f(x) : x > c\}$$
both exist by the Axiom of Completeness, and $\alpha \le f(c) \le \beta$. Discontinuity at $c$ forces $\alpha < \beta$ — if they were equal, the squeeze would give $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$.

Now take any $L$ with $\alpha < L < \beta$ and $L \ne f(c)$. No $x$ satisfies $f(x) = L$: for $x < c$ we have $f(x) \le \alpha < L$, and for $x > c$ we have $f(x) \ge \beta > L$, and $f(c) \ne L$ by choice. But $L$ lies strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, since $f(a) \le \alpha < L < \beta \le f(b)$. That contradicts the intermediate value property, so $f$ is continuous on $(a,b)$; the endpoints are handled by the same argument with one side only.

Two closing remarks. Part (b) is a genuine converse and it needs a hypothesis — an-12's Darboux Theorem produces functions with the intermediate value property that are wildly discontinuous, so "increasing" is not a technicality. And in (a), notice that the range condition was used only at the two endpoints; the theorem is really about a continuous map of a compact interval into itself, which is the one-dimensional case of a fixed-point theorem that runs through the whole of topology.
</details>
