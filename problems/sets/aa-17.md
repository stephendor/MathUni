## Problem 1 (easy–medium — the first theorem, and three uses)

(a) Prove Theorem 8.44 (p. 168): if $f : M\to N$ is a surjective $R$-module homomorphism then $N\cong M/\ker f$.

(b) State the version for an arbitrary $f$, and deduce it from (a).

(c) Use (a) to identify $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$ in the module sense, and to identify the image of $f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $a\mapsto[5a]$.

(d) Compare the proof of (a) with aa-10 Problem 2(a). Which step of the ring version has no counterpart here?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The map is $\bar f(m+\ker f) = f(m)$; well-definedness and injectivity are the same computation read in the two directions.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Restrict the codomain to $\operatorname{im} f$, which is a submodule by aa-16 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Define $\bar f(m+\ker f) = f(m)$. *Well defined:* if $m+\ker f = m'+\ker f$ then $m-m'\in\ker f$, so $f(m)=f(m')$. *Injective:* if $f(m)=f(m')$ then $f(m-m')=0$, so the cosets agree. *Homomorphism:* $\bar f((m+K)+(m'+K)) = f(m+m') = \bar f(m+K)+\bar f(m'+K)$ and $\bar f(r(m+K)) = f(rm) = r\bar f(m+K)$. *Surjective:* because $f$ is.

(b) For arbitrary $f$, apply (a) to $f : M\to\operatorname{im} f$, giving $M/\ker f\cong\operatorname{im} f$. With the projection and the inclusion this is Theorem 8.43's diagram (p. 167).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The projection $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a surjective $\mathbb{Z}$-module map with kernel $n\mathbb{Z}$, so $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the construction reproduces the object, as in aa-10 Problem 5(a).

For $f(a) = [5a]_{12}$: $\ker f = \{a : 12\mid5a\} = 12\mathbb{Z}$, since $\gcd(5,12)=1$ and aa-02 Problem 3(a) applies. So $\operatorname{im} f\cong\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $f$ is onto — multiplication by a unit permutes $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. Contrast aa-16 Problem 6(d), where the multiplier $3$ was not a unit and the image was proper.

(d) The ring version had a third clause to check, $\bar f(1_{R/I}) = 1_S$. There is no such step here, because Definition 8.14 (p. 158) has no clause about a distinguished element — the same absence that made aa-15 Problem 6 count four module maps where there were no ring maps at all.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the third theorem)

Let $M$ be an $R$-module and $K\subseteq L$ submodules.

(a) Prove $L/K$ is a submodule of $M/K$.

(b) Prove Theorem 8.47 (p. 169): $(M/K)\big/(L/K)\cong M/L$.

(c) Apply with $M=\mathbb{Z}$, $K = 12\mathbb{Z}$, $L = 4\mathbb{Z}$, and check by counting.

(d) Which hypothesis makes $K\subseteq L$ rather than $L\subseteq K$ the right way round? Say what fails if they are swapped.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Apply Theorem 8.44 to the map $M/K\to M/L$, $m+K\mapsto m+L$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
That map is well defined precisely because $K\subseteq L$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $L/K = \{\ell+K : \ell\in L\}$ is nonempty, closed under addition since $L$ is, and closed under the action since $r(\ell+K) = r\ell+K$ with $r\ell\in L$. Proposition 8.23 (p. 161) applies.

(b) Define $\varphi : M/K\to M/L$ by $\varphi(m+K) = m+L$. Well defined: if $m-m'\in K$ then $m-m'\in L$, so $m+L = m'+L$ — and this is the only place $K\subseteq L$ is used. It is a surjective homomorphism, and $\varphi(m+K)=0$ means $m\in L$, so $\ker\varphi = L/K$. Theorem 8.44 finishes it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $12\mathbb{Z}\subseteq4\mathbb{Z}$ because $4\mid12$. The theorem gives
$$(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})\big/(4\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.$$
Counting: $|\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}| = 12$, $|4\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}| = |\{[0],[4],[8]\}| = 3$, and $12/3 = 4$ ✓.

(d) Swapping gives $\varphi(m+L) = m+K$, which is not well defined: $m-m'\in L$ does not put $m-m'$ in the smaller $K$. Concretely with $L=4\mathbb{Z}$, $K=12\mathbb{Z}$: $0$ and $4$ agree mod $4$ and differ mod $12$, so the proposed map sends one coset to two values. The containment of submodules runs the same way as in aa-10 Problem 6(d), and opposite to the divisibility of the generators — $K\subseteq L$ here is $12\mathbb{Z}\subseteq4\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $4\mid 12$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the second theorem)

Let $K, L$ be submodules of $M$.

(a) Prove $K+L$ is a submodule and $K\cap L$ is a submodule.

(b) Prove Theorem 8.48 (p. 170): $(K+L)/L\cong K/(K\cap L)$.

(c) Apply with $M = \mathbb{Z}$, $K = 4\mathbb{Z}$, $L = 6\mathbb{Z}$, and identify all four modules involved.

(d) Deduce from (c) a relation between $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$, and compare aa-10 Problem 4.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Apply Theorem 8.44 to the composite $K\hookrightarrow K+L\to(K+L)/L$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $4\mathbb{Z}+6\mathbb{Z} = 2\mathbb{Z}$ and $4\mathbb{Z}\cap6\mathbb{Z} = 12\mathbb{Z}$, by aa-10 Problem 4(a) and 4(b).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Both are aa-16 Problem 2, parts (b) and (a).

(b) Let $\psi : K\to(K+L)/L$ be $\psi(k) = k+L$. It is a homomorphism, being an inclusion followed by a projection. It is surjective: a general element of $(K+L)/L$ is $(k+\ell)+L = k+L = \psi(k)$. And $\psi(k)=0$ means $k\in L$, so $\ker\psi = K\cap L$. Theorem 8.44 gives the isomorphism.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $K+L = 4\mathbb{Z}+6\mathbb{Z} = 2\mathbb{Z}$ and $K\cap L = 12\mathbb{Z}$. So the theorem reads
$$2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\ \cong\ 4\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}.$$
Both have three elements: $\{[0],[2],[4]\}$ inside $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, and $\{[0],[4],[8]\}$ inside $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. The isomorphism sends $4a+12\mathbb{Z}$ to $4a+6\mathbb{Z}$, so $[4]\mapsto[4]$, $[8]\mapsto[2]$.

(d) In general $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ and $m\mathbb{Z}\cap n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$, so Theorem 8.48 says
$$\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\ \cong\ m\mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\mathbb{Z}.$$
Counting both sides — the left has $n/\gcd(m,n)$ elements and the right has $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)/m$ — gives
$$\frac{n}{\gcd(m,n)} = \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{m},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \gcd(m,n)\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = mn.$$
aa-10 Problem 4(d) used that identity to decide when $IJ = I\cap J$; here it comes out of an isomorphism theorem instead of being quoted, which is a fair trade for the reader who wanted to know why it is true.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — generators as a surjection from a free module)

(a) Prove Example 8.46 (p. 169): an $R$-module $M$ is generated by $n$ elements if and only if there is a surjective homomorphism $R^n\to M$.

(b) Deduce that every such $M$ is isomorphic to $R^n/K$ for a submodule $K\subseteq R^n$.

(c) Identify $n$ and $K$ for $M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, and for $M = \mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

(d) The submodule $K$ is called the module of **relations**. Explain the name using (c).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) A homomorphism out of $R^n$ is determined by the images of the $n$ standard generators — aa-16 Problem 4(b), iterated.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) is (a) plus Theorem 8.44.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) If $M = \langle m_1,\dots,m_n\rangle$, define $\varphi(r_1,\dots,r_n) = \sum r_im_i$. This is a homomorphism, and it is onto by Example 8.25 (p. 162), since its image is exactly the set of linear combinations. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\varphi : R^n\to M$ is onto, put $m_i = \varphi(e_i)$; then every $m = \varphi(r_1,\dots,r_n) = \sum r_im_i$, so the $m_i$ generate.

(b) Apply Theorem 8.44 (p. 168) to $\varphi$: $M\cong R^n/\ker\varphi$, and $\ker\varphi$ is a submodule of $R^n$ by aa-16 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: one generator suffices, $n=1$, $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ with $K = \ker\varphi = 6\mathbb{Z}$. So $M\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, unsurprisingly.

$M = \mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: two generators, $(1,[0])$ and $(0,[1])$, so $n=2$ and $\varphi(a,b) = (a,[b])$. Then $K = \ker\varphi = \{(a,b) : a = 0, 2\mid b\} = 0\oplus2\mathbb{Z} = \langle(0,2)\rangle$. So $M\cong\mathbb{Z}^2/\langle(0,2)\rangle$.

(d) The elements of $K$ are exactly the tuples of coefficients that produce $0$ in $M$ — that is, the linear relations the chosen generators satisfy. In the second example the only relation among $(1,[0])$ and $(0,[1])$ is that twice the second is zero, and $K$ is generated by $(0,2)$, which is that sentence written as a vector. Presenting $M$ as $R^n/K$ says: take $n$ free generators and impose exactly these relations. aa-19 makes the case where $K$ too is finitely generated into a definition.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — cyclic modules, and one thing that does not transfer)

(a) Prove Example 8.45 (p. 168): over a commutative ring, every cyclic module is isomorphic to $R/I$.

(b) Prove that every quotient of a cyclic module is cyclic.

(c) Prove that every submodule of a cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-module is cyclic.

(d) Show (c) fails over a general commutative ring: exhibit a cyclic $R$-module with a submodule that is not cyclic.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) A cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-module is $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; use the correspondence theorem and aa-09 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) $R$ itself is always a cyclic $R$-module. What are its submodules?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) This is aa-16 Problem 3(b): if $M=\langle m\rangle$ then $r\mapsto rm$ is a surjection $R\to M$ with kernel the annihilator of $m$, an ideal, and Theorem 8.44 identifies $M$ with $R$ modulo it.

(b) If $M = \langle m\rangle$ and $\pi : M\to M/K$ is the projection, then $\pi(m)$ generates: every element of $M/K$ is $\pi(rm) = r\pi(m)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) A cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-module is $\mathbb{Z}/I$ for an ideal $I$ of $\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ are the $d\mathbb{Z}$ — cyclic, generated by $d$. Submodules of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ containing $n\mathbb{Z}$ (aa-16 Problem 6(b)), so they are the $d\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $d\mid n$ — each generated by $[d]$. So all are cyclic.

(d) $R$ itself is cyclic over $R$, generated by $1$. Its submodules are its ideals, by aa-16 Problem 1(c). So the question is whether every ideal is principal — and in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ it is not: $(2,x)$ needs two generators, by aa-11 Problem 6(c). So $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a cyclic $\mathbb{Z}[x]$-module with a non-cyclic submodule.

What (c) really used was that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID, and the general statement is: **submodules of cyclic modules are cyclic exactly when the ring is a principal ideal ring**. Aluffi's Remark 8.49 (p. 171) says everything in §8.5 transfers to abelian groups because they are $\mathbb{Z}$-modules; this problem is the reminder that transfers in the other direction — from $\mathbb{Z}$ to a general $R$ — are not free.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — quotients of ℤ ⊕ ℤ)

Remark 8.49 (p. 171) notes that all of this applies to abelian groups, being the case $R=\mathbb{Z}$. Use it.

(a) Compute $(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\big/\langle(2,0),(0,3)\rangle$, and identify it as a familiar group.

(b) Compute $(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\big/\langle(1,1)\rangle$.

(c) Compute $(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\big/\langle(2,2)\rangle$, and explain why the answer differs from (b) in kind and not just in size.

(d) In (b) and (c) you changed generators of $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$. State the rule that makes such a change legitimate, and check it in each case.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The submodule is $2\mathbb{Z}\oplus3\mathbb{Z}$, so the quotient splits coordinatewise.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b), (c) Replace the standard basis by one in which the relation vector is a multiple of a single basis element.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\langle(2,0),(0,3)\rangle = 2\mathbb{Z}\oplus3\mathbb{Z}$, since any combination $a(2,0)+b(0,3) = (2a,3b)$. The quotient of a direct sum by a direct sum of submodules is the direct sum of the quotients, so
$$(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})/(2\mathbb{Z}\oplus3\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},$$
the last step by aa-16 Problem 4(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Take $u = (1,1)$ and $v = (0,1)$. Then $\{u,v\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$, since $(1,0) = u-v$ and $(0,1)=v$, and it is independent: $au+bv = (a, a+b) = 0$ forces $a=0$ then $b=0$. So $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}u\oplus\mathbb{Z}v$, and quotienting by $\langle u\rangle$ leaves
$$(\mathbb{Z}u\oplus\mathbb{Z}v)/\mathbb{Z}u\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}v\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}.$$

(c) With the same $u,v$: $(2,2) = 2u$, so the quotient is $(\mathbb{Z}u\oplus\mathbb{Z}v)/\langle 2u\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$.

The difference is not size — both quotients are infinite — but **torsion**. In (b) the quotient is free: no nonzero element is killed by an integer. In (c) the class of $u$ is nonzero and satisfies $2\bar u = 0$. So (b) and (c) are not isomorphic, by the invariant of aa-15 Problem 5(c). Quotienting by $(1,1)$ and by $(2,2)$ — the same *line* in the plane picture — gives genuinely different groups, which is exactly the phenomenon vector spaces do not have and the structure theorem of aa-20 exists to organise.

(d) The rule: a change of generators is legitimate when the new pair generates the same module and is independent — equivalently, when the matrix carrying one pair to the other is invertible **over $\mathbb{Z}$**, i.e. has determinant $\pm1$. In both (b) and (c) the change was
$$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix},$$
sending $(1,0)\mapsto u$ and $(0,1)\mapsto v$, with determinant $1$. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ one could also scale $(2,2)$ to $(1,1)$ and the two quotients would agree — determinant $2$ is invertible there. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is not, and that single failure is the whole difference between (b) and (c).
</details>
