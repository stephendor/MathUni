# an2-05 — Uniform convergence of derivatives and integrals

**Module:** Analysis II · **Unit:** an2-05
**Sources:** Abbott, *Understanding Analysis*, 2nd ed. — §6.2 "Uniform
Convergence of a Sequence of Functions" (pp. 154–163), §6.3 "Uniform Convergence
and Differentiation" (pp. 164–167). Lindström, *Spaces: An Introduction to Real
Analysis* — §4.1 "Modes of continuity" (pp. 79–81), §4.2 "Modes of convergence"
(pp. 81–86), §4.3 "Integrating and differentiating sequences" (pp. 86–92).

Numbered results used, from Lindström: Definition 4.1.1, Proposition 4.1.2,
Definition 4.1.3, Definition 4.2.1, Definition 4.2.2, Proposition 4.2.3,
Proposition 4.2.4, Proposition 4.3.1, Corollary 4.3.2, Corollary 4.3.3,
Proposition 4.3.4, Proposition 4.3.5, Corollary 4.3.6. From Abbott:
Definition 6.2.1, Example 6.2.2, Definition 6.2.3, Theorem 6.2.5, Theorem 6.2.6,
Theorem 6.3.1, Theorem 6.3.2, Theorem 6.3.3. Carried: Definition 3.1.1,
Proposition 3.1.4, Lemma 4.8.8, Theorem 4.6.4. Reported from tda1-07, and
belonging to Oudot: Corollary 3.6.

**Two books, one distinction, and they disagree about the price.** Both define
pointwise and uniform convergence identically, and both prove that uniform
convergence preserves continuity by the same ε/3 argument. They part company on
differentiation. Abbott's **Theorem 6.3.3** assumes only that the $f_n$ are
differentiable and that $(f_n')$ converges uniformly, and pays for the weaker
hypothesis with a Mean Value Theorem argument. Lindström's **Proposition 4.3.5**
additionally assumes the $f_n'$ are *continuous*, and is then, in his own words,
"just Corollary 4.3.2 in a convenient disguise" — three lines, routed through
integration. He calls his own statement one that "may look ugly and
unsatisfactory". Problem 4 is the comparison.

**A duplication worth knowing about.** Lindström gives the Weierstrass M-test
twice: as **Exercise 11 to §4.2** (p. 85), where it is set and named, and as
**Proposition 4.3.4** (p. 89), where it is stated and proved in full. This is the
second time in `an2` that one of his results carries two modalities in one book;
Lemma 3.7.11 was the first. The M-test belongs to an2-06 and appears here only
because Corollary 4.3.3 needs a way to certify uniform convergence of a series.

## Problem 1 (easy — the quantifier, and the doomed proof)

(a) State **Definition 6.2.1** and **Definition 6.2.3** (Abbott) and
**Definition 4.2.1** and **Definition 4.2.2** (Lindström), and confirm the two
books define the same two things. Then write the two definitions with their
quantifiers in order and circle the single difference. Lindström's gloss is the
one to keep: "the $N$ in the last sentence of the definition depends on $x$ — we
may need a much larger $N$ for some $x$'s than for others."

(b) Prove **Proposition 4.2.3**: uniform convergence is equivalent to
$\sup\{d_Y(f_n(x), f(x)) : x \in X\} \to 0$. Lindström notes a subtlety inside the
proof — "note that we may have unstrict inequality $\leq$ for the supremum
although we have strict inequality $<$ for each $x \in X$". Say why that does not
damage the argument, and connect it to the same phenomenon in an2-02's
Lemma 3.7.9.

(c) Now say what (b) means for an2-01. On $C[a,b]$ the sup metric was
$d_1(f,g) = \sup|f(x) - g(x)|$. Conclude, in one sentence, that **uniform
convergence is exactly convergence in the sup metric** — so the whole of an2-01's
and an2-02's machinery applies to it verbatim, and "uniformly Cauchy"
(**Theorem 6.2.5**) is Definition 3.4.1 in that metric.

(d) Abbott's §6.2 contains a *deliberately failed* proof, at pp. 156–157: he

words: "the value of $n$ depends on the point $x$", and $x$ ranges over an
interval that was only determined after $n$ was chosen. Then say which of the
three terms of his quadrilateral inequality is the one that cannot be controlled.

(e) Abbott illustrates the failure numerically with $g_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — what uniform convergence preserves)

(a) Prove **Theorem 6.2.6** (Abbott) and **Proposition 4.2.4** (Lindström): a
uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. They are the same ε/3
argument. Write it once, and then say which of the three terms was the one
Problem 1(d) could not control and what uniformity did to it.

(b) Prove **Theorem 6.2.5**, the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence
(Abbott sets it as Exercise 6.2.6). Then say what it is, in the language of
an2-02: name the metric space, and name the theorem that makes the criterion
usable rather than merely true.

(c) Lindström's Example 1 to §4.3 (p. 86): the tent $f_n$ on $[0,1]$ that is 0
outside $[0, 1/n]$ and rises to height $n$ at $1/(2n)$. Show $f_n \to 0$
pointwise, compute $\int_0^1 f_n$, and conclude that pointwise convergence does
not permit passing the limit through an integral. Then say why the sequence is
not uniformly convergent, using **Proposition 4.2.3** and one number.

(d) Prove **Proposition 4.3.1**: if continuous $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$
then $F_n(x) = \int_a^x f_n$ converges uniformly to $F(x) = \int_a^x f$. Identify
where the *finiteness of the interval* is used — the proof divides $\epsilon$ by
$b - a$, and that division is the whole reason the theorem is about a bounded
interval. Then deduce **Corollary 4.3.2**, the version with a flexible lower
limit.

(e) Deduce **Corollary 4.3.3**: a uniformly convergent series of continuous
functions may be integrated term by term. Lindström's warning is worth quoting:
"This formula may look obvious, but it does not in general hold for series that
only converge pointwise. As we shall see many times later in the book,
interchanging integrals and infinite sums is quite a tricky business." Say which
of your earlier parts is the counterexample that makes the warning concrete.

*(Lindström p. 83 for Proposition 4.2.4 with proof and the remark on ε/3
arguments; p. 86 for the opening of §4.3 and Example 1; p. 87 for
Proposition 4.3.1 with proof and Corollary 4.3.2 with proof; p. 88 for
Corollary 4.3.3 with proof and the warning. Abbott p. 160 for Theorem 6.2.5,
whose proof is Exercise 6.2.6, and Theorem 6.2.6 with proof.)*

For (d), $\int_a^x \frac{\epsilon}{b-a} dt \leq \epsilon$ needs $x - a \leq b - a$,

</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Choose $N$ with $|f_N(x) - f(x)| < \epsilon/3$ *for every $x$* — this is the
one line that differs — then take $\delta$ from continuity of $f_N$ at $c$, and
the three terms give $\epsilon$.
(c) $\int_0^1 f_n = \frac12 \cdot \frac1n \cdot n = \frac12$ for every $n$, while

</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The term that could not be controlled was $|f_N(x) - f(x)|$ for $x$ near $c$.
Uniformity makes the choice of $N$ independent of $x$, so the same $N$ that works
at $c$ works throughout the $\delta$-neighbourhood, and the term is $< \epsilon/3$
there. Nothing else in the argument changes at all — which is the cleanest
possible demonstration that the distinction is about the *order of the
quantifiers* and nothing else.

(b) Theorem 6.2.5 says that a sequence in the metric space $(C_b(A), d_1)$ is
Cauchy if and only if it converges — which is not automatic, and is exactly what
completeness means. What makes the criterion usable is **Theorem 4.6.4**, an2-08's
result that the space is complete: without it, "uniformly Cauchy" would be a
condition with no conclusion attached.

(e) Part (c) is the counterexample. The tents converge pointwise to 0 and their
integrals converge to $1/2$; writing them as a telescoping series
$v_n = f_n - f_{n-1}$ gives a pointwise convergent series whose term-by-term
integral is wrong.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(d) Given $\epsilon > 0$, uniform convergence supplies $N$ with
$|f(t) - f_n(t)| < \frac{\epsilon}{b-a}$ for all $t \in [a,b]$ and $n \geq N$.
Then for every $x \in [a,b]$ and $n \geq N$,
$$|F(x) - F_n(x)| = \left|\int_a^x (f(t) - f_n(t))\,dt\right| \leq \int_a^x |f(t)-f_n(t)|\,dt \leq \int_a^b \frac{\epsilon}{b-a}\,dt = \epsilon.$$
The bound does not depend on $x$, so the convergence is uniform. ∎

division by $b - a$ and the other is the last inequality — and say what the
statement becomes on $[0, \infty)$⟩

## Problem 3 (medium — differentiation, where uniform convergence is not enough)

(a) Lindström's Example 3 (p. 89): $\{\frac{\sin nx}{n}\}$ converges uniformly to
0 and the sequence of derivatives $\{\cos nx\}$ does not converge at all. Verify

they live in an increasingly narrower strip around the $x$-axis, they are all
equally steep at their steepest". Compute the maximum of $|f_n'|$ and confirm it
does not depend on $n$.

(b) Abbott's **Example 6.2.2**(iii): $h_n(x) = x^{1 + \frac{1}{2n-1}}$ on

differentiable, and show the limit is not differentiable at 0. Then say what this
adds to (a) — one of them has a limit that is differentiable and derivatives that
do not converge, and the other has a limit that is not differentiable at all.

term needs "the most delicate touch", in Abbott's phrase, and why the Mean Value

(d) State **Theorem 6.3.2** and **Theorem 6.3.3**. Abbott observes that

(e) Abbott's Exercise 6.3.2: $g_n(x) = x^n/n$ on $[0,1]$. Show $g_n \to 0$
