# tda1-06 — Metrics on diagrams: bottleneck and interleaving

**Module:** TDA I · **Unit:** tda1-06
**Sources:** Oudot, *Persistence Theory: From Quiver Representations to Data
Analysis* — Chapter 3 "Stability", §1: the chapter opening with the two
questions "are these signatures stable? are they informative?", Theorem 3.1
(Isometry) and the split of its statement into a stability and a converse
stability part, and footnote 1 that the bottleneck metric "is oblivious to the
decorations" so the diagrams are taken undecorated throughout (p. 49); partial
matchings, the cost of a matched pair and of an unmatched point, the bottleneck
cost (3.1) and the bottleneck distance (3.2), Example 3.2 with Figure 3.1, and
the statement that $\mathrm{d_b}$ is an extended *pseudo*metric with the
counterexample $\mathbb{Q}^2 \setminus \Delta$ against
$(\mathbb{Q}+\sqrt{2})^2 \setminus \Delta$ and the compactness result of
[72, theorem 4.10] (pp. 50–51); §1.2 on interleavings: the motivating case
$\|f-g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$ and the interleaved sublevel-set filtrations
(3.3), Definition 3.3 with the commuting diagrams (3.4) and (3.5), morphisms of
degree $\varepsilon$, the shift morphism, Definition 3.3 (rephrased), the
statement that $\mathrm{d_i}$ is an extended pseudometric with the
counterexample $\mathbb{I}[0,1]$ against $\mathbb{I}(0,1)$, and the stability
and universality properties attributed to Lesnick (pp. 52–53).
Edelsbrunner and Harer, *Computational Topology: An Introduction* — §VIII.2
"Stability Theorems": the bottleneck distance $W_\infty$ defined by bijections,
with the diagonal added at infinite multiplicity and Figure VIII.5's squares,
and the assertion that $W_\infty$ "satisfies all axioms of a metric"
(pp. 216–217); vines and vineyards under the straight-line homotopy, and the
Stability Theorem for Filtrations (pp. 217–218); homological critical values,
the definition of a *tame* function, the reconstruction of $\mu_p^{a_i,a_j}$ for
such functions, and the Stability Theorem for Tame Functions, stated without
proof (pp. 218–219); the Wasserstein distance and its motivation (p. 219).

**Two books, one object, and a flat contradiction to resolve.** Edelsbrunner
says $W_\infty$ "satisfies all axioms of a metric". Oudot says the same distance
"is not a true distance though, even on the multisets not touching the
diagonal". Problem 2 makes you settle it, and the answer is not that one of them
is wrong.

Interleaves tda1-05, whose guided construction predicted this unit's definition
and got one thing right and one thing wrong — Problem 2(e) scores it; cat-04,
whose mission strip stated the $\varepsilon$-interleaving in full before any
metric existed; and an2-03.

## Problem 1 (easy — the bottleneck distance, two ways)

(a) Define a partial matching $M : P \leftrightarrow Q$ between multisets. State
both constraints and say why "partial" is essential rather than a convenience.
(b) Give the two costs: $\|p-q\|_\infty$ for a matched pair, and
$\frac{|s_y - s_x|}{2}$ for an unmatched point. Prove that the second is the
$\ell^\infty$-distance from $s$ to its nearest point of the diagonal $\Delta$.
(c) Write down the bottleneck cost (3.1) and the bottleneck distance (3.2).
Then write down Edelsbrunner's $W_\infty$, defined by bijections onto a diagram
containing $\Delta$ at infinite multiplicity. Show the two definitions agree,
by turning a partial matching into a bijection and back.
(d) Work Example 3.2. Say why the two possible matchings of $\{p, s\}$ with
$\{p', s'\}$ give the same cost, and what Oudot means by saying the constraint
that $\mathrm{dgm}(f')$ has *two* points near $p, s$ is "well-captured by the
bottleneck distance" — his footnote contrasts this with the Hausdorff distance,
so make the contrast explicit.
(e) Oudot notes that "the actual choice of norm in $\mathbb{R}^2$ does not
matter fundamentally since all norms are equivalent, however the tightest bounds
in the isometry theorem are achieved using the $\ell^\infty$-norm". Say what
"does not matter fundamentally" does and does not mean here — in particular
whether the *value* of $\mathrm{d_b}$ is norm-independent.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) The nearest point of $\Delta$ to $(s_x, s_y)$ is the midpoint of its
coordinates, twice.
(c) Send unmatched points to their diagonal projections.
(d) Hausdorff distance is a distance between *sets*.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The nearest diagonal point to $s = (s_x, s_y)$ in $\ell^\infty$ is
$(\frac{s_x+s_y}{2}, \frac{s_x+s_y}{2})$. ⟨your step: compute both coordinate
differences and take the max.⟩
(d) Hausdorff distance asks only that every point of one set be near *some*
point of the other, so a single point of $\mathrm{dgm}(f)$ could cover both
$p'$ and $s'$. ⟨your step: say why a matching cannot, and what that buys.⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — is it a metric? the books disagree)

(a) Quote both claims. Edelsbrunner, p. 217: "Clearly, $W_\infty(X,Y) = 0$ iff
$X = Y$ … We see that $W_\infty$ satisfies all axioms of a metric". Oudot,
p. 51: the bottleneck distance "is an extended pseudometric … It is not a true
distance though, even on the multisets not touching the diagonal $\Delta$".
(b) Work Oudot's counterexample. Show that $\mathbb{Q}^2 \setminus \Delta$ and
$(\mathbb{Q}+\sqrt{2})^2 \setminus \Delta$ are distinct multisets at bottleneck
distance zero, and identify precisely which metric axiom fails.
(c) Resolve the contradiction. Find the sentence in Edelsbrunner that restricts
the class of diagrams he is quantifying over, and the sentence in Oudot that
restores the metric property. State the class of multisets on which both
authors are right, and name the result Oudot cites for it.
(d) Prove the triangle inequality for $\mathrm{d_b}$, following Oudot's
one-clause justification that matchings compose. Then say exactly why this is
*not* enough to give a metric, i.e. which axiom the composition argument does
not touch.
(e) tda1-05's guided construction predicted this definition before reading it.
It predicted: the definition itself; that non-negativity and symmetry would be
free; that identity of indiscernibles would be doubtful because the infimum need
not be attained; and that the triangle inequality would be the hard axiom.
Score all four against what you have just read. One of them is wrong in an
instructive way — say which, and say what the prediction got right about the
*phenomenon* while attaching it to the wrong axiom.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) Every rational is within $\varepsilon$ of an element of
$\mathbb{Q}+\sqrt{2}$.
(c) Edelsbrunner: "Under the assumptions on the input functions considered in
this book…". Oudot: "In [72, theorem 4.10] a compactness argument…".
(e) How long is Oudot's proof of the triangle inequality?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Edelsbrunner's diagrams "consist of finitely many points above the
diagonal", and Oudot's counterexample multiset is not locally finite. ⟨your
step: state the class on which the infimum is attained, name the theorem Oudot
cites, and say why Theorem 1.13 makes that class contain every diagram this

(e) The triangle inequality takes Oudot a subordinate clause. ⟨your step: say

</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — interleaving, from filtrations and then without them)

(a) Let $f, g : X \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\|f-g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$. Prove
(3.3): $F_i \subseteq G_{i+\varepsilon}$ and $G_i \subseteq F_{i+\varepsilon}$
for all $i$. Then draw the commuting square of sublevel sets and inclusions for
$i \le j$.
(b) Apply $\mathsf{H}_*$ and write down the cross maps $\phi_i$ and $\psi_i$.
State Definition 3.3 in its first form, naming which diagrams (3.4) and (3.5)
each family must make commute.
(c) Oudot remarks that the definition "does not actually need filtrations to
start with in order to be stated". Say what is dropped and what survives, and
connect this to tda1-03's Self-check about Edelsbrunner's analogous remark.
(d) Define a *morphism of degree $\varepsilon$* and the shift morphism
$\mathbb{1}_\mathbb{U}^{\varepsilon+\varepsilon'}$. Then state Definition 3.3
(rephrased) and prove the two forms equivalent, matching each clause of one to a
diagram of the other.
(e) Compare with cat-04's mission strip, which stated the $\varepsilon$-inter\-leaving
as a pair of natural transformations $M \Rightarrow N(\cdot+\varepsilon)$ and
$N \Rightarrow M(\cdot+\varepsilon)$ whose composites are the
$2\varepsilon$-shift maps. Match every part of that description to a part of
Definition 3.3 (rephrased), and say which of the two formulations makes it
clearer why an interleaving is not a morphism.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) $f(x) \le i$ implies $g(x) \le i + \varepsilon$.
(d) A degree-0 morphism is a morphism in the ordinary sense.
(e) $\mathrm{Hom}^\varepsilon$ against natural transformations into a shifted
functor.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium-hard — the interleaving distance and its two properties)

(a) Define $\mathrm{d_i}$ by (3.6). Say what convention Oudot adopts when no
$\varepsilon$-interleaving exists for any $\varepsilon$, and give a pair of
modules for which that happens.
(b) Prove that $\mathrm{d_i}$ satisfies the triangle inequality, by composing an
$\varepsilon$-interleaving with an $\varepsilon'$-interleaving. Be explicit
about the degrees: which $\mathrm{Hom}^\bullet$ does each composite live in?
(c) Show $\mathbb{I}[0,1]$ and $\mathbb{I}(0,1)$ are $\varepsilon$-interleaved
for every $\varepsilon > 0$ and not $0$-interleaved. Conclude that
$\mathrm{d_i}$ is not a metric, and say what a $0$-interleaving would have been.
(d) State the *stability* and *universality* properties of $\mathrm{d_i}$.
Universality says any other stable pseudometric $\mathrm{d}$ satisfies
$\mathrm{d} \le \mathrm{d_i}$ — explain why that makes $\mathrm{d_i}$ the
*largest* such pseudometric, and why largest is the useful extreme.
(e) Oudot notes that these properties were proved by Lesnick "under some
restrictions". Without the text of those restrictions in front of you, say what
you would need to check before citing universality, and how you would phrase a
citation that does not overclaim.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) When the modules are not interleaved at any scale.
(c) A 0-interleaving is a pair of mutually inverse morphisms of degree 0.
(d) A larger distance separates more pairs.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) For $\varepsilon > 0$, the maps $\mathbb{I}[0,1]_i \to \mathbb{I}(0,1)_{i+
\varepsilon}$ can be taken to be identities where both are $\boldsymbol{k}$ and
zero elsewhere. ⟨your step: check the interleaving conditions, then show a
$0$-interleaving would be an isomorphism and that these two modules are not

## Problem 5 (hard — what the two metrics are to each other)

(a) State Theorem 3.1 (Isometry) and split it as Oudot does into a stability
part and a converse stability part. Say which part says diagrams are *stable*
signatures and which says they are *informative*, and why those are the right
words.
(b) State Edelsbrunner's Stability Theorem for Filtrations and his Stability
Theorem for Tame Functions. Define *homological critical value* and *tame
function* as he does. Then compare all three statements: which is an inequality
and which an equality, which is about modules and which about functions, and
what each needs of its inputs.
(c) Edelsbrunner reconstructs $\mu_p^{a_i,a_j}$ for a tame function using
interleaved values $b_i$. Write the formula and check it against tda1-03's
$\mu_p^{i,j}$; say what the $b_i$ are doing and why they are needed here but not
there.
(d) Describe vines and vineyards. Say what the connectedness of a vine expresses
about stability, and reconstruct Edelsbrunner's bound on the slope of a line
segment in the vineyard. Which theorem does that bound prove?
(e) The reflection. This unit's mission strip says: "The interleaving distance
is the algebraic metric; bottleneck is its geometric shadow — stability relates
them."

  First: "metric". Both distances are pseudometrics, and each source gives an
  explicit counterexample to the missing axiom. Give both counterexamples and
  say for which class of objects each distance *is* a metric.

  Second: "geometric shadow". A shadow is a lossy image. Theorem 3.1 says the
  two distances are *equal* on $\mathfrak{q}$-tame modules. Say what is wrong
  with "shadow" and what the right word is, then say what genuine asymmetry
  between the two distances the strip might have been reaching for.

  Third: "stability relates them". Stability is one of the two inequalities in
  Theorem 3.1. Say what the strip leaves out and why the omitted half is the one
  that makes diagrams worth computing.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) Stable: similar modules, similar diagrams. Informative: the converse.
(b) One of the three is an equality.
(e) Second question: what does an isometry lose?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(e) Second question: an isometry loses nothing about distances, so "shadow" is
the wrong picture for the relation Theorem 3.1 asserts. ⟨your step: give the
right word, then find the asymmetry that *is* real — one of the two objects is

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Problem 2 is the unit. Two textbooks assert incompatible things about the same
formula, in the same notation, and both are correct. The habit worth building is
not "check which one is right" but "find the quantifier each one left
implicit" — because that quantifier is where the mathematics is, and neither
author wrote it in the sentence you are comparing.
</details>
