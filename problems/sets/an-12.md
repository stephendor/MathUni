## Problem 1 (easy — the quotient rule, twice)

(a) Use Definition 5.2.1 to find the derivative of $f(x) = 1/x$ directly.
(b) Combine (a) with the Chain Rule (Theorem 5.2.5) to prove part (iv) of Theorem 5.2.4, the quotient rule.
(c) Prove part (iv) again by manipulating the difference quotient for $f/g$ directly, in the style of the product rule's proof.

*(Abbott §5.2, Exercise 5.2.2, p. 136)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), put the difference quotient over a common denominator before taking any limit.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Write $f/g = f \cdot (1/g)$, differentiate the product, and use the chain rule on $1/g$ as the composition of $x \mapsto 1/x$ with $g$. (c) Add and subtract $f(c)g(c)$ in the numerator, as the product rule's proof does.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\dfrac{1/x - 1/c}{x - c} = \dfrac{c - x}{xc(x-c)} = \dfrac{-1}{xc}$, whose limit as $x \to c$ is $-1/c^2$ by an-10's Corollary 4.2.4, since $c \ne 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) By the Chain Rule, $(1/g)'(c) = -g'(c)/[g(c)]^2$, and then the product rule gives
$$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'(c) = f'(c)\frac{1}{g(c)} + f(c)\cdot\frac{-g'(c)}{[g(c)]^2} = \frac{g(c)f'(c) - f(c)g'(c)}{[g(c)]^2}.$$

State where $g(c) \ne 0$ is used, and note that it is needed twice: once so that $1/g$ is defined near $c$ at all, and once so that the Chain Rule's outer function is differentiable at $g(c)$. Continuity of $g$ at $c$ — which Theorem 5.2.3 supplies — is what guarantees $g$ stays away from zero on a neighbourhood, so the quotient is defined on an interval around $c$ rather than only at $c$. That step is silently assumed in most calculus texts and is worth writing out once.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — differentiable at exactly one point)

By imitating the Dirichlet construction, build a function on $\mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable at exactly one point.

*(Abbott §5.2, Exercise 5.2.3, p. 136)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Multiply the Dirichlet function by something that vanishes fast at the chosen point.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $f(x) = x^2$ for rational $x$ and $f(x) = 0$ for irrational $x$. Differentiability at 0 is a squeeze; failure elsewhere follows from failure of continuity, using Theorem 5.2.3 in contrapositive.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
At $c = 0$: $\left|\dfrac{f(x) - f(0)}{x - 0}\right| = \left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x}\right| \le |x|$ for every $x \ne 0$, since $|f(x)| \le x^2$ in both cases. So the difference quotient is squeezed to $0$ and $f'(0) = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
At $c \ne 0$ the function is not even continuous: take rationals $x_n \to c$, giving $f(x_n) = x_n^2 \to c^2$, and irrationals $y_n \to c$, giving $f(y_n) = 0$. Since $c^2 \ne 0$, an-10's Corollary 4.3.3 shows $f$ is discontinuous at $c$, and Theorem 5.2.3 in contrapositive form shows it is not differentiable there.

Two things to say. The squeeze at $0$ works because $|f(x)| \le x^2$ *uniformly over both cases* — the estimate must be checked for rational and irrational $x$ separately even though it looks like one line. And note the shape of the answer: differentiability is a pointwise notion in exactly the same way continuity is, and this function is as extreme a demonstration of that as an-10's Thomae function was.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — how smooth is $x^a \sin(1/x)$?)

Let $g_a(x) = x^a \sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$ and $g_a(0) = 0$. Find a value of $a$ (not necessarily an integer) for each of the following.

(a) $g_a$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ but $g_a'$ is unbounded on $[0,1]$.
(b) $g_a$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ with $g_a'$ continuous but not differentiable at zero.
(c) $g_a$, $g_a'$ both differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ but $g_a''$ not continuous at zero.

*(Abbott §5.2, Exercise 5.2.5, p. 136)*

> **Read $x^a$ as $|x|^a$.** Abbott writes $x^a \sin(1/x)$ and then asks for differentiability *on $\mathbb{R}$* with $a$ potentially non-integer — and as printed those two requirements do not fit together, since $x^{3/2}$ is undefined for $x < 0$. The intended object, and the only one for which the question has an answer, is $g_a(x) = |x|^a \sin(1/x)$. That function is odd: $|-x|^a = |x|^a$ while $\sin(1/(-x)) = -\sin(1/x)$. So everything established on $x > 0$ transfers to $x < 0$ unchanged, and the condition at $0$ is genuinely two-sided. For integer $a$ the two readings agree up to a sign and nothing below changes.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For $x > 0$, $g_a'(x) = a x^{a-1}\sin(1/x) - x^{a-2}\cos(1/x)$; oddness gives the other side without new work. At zero, the derivative must be computed from the definition.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Existence of $g_a'(0)$ needs $a > 1$, by the squeeze $|g_a(x)/x| \le |x|^{a-1}$. Then read off the behaviour of the $x^{a-2}\cos(1/x)$ term, which is the one that misbehaves.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $a = 3/2$: then $g_a'(0) = 0$ exists, since $|g_a(x)/x| \le |x|^{1/2} \to 0$ from both sides. For $x > 0$ the term $-x^{-1/2}\cos(1/x)$ is unbounded as $x \to 0^+$, so $g_a'$ is unbounded on $[0,1]$ as required.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Rather than guessing, work out the whole range, because the three parts of this problem are three consecutive windows in $a$ and seeing that is the point of the exercise. For $x > 0$,
$$g_a'(x) = a x^{a-1}\sin(1/x) - x^{a-2}\cos(1/x),$$
and every question here is decided by which power of $x$ multiplies the $\cos(1/x)$ term, since $\cos(1/x)$ oscillates between $\pm 1$ and never settles. Reading off the three thresholds:

- $g_a'(0)$ exists iff $a > 1$, by the squeeze $|g_a(x)/x| \le |x|^{a-1}$.
- $g_a'$ is continuous at $0$ iff $a > 2$, since $x^{a-2}\cos(1/x) \to 0$ exactly when $a - 2 > 0$.
- $g_a'$ is differentiable at $0$ iff $a > 3$, since the difference quotient $g_a'(x)/x$ carries $-x^{a-3}\cos(1/x)$.

So part (b) — continuous but not differentiable at $0$ — is the window $2 < a \le 3$, and **any** value in it answers the question. Both $a = 5/2$ and $a = 3$ work. At $a = 3$ the difference quotient is $3x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$, whose second term oscillates between $\pm 1$ for arbitrarily small $x$; the endpoint is included, not excluded, and it is the simplest answer to quote.

Taking $a = 5/2$ shows the same thing with the $|x|^a$ reading doing visible work: $g_a'(x) = \frac52 x^{3/2}\sin(1/x) - x^{1/2}\cos(1/x)$ for $x > 0$ with $g_a'(0) = 0$, both terms tending to $0$, while $g_a'(x)/x$ contains $-x^{-1/2}\cos(1/x)$, which not only fails to converge but is unbounded. Since $g_a$ is odd, $g_a'$ is even, so the left-hand side adds no case — the practical payoff of fixing the reading of $x^a$ before starting.

(c) $a = 4$, and the same reading gives it: $a > 3$ makes $g_4'$ differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, while $g_4''$ carries a term $-x^{a-4}\sin(1/x) = -\sin(1/x)$, bounded and oscillating, so $g_4''$ has no limit at $0$ and is not continuous there. The window for (c) is $3 < a \le 4$, one notch along from (b)'s.

For each part, verify the value at zero from Definition 5.2.1 rather than by substituting into the formula for $x \ne 0$ — the formula does not apply at 0, and the whole exercise lives at that one point. Then say in a sentence what the family shows: differentiability, continuous differentiability, and twice-differentiability are genuinely different conditions, and a single parameter interpolates between them.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — finishing Darboux)

(a) Assume $g$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ with $g'(a) < 0 < g'(b)$. Show there is a point $x \in (a,b)$ with $g(a) > g(x)$, and a point $y \in (a,b)$ with $g(y) < g(b)$.
(b) Complete the proof of Darboux's Theorem.

*(Abbott §5.2, Exercise 5.2.6, p. 137)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$g'(a) < 0$ says a limit is negative. Unpack the definition of that limit to get an inequality about actual values of $g$ near $a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), apply the definition of the derivative at $a$ with $\epsilon = |g'(a)|$ to find points just to the right of $a$ where the difference quotient is negative; since the denominator is positive there, the numerator is negative. For (b), use the Extreme Value Theorem and the Interior Extremum Theorem.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Since $\lim_{x \to a}\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a} = g'(a) < 0$, taking $\epsilon = -g'(a)/2 > 0$ gives $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < x - a < \delta$ implies the quotient is within $\epsilon$ of $g'(a)$, hence negative. For such $x$, $x - a > 0$, so $g(x) - g(a) < 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $g$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, hence continuous there by Theorem 5.2.3, so an-11's Extreme Value Theorem gives a point $c \in [a,b]$ where $g$ attains its minimum. By (a) there is a point of $(a,b)$ with value below $g(a)$, so $c \ne a$; and a point with value below $g(b)$, so $c \ne b$. Hence $c \in (a,b)$, and Theorem 5.2.6 gives $g'(c) = 0$.

Translating back through $g(x) = f(x) - \alpha x$ from the lesson, $g'(c) = f'(c) - \alpha = 0$, so $f'(c) = \alpha$, which is Darboux's Theorem.

The step worth emphasising is why $c$ has to be interior. Part (a) is exactly the work of ruling out the two endpoints, and without it the Interior Extremum Theorem could not be applied at all — as an-12's self-check 2 showed, a minimum at an endpoint says nothing about the derivative there.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — derivative bounds become function bounds)

(a) Show that if $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $f'$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then $f$ is Lipschitz on $[a,b]$: there is $M > 0$ with $|f(x) - f(y)| \le M|x - y|$ for all $x, y$.
(b) Show that if in addition $|f'(x)| < 1$ on $[a,b]$, then $f$ is contractive: there is $0 < s < 1$ with $|f(x) - f(y)| \le s|x-y|$ for all $x, y$.

*(Abbott §5.3, Exercises 5.3.1 and 5.3.2, p. 143)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The Mean Value Theorem converts the quotient $(f(x)-f(y))/(x-y)$ into a single value of $f'$. What you then need is a bound on all values of $f'$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply Theorem 5.3.2 on $[y,x]$, then bound $|f'(c)|$ using an-11's Extreme Value Theorem, which applies because $f'$ is continuous on a compact set.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For $x \ne y$, say $y < x$, the Mean Value Theorem gives $c \in (y,x)$ with $f(x) - f(y) = f'(c)(x-y)$. Since $f'$ is continuous on the compact $[a,b]$, $|f'|$ is continuous too and attains a maximum $q$ there. Hence $|f(x)-f(y)| = |f'(c)||x-y| \le q|x-y|$.

Take $M = q + 1$ rather than $M = q$, and notice why. The problem asks for $M > 0$, and $q = 0$ for every constant $f$ — a perfectly legitimate input which the bound $M = q$ would fail to serve. Enlarging a bound never breaks it, so $M = q+1 > 0$ works for every $f$ at no cost. Degenerate inputs are worth a moment's thought in both parts of this problem.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) The same argument bounds the quotient by $q = \max_{[a,b]}|f'|$, and the point is that this maximum is *attained*, so $|f'(x)| < 1$ everywhere forces $q < 1$ rather than merely $q \le 1$. That distinction is the whole exercise: on an open or unbounded interval the supremum of $|f'|$ could be 1 without being attained, and the conclusion would fail.

Do not then set $s = q$. The problem asks for $0 < s < 1$, and a constant $f$ gives $q = 0$, which is not in that range — the estimate is fine but the constant it produces is not the one that was asked for. Take
$$s = \frac{q+1}{2}.$$
Then $s \ge 1/2 > 0$ always; $s < 1$ because $q < 1$; and $q \le s$ because $q < 1$, so the bound $|f(x)-f(y)| \le q|x-y| \le s|x-y|$ still holds. Averaging with the endpoint you are strictly inside is the standard way to convert a non-strict bound into a strict interior one, and it costs nothing.

Give an example making that concrete — $f(x) = x - \arctan x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ has $0 \le f'(x) = x^2/(1+x^2) < 1$ everywhere, and $f$ is not contractive on $\mathbb{R}$, since the quotient approaches 1.

Then note where this is going: a contractive function on a closed interval has a unique fixed point, found by iterating from any starting value, which is an-10's Exercise 4.3.9. The chain runs derivative bound, then Lipschitz constant, then fixed point — and every link is the Mean Value Theorem converting information about $f'$ into information about $f$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — three conjectures about derivatives)

Decide each, with proof or counterexample.

(a) If a derivative function is not constant, then it must take some irrational values.
(b) If $f'$ exists on an open interval and $f'(c) > 0$ at some point $c$, then $f' > 0$ on some neighbourhood $V_\delta(c)$.
(c) If $f$ is differentiable on an interval containing zero and $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x) = L$, then $f'(0) = L$.

*(Abbott §5.2, Exercise 5.2.8, p. 137)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is Darboux's Theorem plus a counting argument. (b) asks whether a derivative is locally sign-preserving — which would follow from continuity, and derivatives need not be continuous. (c) is the one that is true and needs the Mean Value Theorem.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), a non-constant derivative takes two distinct values, hence by Theorem 5.2.7 every value between them. For (b), look for a derivative that is positive at a point and takes negative values arbitrarily close to it. For (c), write $f'(0)$ as a limit of difference quotients and convert each quotient into a value of $f'$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) True. If $f'(a) \ne f'(b)$ then Darboux's Theorem puts every value between them in the range of $f'$, and an-01's density of the irrationals in $\mathbb{R}$ guarantees that interval contains irrational numbers.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) False. Take $g(x) = x/2 + x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$ and $g(0) = 0$. Then $g'(0) = 1/2 > 0$, but for $x \ne 0$, $g'(x) = 1/2 + 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$, and along $x_n = 1/(2n\pi)$ this equals $1/2 + 0 - 1 = -1/2 < 0$ for every $n$. So every neighbourhood of $0$ contains points where $g' < 0$.

(c) True. Fix $x \ne 0$ in the interval. The Mean Value Theorem on the interval between $0$ and $x$ gives a point $c_x$ strictly between them with $\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x-0} = f'(c_x)$. As $x \to 0$ we have $c_x \to 0$, so $f'(c_x) \to L$ by hypothesis, and therefore the difference quotient tends to $L$. By Definition 5.2.1 that says $f'(0) = L$.

Note what (b) and (c) together say, since it is easy to think they conflict. A derivative can fail to be continuous at a point (b), but it cannot have a *limit* at that point different from its value (c). Discontinuities of derivatives are always of the oscillating kind — never jumps and never removable — which is Darboux's Theorem seen from another angle.
</details>
