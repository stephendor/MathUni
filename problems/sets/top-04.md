## Problem 1 (easy — the definition, and how little of it you have to check)

$f : X \to Y$ is **continuous** if for each open subset $V$ of $Y$, the set
$f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ (p. 102).

(a) Munkres observes that if the topology on $Y$ is given by a basis
$\mathcal{B}$, it suffices to check that $f^{-1}(B)$ is open for every basis
element; and if by a subbasis $\mathcal{S}$, that $f^{-1}(S)$ is open for every
subbasis element. Prove both reductions, using the two set identities he gives
(p. 103). Say which property of preimages is doing the work in each, and why the
subbasis reduction needs *both* identities while the basis reduction needs one.
(b) Work Munkres's Example 3 in full. Let $\mathbb{R}$ carry the standard
topology and $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ the lower limit topology. Show that the identity
$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_\ell$ is **not** continuous and that the identity
$g : \mathbb{R}_\ell \to \mathbb{R}$ **is**. Then state the general principle
about identity maps and fineness that these two facts are instances of, and
prove it.
(c) Munkres writes that continuity "depends not only upon the function $f$
itself, but also on the topologies specified for its domain and range" (p. 102).
Give one function $X \to Y$ of sets which is continuous for one choice of
topologies on the same two sets and not for another, and say which of the four
possible fineness comparisons makes an identity map continuous.
(d) Munkres proves one direction of the equivalence of his definition with the
$\epsilon$-$\delta$ definition on $\mathbb{R}$ and writes "Proving that the
$\epsilon$-$\delta$ definition implies our definition is no harder; we leave it
to you" (p. 103). Do the direction he leaves.

*(Munkres §18, p. 102 for the definition and the relativity remark; p. 103 for
the basis and subbasis reductions and for Example 1, whose converse direction is
left to the reader; p. 104 for Example 3.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), preimage commutes with unions *and* with intersections — which is more
than images do. For (d), start from $\epsilon$-$\delta$ and take $V$ open in the
range.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $f^{-1}(\bigcup B_\alpha) = \bigcup f^{-1}(B_\alpha)$ handles the basis case.
For a subbasis you must first pass from subbasis elements to basis elements —
which are finite intersections — using
$f^{-1}(S_1 \cap \cdots \cap S_n) = f^{-1}(S_1) \cap \cdots \cap f^{-1}(S_n)$,
and then run the basis case.
(b) The identity $\mathrm{id} : (X, \mathcal{T}) \to (X, \mathcal{T}')$ is
continuous if and only if $\mathcal{T}$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}'$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $f^{-1}([a,b)) = [a,b)$, which is not open in $\mathbb{R}$; whereas
$g^{-1}((a,b)) = (a,b)$, which is open in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ since every $(a,b)$
is a union of sets $[x,b)$.
(d) Given $V$ open in the range and $x_0 \in f^{-1}(V)$, choose $\epsilon$ with
$(f(x_0)-\epsilon, f(x_0)+\epsilon) \subset V$, then the $\delta$ the hypothesis
supplies; $(x_0-\delta, x_0+\delta) \subset f^{-1}(V)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Basis.* Let $V$ be open in $Y$. By Lemma 13.1 (top-02),
$V = \bigcup_{\alpha \in J} B_\alpha$ for basis elements $B_\alpha$. Then
$$f^{-1}(V) = f^{-1}\Big(\bigcup_\alpha B_\alpha\Big) = \bigcup_\alpha f^{-1}(B_\alpha),$$
a union of open sets, hence open. The property used is that preimage commutes
with arbitrary unions.
⟨your step: the subbasis case — say what a basis element of the topology
generated by $\mathcal{S}$ looks like, apply the second identity, and then say
why you still need the first⟩

(b) $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_\ell$. The set $[a,b)$ is open in
$\mathbb{R}_\ell$, being a basis element. Its preimage under the identity is
$[a,b)$ itself, which is not open in $\mathbb{R}$: no interval about $a$ lies
inside it. So $f$ is not continuous.
⟨your step: $g$, and then the general principle with its proof⟩

(c), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — four faces of continuity)

**Theorem 18.1** (p. 104). For $f : X \to Y$ the following are equivalent:
(1) $f$ is continuous; (2) for every $A \subset X$, $f(\bar{A}) \subset \overline{f(A)}$;
(3) for every closed $B \subset Y$, $f^{-1}(B)$ is closed in $X$; (4) for each
$x \in X$ and each neighbourhood $V$ of $f(x)$ there is a neighbourhood $U$ of
$x$ with $f(U) \subset V$.

(a) Prove the theorem. Munkres runs
$(1) \Rightarrow (2) \Rightarrow (3) \Rightarrow (1)$ and
$(1) \Rightarrow (4) \Rightarrow (1)$; do the same, and say why that pattern of
implications suffices rather than proving all twelve.
(b) Condition (4) is the one that localises: Munkres says that if it holds at a
particular $x$ then $f$ is **continuous at the point $x$** (p. 104). Show that
$f$ is continuous if and only if it is continuous at every point, and identify
which step of his $(4) \Rightarrow (1)$ argument is the globalisation.
(c) Condition (2) is an inclusion, not an equality. Give a continuous $f$ and a
set $A$ with $f(\bar{A}) \neq \overline{f(A)}$, and say which of the two sides
is the larger in your example. Then show that if $f$ is a homeomorphism the
inclusion is an equality for every $A$.
(d) Which of (1)–(4) would you use to prove that a composite of continuous maps
is continuous, and which to prove that the preimage of a closed set under a
continuous map is closed? Answer both in one line each, then say why (2) is the
awkward one to compute with.

*(Munkres §18, pp. 104–105 for Theorem 18.1 and its proof; p. 104 for the
definition of continuity at a point.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), a map that is not surjective, or one whose image fails to be closed,
will separate the two sides.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) The two cycles together give every implication by composition: any of
(1),(2),(3) reaches any other round the first cycle, and (4) is tied to (1) in
both directions.
(c) Take $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = \arctan x$, and
$A = \mathbb{R}$: then $\bar{A} = \mathbb{R}$, $f(\bar{A}) = (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$
and $\overline{f(A)} = [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The globalisation is the last sentence: $f^{-1}(V) = \bigcup_x U_x$, a union
of open sets. (c) The right-hand side is the larger; for a homeomorphism apply
(2) to $f$ and to $f^{-1}$ and combine.
(d) Composites: (1), directly. Preimage of a closed set: (3), which *is* the
statement.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $(1) \Rightarrow (2)$. Let $A \subset X$ and let $x \in \bar{A}$; we show
$f(x) \in \overline{f(A)}$. Let $V$ be a neighbourhood of $f(x)$. Then
$f^{-1}(V)$ is open (continuity) and contains $x$, so it is a neighbourhood of
$x$; since $x \in \bar{A}$, Theorem 17.5(a) says it meets $A$, at some $y$ say.
Then $f(y) \in V \cap f(A)$, so $V$ meets $f(A)$. As $V$ was arbitrary,
$f(x) \in \overline{f(A)}$ by Theorem 17.5(a) again.
⟨your step: $(2) \Rightarrow (3)$, which needs $f(f^{-1}(B)) \subset B$ — say
where that inclusion can be strict and why it does not matter here⟩

(b), (c), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — homeomorphism, and the three ways it can fail)

$f : X \to Y$ bijective with $f$ and $f^{-1}$ both continuous is a
**homeomorphism**; equivalently, a bijection with $f(U)$ open if and only if $U$
is open (p. 105). A property of $X$ expressed entirely in terms of the open sets
of $X$ is a **topological property**. If $f : X \to Y$ is injective and
continuous and $f : X \to f(X)$ is a homeomorphism onto its image as a subspace
of $Y$, then $f$ is an **imbedding**.

(a) Prove the equivalence of the two definitions of homeomorphism.
(b) Munkres's Example 6 gives two bijective continuous maps that are not
homeomorphisms: the identity $g : \mathbb{R}_\ell \to \mathbb{R}$, and
$F : [0,1) \to S^1$ with $F(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$. Verify both. For
$F$, follow his argument: exhibit an open set of $[0,1)$ whose image is not open
in $S^1$, and say precisely what has to be shown about the point $F(0)$.
(c) His Example 7 expands the range of $F$ to get
$g : [0,1) \to \mathbb{R}^2$, "a continuous injective map that is not an
imbedding". Explain why the *same* map becomes an imbedding or not depending
only on the codomain named, and reconcile this with Theorem 18.2(e), which says
expanding the range preserves continuity.
(d) Prove that being Hausdorff is a topological property, directly from
Munkres's characterisation of what a topological property is. Then give a
property of a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is *not* topological, with two
homeomorphic subsets disagreeing on it.

*(Munkres §18, p. 105 for the definitions of homeomorphism, topological property
and imbedding; p. 106 for Examples 4, 5 and Example 6; p. 107 for the
continuation of its $S^1$ example and for Example 7; pp. 107–108 for Theorem 18.2.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d)'s non-topological property, "bounded" is a good candidate — Munkres's
Example 5 gives you the homeomorphism you need.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) For $F$, take $U = [0, 1/4)$. Its image is an arc containing the point
$p = F(0) = (1,0)$, and you must show no open $V$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has
$V \cap S^1 \subset F(U)$ — any disc about $p$ contains points of $S^1$ with
parameter just below 1.
(c) Being an imbedding is a statement about $X \to f(X)$, and $f(X)$ carries the
subspace topology from whichever space you called the codomain — but $f(X)$ and
its subspace topology are the *same* whether you regard $F$ as landing in $S^1$
or in $\mathbb{R}^2$. So the failure is the same failure, and Example 7 is
Example 6 restated; what changes is only that "not surjective" now hides it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) Hausdorff is topological: a homeomorphism carries a disjoint pair of open
sets separating $x_1, x_2$ to a disjoint pair separating $f(x_1), f(x_2)$.
Boundedness is not: $(-1,1)$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are homeomorphic by Example 5's
$F(x) = x/(1-x^2)$, and one is bounded while the other is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Suppose $f$ is a bijection with $f, f^{-1}$ continuous. Let $U$ be open in
$X$. Continuity of $f^{-1}$ says $(f^{-1})^{-1}(U)$ is open in $Y$; and
$(f^{-1})^{-1}(U) = f(U)$ because $f$ is a bijection. So $U$ open $\Rightarrow$
$f(U)$ open.
⟨your step: the converse, and the other half of the biconditional⟩

(b) *The identity $g : \mathbb{R}_\ell \to \mathbb{R}$.* It is continuous by
Problem 1(b). It is bijective. But $g^{-1} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_\ell$ is
the identity in the other direction, which Problem 1(b) showed is not
continuous. So $g$ is not a homeomorphism.
*The map $F : [0,1) \to S^1$.* ⟨your step: take $U = [0,1/4)$, note it is open in
$[0,1)$ — say why — and then show $F(U)$ is not open in $S^1$⟩

(c), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the construction kit, and the pasting lemma's hypotheses)

**Theorem 18.2** (pp. 107–108) lists six rules: constant maps, inclusions,
composites, restricting the domain, restricting or expanding the range, and the
local formulation of continuity. **Theorem 18.3**, the pasting lemma (p. 108):
if $X = A \cup B$ with $A, B$ closed in $X$, and $f : A \to Y$, $g : B \to Y$
are continuous and agree on $A \cap B$, then they combine to a continuous
$h : X \to Y$.

(a) Prove Theorem 18.2(f), the local formulation, and then deduce (d) from (b)
and (c). Which of the six rules is used in the proof of which other?
(b) Prove the pasting lemma. Munkres's proof works with *closed* sets rather
than open ones; say why that is the natural choice given the hypotheses, and
which part of Theorem 18.1 licenses it.
(c) Munkres remarks that the pasting lemma "also holds if $A$ and $B$ are open
sets in $X$", and that this is "just a special case" of Theorem 18.2(f). Prove
that reduction — that is, derive the open version from 18.2(f) — and say why the
closed version is *not* a special case of 18.2(f).
(d) Work Munkres's Example 8's three displays. Show that $h$ (defined by $x$ for
$x \le 0$ and $x/2$ for $x \ge 0$) is continuous; that $k$ ($x-2$ for $x \le 0$,
$x+2$ for $x \ge 0$) does not define a function at all; and that $l$ ($x-2$ for
$x < 0$, $x+2$ for $x \ge 0$) is a function but is not continuous, using his
witness that $l^{-1}((1,3))$ is not open. For each of the three, name which
hypothesis of Theorem 18.3 holds and which fails.

*(Munkres §18, pp. 107–108 for Theorem 18.2 and its proof; p. 108 for
Theorem 18.3; p. 109 for the remark about open sets and for Example 8.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (c), the pieces $A$ and $B$ of the closed version are not open, so 18.2(f)'s
hypothesis — a cover by *open* sets on each of which the restriction is
continuous — is not available.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) The identity $h^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(C) \cup g^{-1}(C)$ holds for any $C$. With
$C$ closed, $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed in $A$ and hence — by top-03's Theorem 17.3,
since $A$ is closed in $X$ — closed in $X$; same for $g$; and a union of two
closed sets is closed by Theorem 17.1(3). Theorem 18.1(3) then converts "preimage
of every closed set is closed" back into continuity.
(d) For $l$: the two pieces are $(-\infty, 0)$ and $[0, \infty)$, one open and
one closed, so neither the closed version nor the open version of the pasting
lemma applies, and indeed the conclusion fails.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The open version: $X = A \cup B$ with both open, and $h|A = f$, $h|B = g$
continuous, is exactly the hypothesis of 18.2(f) with the two-element open cover
$\{A, B\}$. The closed version is not a special case because 18.2(f) needs the
cover to be by open sets; that is why 18.3 needs its own proof, run on closed
sets.
(d) $k$ fails at $x = 0$: the two formulas give $-2$ and $2$, so the agreement
hypothesis fails and there is no function to speak of.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Let $C$ be closed in $Y$. By elementary set theory
$h^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(C) \cup g^{-1}(C)$: a point of $A$ lands in $C$ under $h$
exactly when it does under $f$, and likewise for $B$.
Since $f$ is continuous, $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed in $A$ by Theorem 18.1(3) applied
to $f$. Since $A$ is closed in $X$, Theorem 17.3 (top-03) makes $f^{-1}(C)$
closed in $X$.
⟨your step: the same for $g$, then combine and convert back to continuity —
naming the two results you use⟩

(d) *$h$.* The pieces are $A = (-\infty, 0]$ and $B = [0, \infty)$, both closed
in $\mathbb{R}$, with $A \cup B = \mathbb{R}$ and $A \cap B = \{0\}$. On the
overlap both formulas give $0$, so they agree. Each piece is continuous
(polynomial, restricted). Theorem 18.3 applies and $h$ is continuous.
⟨your step: $k$ and $l$, each with the hypothesis that fails named⟩

(a), (c) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — into a product is easy, out of one is not)

**Theorem 18.4** (p. 110): $f : A \to X \times Y$ with $f(a) = (f_1(a), f_2(a))$
is continuous if and only if $f_1$ and $f_2$ are. The maps $f_1, f_2$ are the
**coordinate functions** of $f$.

Munkres follows the theorem immediately with this, on the same page:

> There is no useful criterion for the continuity of a map $f : A \times B \to X$
> whose *domain* is a product space. One might conjecture that $f$ is continuous
> if it is continuous "in each variable separately," but this conjecture is not
> true. (See Exercise 12.)

(a) Prove Theorem 18.4. Both directions. In the forward direction you need that
the projections $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are continuous — prove that too, from the
definition of the product topology (top-02, §15).
(b) Define what it means for $F : X \times Y \to Z$ to be **continuous in each
variable separately**, in terms of the maps $F(\cdot, y_0)$ and $F(x_0, \cdot)$
and Theorem 18.2. Then prove that continuity implies separate continuity — so
that the failure is genuinely one-directional.
(c) Work Munkres's Exercise 12 (p. 112). Let
$F : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be
$$F(x, y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2} & (x,y) \neq (0,0),\\[4pt] 0 & (x,y) = (0,0).\end{cases}$$
Show $F$ is continuous in each variable separately; compute
$g(x) = F(x, x)$; and conclude that $F$ is not continuous. State exactly which
point the discontinuity is at, and why the two computations together settle it.
(d) **The at1-01 clause.** at1-01 defines a deformation retraction as a family
$f_t : X \to X$ and says the continuity requirement "is not the statement that
each $f_t$ is continuous. It is the stronger statement that the single map on
the product $X \times I$ is continuous — jointly in the point and the time."
Using (b) and (c), say precisely what would go wrong if the weaker reading were
adopted: give the general shape of a "deformation retraction" that satisfies the
weak condition and not the strong one, and name which of Theorem 18.4's two
directions is *unavailable* for maps out of a product.

*(Munkres §18, p. 110 for Theorem 18.4, its proof and the quoted paragraph;
p. 112, Exercise 12, for the function $F$, which is set as an exercise and not
worked in the text. The at1-01 quotation is from `lessons/at1/at1-01.html`,
already on `main`.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (c), compute $F(x,x)$ before doing anything clever; the algebra is one line
and it settles everything.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Forward: $f_i = \pi_i \circ f$, a composite of continuous maps
(Theorem 18.2(c)). Backward: it suffices to check preimages of basis elements
$U \times V$, and $f^{-1}(U \times V) = f_1^{-1}(U) \cap f_2^{-1}(V)$.
(b) $F(\cdot, y_0)$ is the composite of $x \mapsto (x, y_0)$ with $F$; the first
map is continuous by Theorem 18.4 because its coordinate functions are the
identity and a constant.
(d) The weak reading only asks that each slice $f_t$ be continuous, which by (b)
is implied by — and does not imply — joint continuity. So a "deformation
retraction" could move points continuously at each fixed time while the motion
jumps as $t$ varies, and no theorem in §18 rules it out, because Theorem 18.4
runs only for maps *into* a product.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) $g(x) = x^2/(2x^2) = 1/2$ for $x \neq 0$, and $g(0) = F(0,0) = 0$. So $g$ is
not continuous at $0$. But $g = F \circ d$ where $d(x) = (x,x)$ is continuous by
Theorem 18.4; if $F$ were continuous, $g$ would be continuous by
Theorem 18.2(c). Hence $F$ is not continuous — at the origin.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Projections are continuous.* $\pi_1^{-1}(U) = U \times Y$ and
$\pi_2^{-1}(V) = X \times V$; both are basis elements of the product topology
whenever $U$ and $V$ are open, hence open.
*Forward.* $f_1 = \pi_1 \circ f$ and $f_2 = \pi_2 \circ f$, composites of
continuous maps, so both are continuous by Theorem 18.2(c).
*Backward.* ⟨your step: it suffices to check preimages of basis elements — why?
— and then compute $f^{-1}(U \times V)$⟩

(c) $F$ is continuous in each variable separately: fix $y_0 \neq 0$; then
$x \mapsto xy_0/(x^2+y_0^2)$ is a quotient of polynomials with non-vanishing
denominator, hence continuous. Fix $y_0 = 0$; then $F(x, 0) = 0$ for every $x$
including $x = 0$, a constant map, continuous by Theorem 18.2(a).
⟨your step: the other variable by symmetry; then $g(x) = F(x,x)$, and the
conclusion — say which theorem you use to get from "$g$ discontinuous" to "$F$
discontinuous"⟩

(b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>
