**Module:** Topology · **Unit:** top-05
**Sources:** Munkres, *Topology*, 2nd ed. — §15 "The Product Topology on
$X \times Y$" (pp. 86–88) and §19 "The Product Topology" (pp. 112–118).
Numbered results used: Theorem 15.1, Theorem 15.2, Theorem 19.1, Theorem 19.2,
Theorem 19.3, Theorem 19.4, Theorem 19.5, Theorem 19.6.

Interleaves top-04 throughout — Theorem 19.6 is Theorem 18.4 with an arbitrary
index set, and the point of the unit is which of its two directions survives —
and top-02 in Problem 1, since both topologies here are specified by a basis or
a subbasis rather than by listing open sets.

**Cited by at2-05**, which uses this unit's product topology as the input to the

Munkres leaves most of §19's easy theorems to his exercises: "most of the proofs

Exercises 1, 2 and 3. Problems 2 and 3 are those.

## Problem 1 (easy — two factors, two descriptions)

For two spaces, the **product topology** on $X \times Y$ has as basis the sets
$U \times V$ with $U$ open in $X$ and $V$ open in $Y$ (p. 86).

(a) Verify that this collection is a basis, using Munkres's identity
$$(U_1 \times V_1) \cap (U_2 \times V_2) = (U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2).$$
Then explain his remark that the collection $\mathcal{B}$ "is not a topology on
$X \times Y$", by giving an open set of $X \times Y$ that is not a product of
two sets.
(b) Prove Theorem 15.1: if $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $X$ and $\mathcal{C}$ a
basis for $Y$, then $\{B \times C\}$ is a basis for $X \times Y$. Say which
lemma of top-02 you use and what its hypothesis required you to check.
(c) Prove Theorem 15.2: the collection
$\mathcal{S} = \{\pi_1^{-1}(U)\} \cup \{\pi_2^{-1}(V)\}$ is a subbasis for the
product topology on $X \times Y$. Munkres's proof turns on the single identity
$U \times V = \pi_1^{-1}(U) \cap \pi_2^{-1}(V)$; verify it and say which
inclusion of topologies each half of the argument gives.
(d) Using Theorem 15.1 and top-02's Problem 2(c), write down a **countable**
basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$, and prove it is one.

*(Munkres §15, p. 86 for the definition, the basis check and the remark that
$\mathcal{B}$ is not a topology; pp. 86–87 for Theorem 15.1; p. 88 for
Theorem 15.2.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), draw two disjoint open rectangles in the plane and ask whether their
union is a product.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) One inclusion is immediate because every element of $\mathcal{S}$ is open in
the product topology, and a topology contains all unions of finite intersections
of its members. The other needs the identity, which exhibits every *basis*
element of the product topology as a finite intersection of subbasis elements.
(d) Products of intervals with rational endpoints; the countable basis for
$\mathbb{R}$ is top-02's $\mathcal{B}_\mathbb{Q}$, and a product of two countable
sets is countable.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The union of $(0,1) \times (0,1)$ and $(2,3) \times (2,3)$ is open and is not
a product: its image under $\pi_1$ meets $(2,3)$ and its image under $\pi_2$
meets $(0,1)$, but the point $(0.5, 2.5)$ is not in it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Basis condition (1).* $X \times Y$ is itself of the form $U \times V$ with
$U = X$, $V = Y$, so every point lies in a basis element.
*Condition (2).* Given basis elements $U_1 \times V_1$ and $U_2 \times V_2$ and a
point in their intersection, Munkres's identity shows the intersection is itself
$(U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2)$, a basis element, since $U_1 \cap U_2$ and
$V_1 \cap V_2$ are open. So $B_3$ may be taken to be the intersection itself.
⟨your step: the open set that is not a product⟩

(c) $U \times V = \pi_1^{-1}(U) \cap \pi_2^{-1}(V)$, since a point $(x,y)$ lies
in the left side exactly when $x \in U$ and $y \in V$, which is exactly the two
conditions on the right.
⟨your step: run both inclusions and name which one each fact gives⟩

(b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — arbitrarily many factors, and the two candidates)

For an indexed family $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in J}$ Munkres gives two topologies
on $\prod X_\alpha$. The **box topology** has as basis all $\prod U_\alpha$ with
each $U_\alpha$ open (p. 114). The **product topology** is generated by the
subbasis $\mathcal{S} = \bigcup_\beta \mathcal{S}_\beta$, where
$\mathcal{S}_\beta = \{\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta) : U_\beta \text{ open in } X_\beta\}$
(p. 114).

(a) Verify that the box basis is a basis, using
$\left(\prod U_\alpha\right) \cap \left(\prod V_\alpha\right) = \prod (U_\alpha \cap V_\alpha)$.
Then check that $\mathcal{S}$ satisfies Munkres's condition for a subbasis —
that its union is the whole product — and say which $\beta$ and which $U_\beta$
you used.
(b) Derive Theorem 19.1 from the definitions, following Munkres's computation
(p. 115): intersecting two elements of the *same* $\mathcal{S}_\beta$ gives
nothing new, so a typical basis element is
$\pi_{\beta_1}^{-1}(U_{\beta_1}) \cap \cdots \cap \pi_{\beta_n}^{-1}(U_{\beta_n})$,
which equals $\prod U_\alpha$ with $U_\alpha = X_\alpha$ for all but finitely
many $\alpha$.
(c) Deduce Munkres's two immediate consequences: that for finite products the
two topologies are "precisely the same", and that in general the box topology is
finer. For the second, decide whether "finer" can be strengthened to "strictly
finer" for every infinite $J$, or whether extra hypotheses on the $X_\alpha$ are
needed — prove your answer, and check it against the case where every $X_\alpha$
is a one-point space.
(d) Prove Theorem 19.2 (Munkres's Exercise 1, p. 118): if each $X_\alpha$ has
basis $\mathcal{B}_\alpha$, then the sets $\prod B_\alpha$ form a basis for the
box topology, and the sets $\prod B_\alpha$ with $B_\alpha \in \mathcal{B}_\alpha$
for finitely many $\alpha$ and $B_\alpha = X_\alpha$ otherwise form a basis for
the product topology.

*(Munkres §19, p. 113 for $J$-tuples and the cartesian product; p. 114 for both
definitions and the projection maps; p. 115 for the basis computation,
Theorem 19.1 and the two consequences; p. 116 for Theorem 19.2, whose proof is
Exercise 1.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (c), test the one-point-space case before answering — it is the degenerate
case that decides whether the strengthening is legitimate.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) The box topology is finer because every product-topology basis element is a
box basis element. Strictness needs a box basis element that is *not* open in
the product topology, and to produce one you need infinitely many $X_\alpha$ to
have a proper non-empty open subset. If every $X_\alpha$ is a single point the
product is a single point and both topologies are the indiscrete one.
(d) Apply Lemma 13.2 (top-02) in each case: the candidate sets are open, and
every point of an open set has one of them around it inside.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Not for every infinite $J$. The correct statement: the box topology is
strictly finer as soon as infinitely many $X_\alpha$ have a proper non-empty open
subset. If all but finitely many $X_\alpha$ are indiscrete — one point being the
extreme case — the two topologies coincide.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Fix $\beta$ and note
$\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta) \cap \pi_\beta^{-1}(V_\beta) = \pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta \cap V_\beta)$,
so intersecting within one $\mathcal{S}_\beta$ stays in $\mathcal{S}_\beta$.
Hence a general finite intersection may be taken over *distinct* indices
$\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n$, giving
$$B = \pi_{\beta_1}^{-1}(U_{\beta_1}) \cap \cdots \cap \pi_{\beta_n}^{-1}(U_{\beta_n}).$$
A point $\mathbf{x} = (x_\alpha)$ lies in $B$ exactly when $x_{\beta_i} \in U_{\beta_i}$
for each $i$, with no condition at all on the other coordinates.
⟨your step: rewrite $B$ as a product $\prod U_\alpha$ and say what $U_\alpha$ is
for $\alpha \notin \{\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n\}$; then state Theorem 19.1⟩

(a), (c), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the theorems that do not care which topology you chose)

Munkres lists three results that hold in both topologies, and proves only the
last (p. 116).

(a) Prove Theorem 19.3 (his Exercise 2): if $A_\alpha$ is a subspace of
$X_\alpha$ for each $\alpha$, then $\prod A_\alpha$ is a subspace of
$\prod X_\alpha$, when both carry the box topology or both the product topology.
Say where the "both the same" hypothesis is used.
(b) Prove Theorem 19.4 (his Exercise 3): a product of Hausdorff spaces is
Hausdorff in either topology. Compare with top-03's Theorem 17.11, which gave
the two-factor case, and say what changes.
(c) Prove Theorem 19.5: $\prod \bar{A}_\alpha = \overline{\prod A_\alpha}$ in
either topology. Munkres proves this one; reproduce both directions and note
where the forward direction uses the axiom of choice.
(d) Theorem 19.5 is an *equality*, whereas top-04's Theorem 18.1(2) gave only
the inclusion $f(\bar{A}) \subset \overline{f(A)}$. Explain why a product
behaves better here than a general continuous image — that is, what the
projections give you that an arbitrary map does not.

*(Munkres §19, p. 115 for the remark that most proofs are left to the exercises;
p. 116 for Theorems 19.3, 19.4 and 19.5, the last with its proof; p. 118 for
Exercises 2 and 3.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c) forwards, you must pick a point $y_\alpha \in U_\alpha \cap A_\alpha$
for *every* $\alpha$ at once.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Compare bases: a basis element of the subspace topology on $\prod A_\alpha$
is $\left(\prod U_\alpha\right) \cap \prod A_\alpha = \prod (U_\alpha \cap A_\alpha)$,
which is the general basis element of the product (or box) topology on
$\prod A_\alpha$ — but only if the finiteness restriction on the $U_\alpha$
matches on both sides, which is why the topologies must be the same kind.
(d) Every projection is continuous *and* the basis elements of the product are
built from the factors, so the closure condition can be tested coordinatewise in
both directions. A general $f$ gives only one direction.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Forwards: given $\mathbf{x} \in \prod \bar{A}_\alpha$ and a basis element
$U = \prod U_\alpha$ containing it, choose $y_\alpha \in U_\alpha \cap A_\alpha$
for each $\alpha$ — this is a choice over the whole index set — and then
$\mathbf{y} \in U \cap \prod A_\alpha$.
Backwards: for a fixed $\beta$ and $V_\beta$ open about $x_\beta$, the set
$\pi_\beta^{-1}(V_\beta)$ is open in either topology and meets $\prod A_\alpha$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) *Forwards.* Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_\alpha) \in \prod \bar{A}_\alpha$ and let
$U = \prod U_\alpha$ be a basis element (box or product) containing $\mathbf{x}$.
For each $\alpha$ we have $x_\alpha \in \bar{A}_\alpha$ and $U_\alpha$ is open
containing $x_\alpha$, so by Theorem 17.5(a) $U_\alpha$ meets $A_\alpha$; choose
$y_\alpha \in U_\alpha \cap A_\alpha$. Then $\mathbf{y} = (y_\alpha)$ lies in
$U \cap \prod A_\alpha$. As $U$ was arbitrary, $\mathbf{x} \in \overline{\prod A_\alpha}$.
⟨your step: name the set-theoretic principle the phrase "choose $y_\alpha$ for
each $\alpha$" is invoking, and say why the backward direction does not need it⟩

⟨your step: the backward direction⟩

(a), (b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the theorem that separates them)

**Theorem 19.6** (p. 117): let $f : A \to \prod_{\alpha \in J} X_\alpha$ be
$f(a) = (f_\alpha(a))_{\alpha \in J}$ and give $\prod X_\alpha$ the **product**
topology. Then $f$ is continuous if and only if each $f_\alpha$ is continuous.

Munkres introduces it with: "Here is a theorem that does not hold if
$\prod X_\alpha$ is given the box topology" (p. 116).

(a) Prove Theorem 19.6. Both directions. In the converse direction, say which
reduction from top-04 lets you check only subbasis elements, and why that
reduction is what makes the *product* topology work.
(b) Work Munkres's Example 2 in full. Let
$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ be $f(t) = (t, t, t, \dots)$, and let
$$B = (-1,1) \times \left(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right) \times \left(-\tfrac13, \tfrac13\right) \times \cdots.$$
Show $B$ is open in the box topology; show each coordinate function of $f$ is
continuous; and show $f^{-1}(B)$ is not open in $\mathbb{R}$, following his
argument through $\pi_n$.
(c) Munkres's Exercise 5 (p. 118) asks: "One of the implications stated in
Theorem 19.6 holds for the box topology. Which one?" Answer it and prove the
surviving implication for the box topology. Then say, in one sentence, which
feature of the box topology kills the other implication.
(d) Compare with top-04. Theorem 18.4 was the two-factor case and needed no
choice of topology, because for finite products there is only one. State
precisely what Theorem 19.6 adds, and what top-04's Exercise 12 warning about
maps *out of* a product becomes in this setting — does the arbitrary-index
version make the domain case any better?

*(Munkres §19, p. 116 for the sentence introducing Theorem 19.6; p. 117 for the
theorem, its proof and Example 2; p. 118 for Exercise 5, which is set and not
answered in the text.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (b), if $f^{-1}(B)$ were open it would contain an interval $(-\delta,\delta)$
about $0$; push that through $\pi_n$ for every $n$ at once.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) The subbasis reduction of top-04 (§18, p. 103) says continuity may be
checked on subbasis elements alone. The product topology's subbasis elements are
exactly the $\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)$, and
$f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)) = f_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)$ — one coordinate at a
time. The box topology has no such small subbasis; its basis elements constrain
all coordinates simultaneously.
(c) The surviving implication is: $f$ continuous $\Rightarrow$ each $f_\alpha$
continuous. It survives because the projections remain continuous in the box
topology, and a composite of continuous maps is continuous.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) If $(-\delta,\delta) \subset f^{-1}(B)$ then
$f((-\delta,\delta)) \subset B$, so applying $\pi_n$ gives
$(-\delta,\delta) \subset (-1/n, 1/n)$ for every $n$ — impossible, since
$1/n \to 0$ while $\delta > 0$ is fixed.
(d) The domain case gets no better: Theorem 19.6 is still about maps *into* a
product, and top-04's Exercise 12 already lives inside $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$,
a finite product where box and product agree — so the counterexample is
untouched by anything in §19.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Projections are continuous.* If $U_\beta$ is open in $X_\beta$, then
$\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta)$ is by definition a subbasis element of the product
topology, hence open.
*Forwards.* $f_\beta = \pi_\beta \circ f$ is a composite of continuous maps,
hence continuous by Theorem 18.2(c).
*Backwards.* ⟨your step: invoke the subbasis reduction, compute
$f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U_\beta))$, and conclude⟩

(b) Each coordinate function is $f_n(t) = t$, the identity, which is continuous.
So $f$ is continuous into the product topology by Theorem 19.6. For the box
topology, $B = \prod_n (-1/n, 1/n)$ is a basis element, hence open.
⟨your step: suppose $f^{-1}(B)$ open, extract the interval about $0$, and derive
the contradiction⟩

(c), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — why "prefer", and what the section does not settle)

Munkres is unusually candid on p. 115. Having compared the two topologies he
writes: "What is not so clear is why we prefer the product topology to the box
topology. The answer will appear as we continue our study of topology." He then
records the convention that $\prod X_\alpha$ always carries the product topology
unless stated otherwise, and adds that the box topology "is not so important; we
shall use it primarily for constructing counterexamples."

(a) Assemble the evidence §19 itself supplies for the preference. List every
result in the section that holds in both topologies and every one that holds
only in the product topology, and say what the score is at the end of the
section. Is Theorem 19.6 the only separating result Munkres gives here?
(b) Munkres says the answer "will appear as we continue". Name two later
sections of his book where a theorem about finite products is generalised to
arbitrary products, and state what each would look like in the box topology.
You may cite section numbers and titles from the table of contents; do not
assert the content of a theorem you have not read.
(c) The box topology is not *wrong* — it is a topology, and Theorems 19.1
through 19.5 hold in it. Write one paragraph distinguishing "wrong" from "less
useful", using Theorem 19.6 and Example 2 as the evidence, and say what would
have to be true for a topology on $\prod X_\alpha$ to be wrong in a stronger
sense.
(d) **The mission clause.** This unit's mission strip says the subtlety here
"returns in multiparameter persistence". Using Theorem 19.1, decide whether it
does. State the number of parameters a multiparameter persistence module is
indexed by, say what Theorem 19.1 asserts for products of that many factors, and
give your verdict with the reason. If the verdict is negative, say what
*would* have to change about the indexing for the §19 subtlety to become
relevant.

*(Munkres §19, p. 115 for the quoted passages and the convention; pp. 115–117
for the inventory of results in (a). Part (b) asks for section titles only —
this unit has read §§12–19 and nothing later. Part (d) is not in Munkres at all
and is answered from Theorem 19.1 plus the definition of a multiparameter
module.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d), count the parameters. Is that number finite?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Both topologies: Theorems 19.2, 19.3, 19.4, 19.5. Product only: Theorem 19.6,
and by Exercise 5 only one of its two implications is lost. So within §19 the
score is four all-square and one half-result in favour — Munkres is right that
the section does not settle it.
(d) Theorem 19.1's first consequence is that for *finite* products the two
topologies are precisely the same. A multiparameter persistence module has
finitely many parameters — typically two or three — so any product of that many
factors is a finite product and the box/product distinction collapses.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) A topology would be *wrong* in the strong sense if it failed to make the
projections continuous, or failed to be a topology at all. The box topology does
neither. What it fails is a universal property: it is too fine for maps into it
to be detected coordinatewise.
(d) The subtlety does not return. It would become relevant only if the indexing
poset were an infinite product — infinitely many parameters — which no
multiparameter persistence module has.
</details>
