## Problem 1 (easy — contradiction, adapting a known proof) [interleaves an-01]

Adapt the classic $\sqrt2$ irrationality proof (Theorem 1.1.1) to show $\sqrt3$ is irrational. Then explain in one or two sentences why the identical argument, applied verbatim to $\sqrt4$, must fail somewhere — and say exactly where.

*(Abbott §1.1, Exercise 1.2.1, cited directly in the an-01 lesson's "you try")*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The $\sqrt2$ proof uses "$p^2$ even $\Rightarrow p$ even" at a key step. What is the analogous fact for 3 instead of 2?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Assume $\sqrt3=p/q$ in lowest terms, square, and use "$3\mid p^2 \Rightarrow 3\mid p$" (provable via cases on $p \bmod 3$, or by unique factorisation).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$3q^2=p^2$, so $3\mid p^2$, hence $3\mid p$; write $p=3k$. Substitute: $3q^2=9k^2$, so $q^2=3k^2$, hence $3\mid q$ too — contradicting lowest terms.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
This mirrors the $\sqrt2$ proof exactly, with 3 replacing 2 throughout, and works because 3 is prime (so "$3\mid p^2\Rightarrow3\mid p$" holds). For $\sqrt4$: the argument would need "$4\mid p^2\Rightarrow4\mid p$", which is **false** ($p=2$: $4\mid p^2=4$ but $4\nmid p=2$) — because 4 is not prime, so the key divisibility lemma breaks down. This is exactly why $\sqrt4=2$ is rational: the proof mechanism specifically requires primality.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — computing sup/inf)

Compute, without full formal proofs (informal justification is fine), the suprema and infima of the sets:

(a) $\{n/(n+1) : n\in\mathbb{N}\}$;
(b) $\{(-1)^n/n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$;
(c) $\{1/n - 1/m : n,m\in\mathbb{N}\}$.

*(Abbott §1.3, Exercise 1.3.6, p. 18)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), list the first few terms: $1/2, 2/3, 3/4,\ldots$ — where are they heading, and do they ever arrive?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Identify which bound (sup or inf) is actually attained as a max/min of the set, and which is only a limiting value never reached.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a): the terms increase toward 1 but never reach it (sup = 1, not attained); the smallest term is $n=1$, giving $1/2$ (inf = 1/2, attained, so it's a genuine minimum).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) sup $=1$ (not attained), inf $=1/2$ (attained, $=$ min). (b) terms oscillate: positive terms $1/2,1/4,\ldots\to0^+$, negative terms $-1,-1/3,\ldots\to0^-$; sup $=1/2$ (attained at $n=2$), inf $=-1$ (attained at $n=1$). (c) as $n\to\infty,m=1$: values approach $-1$; as $m\to\infty,n=1$: values approach $1$; sup $=1$ (not attained, e.g. $n=1,m\to\infty$), inf $=-1$ (not attained). State explicitly, for each set, whether the extremal value is a genuine max/min or only a limiting bound.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, from Definition 1.3.2)

Prove that if $a$ is an upper bound for $A$, and $a$ is also an element of $A$, then it must be that $a=\sup A$.

*(Abbott §1.3, Exercise 1.3.7, p. 18)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 1.3.2 has two parts. You already have part (i) for free (that's given). What remains?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show part (ii): if $b$ is any upper bound for $A$, then $a \le b$. Use the fact that $a\in A$ directly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Let $b$ be any upper bound for $A$. Since $a\in A$ and $b$ is an upper bound for $A$, by the definition of upper bound, $a\le b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
This establishes condition (ii) of Definition 1.3.2 for $s=a$: every upper bound $b$ satisfies $a\le b$. Combined with the given hypothesis that $a$ is itself an upper bound (condition (i)), both parts of Definition 1.3.2 hold for $s=a$, so $a=\sup A$. This is exactly the shortcut used in the an-01 lesson's worked Example 1.3.3 (sup $\{1/n\}=1$).
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, greatest lower bound from completeness) [interleaves an-01]

Let $A$ be bounded below, and define $B=\{b\in\mathbb{R} : b \text{ is a lower bound for } A\}$. Show that $\sup B=\inf A$.

*(Abbott §1.3, Exercise 1.3.3(a), p. 17 — this is the exact argument the an-01 lesson's self-check 4 references as the reason completeness doesn't need a separate infimum axiom)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$B$ is itself a set of real numbers — is it bounded above? If so, by the Axiom of Completeness, $\sup B$ exists.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show $\sup B$ satisfies both defining conditions of $\inf A$: (i) $\sup B$ is a lower bound for $A$, and (ii) $\sup B$ is the *greatest* lower bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$B$ is bounded above (any element of $A$ is an upper bound for $B$, since every lower bound of $A$ is $\le$ every element of $A$), so by completeness $\sup B$ exists; call it $s$. To show $s$ is a lower bound for $A$: suppose not, i.e. some $a_0\in A$ has $a_0<s$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If some $a_0\in A$ satisfied $a_0<s$, then $a_0$ would be an upper bound for $B$ smaller than $s=\sup B$ — contradicting that $s$ is the *least* upper bound. So no such $a_0$ exists; $s$ is a lower bound for $A$, i.e. $s\in B$. Since $s\in B$ and $B$ is exactly the set of lower bounds, and $s$ is an upper bound for $B$ (as $\sup B$), $s$ is at least as large as every lower bound of $A$ — i.e. $s$ is the greatest lower bound, $s=\inf A$. Write this up as a full two-directional argument.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — proof, existence via completeness, proof problem)

Finish the following: let $T=\{t\in\mathbb{R} : t^2<2\}$ and $\alpha=\sup T$ (which exists by the Axiom of Completeness). Show that the assumption $\alpha^2>2$ leads to a contradiction of the fact that $\alpha=\sup T$. (You may assume the case $\alpha^2<2$ has already been ruled out similarly.)

*(Abbott §1.4, Exercise 1.4.6(a), p. 27 — completing Theorem 1.4.5, the an-01 lesson's headline existence proof)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
If $\alpha^2>2$, you want to find a *smaller* upper bound for $T$ than $\alpha$ — that would contradict $\alpha$ being the *least* upper bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Try $\alpha - \epsilon$ for a suitably small $\epsilon>0$, and show $(\alpha-\epsilon)^2>2$ still, meaning $\alpha-\epsilon$ is still an upper bound for $T$ but smaller than $\alpha$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Since $\alpha^2>2$, let $\epsilon = \dfrac{\alpha^2-2}{2\alpha}>0$ (assuming $\alpha>0$, which holds since $\alpha \ge 1$ as $1\in T$). Show $\epsilon<\alpha$ and compute $(\alpha-\epsilon)^2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$(\alpha-\epsilon)^2 = \alpha^2 - 2\alpha\epsilon+\epsilon^2 > \alpha^2-2\alpha\epsilon = \alpha^2-(\alpha^2-2)=2$ (dropping the positive $\epsilon^2$ term only strengthens the inequality). So $(\alpha-\epsilon)^2>2$, meaning for any $t\in T$ (so $t^2<2$), $t^2<2<(\alpha-\epsilon)^2$, forcing $t<\alpha-\epsilon$ (since both are positive). Thus $\alpha-\epsilon$ is an upper bound for $T$ strictly smaller than $\alpha$ — contradicting $\alpha=\sup T$ being the *least* upper bound. Therefore $\alpha^2>2$ is impossible. **Any logically valid alternative completion (e.g. a slightly different choice of $\epsilon$) earns full marks provided the contradiction is airtight.**
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof problem, Archimedean-style rigour)

Use the Archimedean Property of $\mathbb{R}$ to rigorously prove that $\inf\{1/n : n\in\mathbb{N}\}=0$.

*(Abbott §1.4, Exercise 1.4.4, p. 27)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Showing $0$ is *a* lower bound is easy. The hard part — the part that genuinely needs the Archimedean Property — is showing it's the *greatest* lower bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use Definition 1.3.2's part (ii) style argument, but for infimum (flip the inequality direction): show that for any $\epsilon>0$, some element of the set is $<\epsilon$, so no positive number can be a lower bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$0$ is a lower bound: $1/n>0$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. Suppose for contradiction some $\epsilon>0$ is a lower bound greater than $0$. By the Archimedean Property, there exists $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $n>1/\epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Then $1/n<\epsilon$, contradicting that $\epsilon$ is a lower bound for $\{1/n\}$ (since $1/n$ is an element of the set smaller than the claimed lower bound $\epsilon$). So no $\epsilon>0$ can be a lower bound; combined with $0$ being a lower bound, $0$ is the *greatest* lower bound, i.e. $\inf\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}=0$. Cite the Archimedean Property precisely as the tool that produces the needed $n$.
</details>
