## Problem 1 (easy — proof, linearity as a subspace condition) [interleaves la-05]

Let $T$ be a function from $V$ to $W$. The **graph** of $T$ is the subset of $V\times W$ defined by
$$\text{graph of } T = \{(v,Tv)\in V\times W : v\in V\}.$$
Prove that $T$ is a linear map if and only if the graph of $T$ is a subspace of $V\times W$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 1, p. 98)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Both directions run through la-02's three-condition subspace test applied inside $V\times W$, where the operations are performed slot by slot (Definition 3.71). Write out what "closed under addition" says about a pair of points of the graph.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Closure under addition says $(v,Tv)+(u,Tu)=(v+u,Tv+Tu)$ is in the graph. But every element of the graph with first slot $v+u$ has second slot $T(v+u)$ — that is what makes the graph a *function's* graph. So closure is exactly additivity. Do the same for scalar multiplication, and check the identity condition separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) If $T$ is linear: $(0,T0)=(0,0)$ is in the graph by la-05's Theorem 3.11; sums and scalar multiples of graph points are graph points by additivity and homogeneity. ($\Leftarrow$) If the graph is a subspace, closure under addition gives $(v+u, Tv+Tu)$ in the graph, and comparing second slots with $(v+u,T(v+u))$ gives $T(v+u)=Tv+Tu$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The comparison step deserves a sentence of its own: two elements of the graph with the same first slot must be equal, because the graph contains exactly one point with each first slot. That is where being a function is used, and it is the hinge of the whole argument. Homogeneity comes out the same way from closure under scaling.

Note that the identity condition is *not* automatic in the ($\Leftarrow$) direction either — but you get it free, since a subspace contains $0=(0,0)$ and the only graph point with first slot $0$ is $(0,T0)$, forcing $T0=0$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, the subspace is determined)

Suppose $v,x$ are vectors in $V$ and $U,W$ are subspaces of $V$ such that $v+U=x+W$. Prove that $U=W$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 7, p. 99)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
An affine subset can be written as a translate in many ways, but the statement says the *subspace* is not one of the things that can vary. Start by locating a convenient point of the common set.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$0\in U$, so $v\in v+U=x+W$; write $v=x+w_0$ with $w_0\in W$. Then $v-x\in W$, so Theorem 3.85 (applied with the subspace $W$) gives $v+W=x+W$. Substituting, $v+U=v+W$, and now the two translates share the same base point — which makes comparing $U$ and $W$ a matter of cancelling $v$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For $u\in U$: $v+u\in v+U=v+W$, so $v+u=v+w$ for some $w\in W$, whence $u=w\in W$. So $U\subseteq W$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The reverse inclusion is the same argument with the roles exchanged, so $U=W$. Two remarks. First, the step $v+U=x+W\Rightarrow v+W=x+W$ uses Theorem 3.85 with $W$, not with $U$ — getting the subspace right there is the only subtlety. Second, the result is what makes the phrase "parallel to $U$" in Definition 3.81 well posed: an affine subset determines the subspace it is parallel to, so there is no ambiguity in speaking of *the* subspace.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, lifting a basis through the quotient) [interleaves la-04]

Suppose $U$ is a subspace of $V$, that $v_1+U,\dots,v_m+U$ is a basis of $V/U$, and that $u_1,\dots,u_n$ is a basis of $U$. Prove that $v_1,\dots,v_m,u_1,\dots,u_n$ is a basis of $V$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 13, p. 100)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Two things to check: spanning and independence. For spanning, take $v\in V$, push it into the quotient with the quotient map $\pi$, and expand there — then correct the error, which will lie in $U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Spanning: $v+U=\sum a_j(v_j+U)=(\sum a_jv_j)+U$, so $v-\sum a_jv_j\in U$ by Theorem 3.85 and is therefore a combination of the $u_k$. Independence: from $\sum a_jv_j+\sum b_ku_k=0$, apply $\pi$; the $u$-terms die because $\operatorname{null}\pi=U$, leaving a relation among the $v_j+U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Applying $\pi$ gives $\sum a_j(v_j+U)=0+U$, so every $a_j=0$ by independence in $V/U$. The original relation collapses to $\sum b_ku_k=0$, and independence of $u_1,\dots,u_n$ finishes it.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So the combined list spans and is independent, hence is a basis. Two things to state. The identity $\sum a_j(v_j+U)=(\sum a_jv_j)+U$ is Definition 3.86 applied repeatedly, not an obvious rearrangement, since the operations on the left live in $V/U$ and those on the right in $V$. And the result gives a second proof of Theorem 3.89, since the basis just built has length $m+n=\dim V/U+\dim U$ — you may note that, but do not use Theorem 3.89 in the proof, or the argument becomes circular.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, codimension one) [interleaves la-08]

Suppose $U$ is a subspace of $V$ such that $\dim V/U=1$. Prove that there exists $\varphi\in\mathcal{L}(V,\mathbb{F})$ such that $\operatorname{null}\varphi=U$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 16, p. 100)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You already have one linear map out of $V$ whose null space is exactly $U$ — but it lands in $V/U$, not in $\mathbb{F}$. Fix that by composing with something.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The quotient map $\pi:V\to V/U$ has $\operatorname{null}\pi=U$. Since $\dim V/U=1=\dim\mathbb{F}$, la-08's Theorem 3.59 supplies an isomorphism $\psi:V/U\to\mathbb{F}$. Put $\varphi=\psi\circ\pi$ and compute its null space, using injectivity of $\psi$ at the key step.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\varphi$ is linear as a composition of linear maps. $\varphi(v)=0$ means $\psi(\pi v)=0$; since $\psi$ is injective, $\operatorname{null}\psi=\{0\}$ (la-06, Theorem 3.16), so $\pi v=0+U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
And $\pi v=0+U$ means $v\in U$ by Theorem 3.85, while conversely every $u\in U$ has $\pi u=0+U$ and hence $\varphi(u)=0$. So $\operatorname{null}\varphi=U$ exactly.

Say why the composition is the natural move: $\pi$ is the *only* map you are handed whose null space is prescribed, and everything else is bookkeeping to change its target. Note also that $\varphi$ is far from unique — any nonzero scalar multiple works, and so does any other choice of $\psi$ — which is worth one sentence, because "there exists" problems are often marked down for implying a canonical answer.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — a characterisation of affine subsets)

Prove that a nonempty subset $A$ of $V$ is an affine subset of $V$ if and only if
$$\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w\in A$$
for all $v,w\in A$ and all $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 8, p. 99)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The forward direction is a computation. For the converse you must *produce* a subspace: pick any $a\in A$ and consider the translated set $U=\{x-a : x\in A\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) Write $A=a+U$, $v=a+u_1$, $w=a+u_2$, and expand $\lambda v+(1-\lambda)w$; the $a$-terms recombine into a single $a$ because the coefficients sum to $1$. ($\Leftarrow$) Show $U=A-a$ satisfies la-02's three conditions. Containing $0$ is immediate. Closure under scaling comes from the hypothesis with $w=a$. Closure under addition needs the hypothesis applied to two vectors you first have to build, using the scaling you have just proved.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) $\lambda(a+u_1)+(1-\lambda)(a+u_2)=a+(\lambda u_1+(1-\lambda)u_2)\in a+U$.
($\Leftarrow$) For $u=x-a\in U$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$: $\lambda x+(1-\lambda)a\in A$ by hypothesis, and that vector minus $a$ is $\lambda u$, so $\lambda u\in U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For closure under addition, let $u_1,u_2\in U$. By the scaling just proved, $2u_1,2u_2\in U$, i.e. $a+2u_1$ and $a+2u_2$ are in $A$. Apply the hypothesis to those two with $\lambda=\tfrac12$:
$$\tfrac12(a+2u_1)+\tfrac12(a+2u_2)=a+u_1+u_2\in A,$$
so $u_1+u_2\in U$. Hence $U$ is a subspace and $A=a+U$ is affine.

Two points for the write-up. The coefficients $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ summing to $1$ is what keeps the base point $a$ from being scaled away — that is the entire reason the condition characterises affine rather than linear subsets. And the addition step uses $\tfrac12$, so it depends on $2$ being invertible in $\mathbb{F}$; that is fine for $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, and worth a remark, since the statement genuinely fails over a field of characteristic $2$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the universal property of the quotient)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ and $U$ is a subspace of $V$. Let $\pi$ denote the quotient map from $V$ onto $V/U$. Prove that there exists $S\in\mathcal{L}(V/U,W)$ with $T=S\circ\pi$ **if and only if** $U\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$.

*(Axler §3.E, Exercise 18, p. 100)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction is a two-line computation once you know what $\pi$ does to a vector of $U$. The other asks you to build $S$, and the only possible formula is forced — the difficulty is showing that formula defines anything at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) For $u\in U$, $\pi u=u+U=0+U$ by Theorem 3.85, so $Tu=S(0+U)=0$. ($\Leftarrow$) Define $S(v+U)=Tv$. This is the only formula compatible with $T=S\circ\pi$, so there is nothing to choose; the work is entirely in checking it is well defined, and that is where $U\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$ is spent. Compare Definition 3.90, which is this construction in the special case $U=\operatorname{null}T$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Well-definedness: if $v+U=v'+U$ then $v-v'\in U\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$, so $T(v-v')=0$ and $Tv=Tv'$. Hence the value $S(v+U)$ does not depend on the representative chosen.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Linearity of $S$ follows from the definitions of the operations on $V/U$: $S((v+U)+(w+U))=S((v+w)+U)=T(v+w)=Tv+Tw=S(v+U)+S(w+U)$, and similarly for scalars. Finally $(S\circ\pi)(v)=S(v+U)=Tv$, so $T=S\circ\pi$.

What the result says is worth stating in your final sentence, because it is the reason quotients exist. *A linear map factors through $V/U$ exactly when it already ignores $U$.* The quotient is the universal way of forgetting $U$: anything that was going to forget $U$ anyway can be rerouted through it, and nothing else can. In the mission's setting, with $U$ the boundaries and $T$ any construction that vanishes on boundaries, this is the theorem that lets the construction be defined on homology classes rather than on cycles.
</details>
