## Problem 1 (easy — the Leibniz rule and its consequences)

(a) Take $\varphi, \psi \in C^0(X;R)$. Compute both sides of Lemma 3.6 directly
on a singular $1$-simplex $\sigma$ and verify the identity in this case.
(b) Using Lemma 3.6, prove that the cup product of two cocycles is a cocycle.
(c) Using Lemma 3.6, prove that the cup product of a cocycle and a coboundary is
a coboundary, in either order. Note where the sign $(-1)^k$ matters and where it
does not.
(d) Conclude that there is a well-defined induced product
$H^k(X;R) \times H^\ell(X;R) \to H^{k+\ell}(X;R)$, and identify the identity
element when $R$ has one.

*(Hatcher §3.2, Lemma 3.6 and the paragraph following it, pp. 206–207)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$(\varphi \smile \psi)(\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma|[v_0,\dots,v_k])\,\psi(\sigma|[v_k,\dots,v_{k+\ell}])$,
the product on the right being taken in $R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) and (c) are one-line consequences: set $\delta\varphi = 0$, or
$\delta\psi = 0$, in the Leibniz rule and read what is left.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $\delta\varphi = 0$ then $\delta(\varphi \smile \psi) = (-1)^k \varphi \smile \delta\psi$,
so $\varphi \smile \delta\psi = \pm\delta(\varphi \smile \psi)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For $\varphi,\psi \in C^0$ and $\sigma : [v_0,v_1] \to X$,
$(\varphi \smile \psi)(\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma|[v_0])\psi(\sigma|[v_0])$ when
$k = \ell = 0$ — both factors are evaluated at $v_0$ since $k = 0$ — so
$\varphi \smile \psi$ is the pointwise product function. Then
$\delta(\varphi\smile\psi)(\sigma) = \varphi(v_1)\psi(v_1) - \varphi(v_0)\psi(v_0)$,
while $(\delta\varphi \smile \psi)(\sigma) + (\varphi \smile \delta\psi)(\sigma) = (\varphi(v_1)-\varphi(v_0))\psi(v_1) + \varphi(v_0)(\psi(v_1)-\psi(v_0))$.
Expanding, the two middle terms cancel and the identity holds.
(b) If $\delta\varphi = 0$ and $\delta\psi = 0$ then Lemma 3.6 gives
$\delta(\varphi\smile\psi) = 0$.
(c) If $\delta\varphi = 0$ then $\delta(\varphi\smile\psi) = (-1)^k\varphi\smile\delta\psi$,
so $\varphi \smile \delta\psi = \pm\delta(\varphi\smile\psi)$ is a coboundary; if
$\delta\psi = 0$ then $\delta\varphi \smile \psi = \delta(\varphi \smile \psi)$
outright. The sign is invisible in the second case and only affects which
coboundary in the first — either way the class is zero.
(d) By (b) the product of cocycles is a cocycle, and by (c) changing either
representative by a coboundary changes the product by a coboundary, so the class
of $\varphi \smile \psi$ depends only on the classes of $\varphi$ and $\psi$.
The identity is the class $1 \in H^0(X;R)$ of the $0$-cocycle taking the value
$1$ on every singular $0$-simplex.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — Example 3.9 by hand)

Let $X_m$ be the $2$-complex obtained by attaching a $2$-cell to $S^1$ by the
degree $m$ map, with the $\Delta$-complex structure of Example 3.9: a regular
$m$-gon of triangles $T_i$ around a central vertex, outer edges identified.

(a) Explain why the cocycle condition on $\varphi$ forces
$\varphi(e_i) + \varphi(e) = \varphi(e_{i+1})$, and why $\varphi(e_i) = i$ is a
valid choice.
(b) Compute $(\varphi \smile \varphi)(T_i)$ and then the value of
$\varphi \smile \varphi$ on the generator $\sum_i T_i$ of $H_2(X_m;\mathbb{Z}_m)$.
(c) Evaluate that sum in $\mathbb{Z}_m$ for $m = 3$, $m = 4$ and $m = 6$, and
state $\alpha^2$ in each case.
(d) Why is the cup product structure of $X_m$ with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients
uninteresting, while the $\mathbb{Z}_m$ one is not?

*(Hatcher §3.2, Example 3.9, pp. 208–209)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In $\mathbb{Z}_m$, the sum $0 + 1 + \cdots + (m-1)$ is $0$ for odd $m$ and $k$
for $m = 2k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Pair off $1$ with $m-1$, $2$ with $m-2$, and so on; what is left over depends on
the parity of $m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(\varphi \smile \varphi)(T_i) = \varphi(e_i)\varphi(e) = i \cdot 1 = i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\delta\varphi(T_i) = \varphi(e_i) + \varphi(e) - \varphi(e_{i+1})$ by the
coboundary formula on the triangle $T_i$, whose boundary edges are $e_i$, $e$,
$e_{i+1}$ in the stated ordering; setting this to zero gives the recurrence.
Taking $\varphi(e) = 1$, the recurrence has the solution $\varphi(e_i) = i$ in
$\mathbb{Z}_m$, consistent mod $m$ because subscripts are taken mod $m$.
(b) $(\varphi\smile\varphi)(T_i) = \varphi(e_i)\varphi(e) = i$, so on
$\sum_i T_i$ the value is $0 + 1 + \cdots + (m-1)$.
(c) $m = 3$: $0+1+2 = 3 = 0$, so $\alpha^2 = 0$. $m = 4$: $0+1+2+3 = 6 = 2$, so
$\alpha^2 = 2\beta$. $m = 6$: $0+1+\cdots+5 = 15 = 3$, so $\alpha^2 = 3\beta$.
In general $\alpha^2 = 0$ for odd $m$ and $\alpha^2 = k\beta$ for $m = 2k$.
(d) With $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients $H^n(X_m;\mathbb{Z})$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ for
$n = 0$ and $\mathbb{Z}_m$ for $n = 2$, with $H^1 = 0$; there are no
$1$-dimensional classes to multiply, so the ring is determined by the additive
structure. With $\mathbb{Z}_m$ coefficients all three of $H^0, H^1, H^2$ are
$\mathbb{Z}_m$, so a product of two $1$-dimensional classes can land somewhere
non-zero — and does, for even $m$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — what graded commutativity forbids)

(a) State Theorem 3.11 and derive from it that $2\alpha^2 = 0$ for every
$\alpha \in H^k(X,A;R)$ with $k$ odd.
(b) Deduce that if $H^{2k}(X,A;R)$ has no element of order two then $\alpha^2 = 0$
for every $\alpha \in H^k(X,A;R)$ with $k$ odd.
(c) Apply (b) to $X_m$ of Problem 2 with $R = \mathbb{Z}_m$ to bound the
possibilities for $\alpha^2$, for $m$ odd and for $m$ even, and compare with the
answer you computed. Which case does Theorem 3.11 settle outright, and which
does it merely restrict?
(d) In Example 3.7 the relations include $\alpha_i \smile \alpha_i = 0$.
Show that Theorem 3.11 alone already forces this, given what $H^2(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is.

*(Hatcher §3.2, Theorem 3.11 and the remarks after it, p. 210; Example 3.7, pp. 207–208)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Put $\beta = \alpha$ and $\ell = k$ in the identity of Theorem 3.11.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$\alpha^2 = (-1)^{k^2}\alpha^2$, and $k^2$ is odd exactly when $k$ is.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free, so it has no element of
order two.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Theorem 3.11 says $\alpha \smile \beta = (-1)^{k\ell}\beta \smile \alpha$ for
$\alpha \in H^k(X,A;R)$, $\beta \in H^\ell(X,A;R)$, $R$ commutative. Taking
$\beta = \alpha$ and $\ell = k$ gives $\alpha^2 = (-1)^{k^2}\alpha^2 = -\alpha^2$
when $k$ is odd, i.e. $2\alpha^2 = 0$.
(b) $\alpha^2 \in H^{2k}(X,A;R)$ and $2\alpha^2 = 0$, so $\alpha^2$ has order
dividing $2$; if there is no element of order exactly two, $\alpha^2 = 0$.
(c) For $m$ odd, $H^2(X_m;\mathbb{Z}_m) \cong \mathbb{Z}_m$ has odd order, so no
element of order two, and (b) gives $\alpha^2 = 0$ outright — matching the
computation. For $m$ even, $\mathbb{Z}_m$ has exactly one element of order two,
namely $k = m/2$, so Theorem 3.11 restricts $\alpha^2$ to $0$ or $k\beta$ but
cannot decide between them; Example 3.9's explicit computation is what settles it
as $k\beta$.
(d) $\alpha_i \in H^1(M;\mathbb{Z})$ has odd dimension, so $2\alpha_i^2 = 0$; and
$\alpha_i^2 \in H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, which is torsion-free and so
has no element of order two. Hence $\alpha_i^2 = 0$ without any computation with
cocycles.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — computing in a cohomology ring) [interleaves aa-05]

Let $M$ be the closed orientable surface of genus $2$, with the classes
$\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2 \in H^1(M;\mathbb{Z})$ and generator
$\gamma \in H^2(M;\mathbb{Z})$ of Example 3.7, satisfying
$\alpha_i \smile \beta_j = \gamma$ if $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise,
$\alpha_i \smile \alpha_j = \beta_i \smile \beta_j = 0$, and
$\alpha_i \smile \beta_j = -(\beta_i \smile \alpha_j)$.

(a) Using distributivity, compute $(\alpha_1 + \beta_2) \smile (\beta_1 + \alpha_2)$.
(b) Compute $(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \beta_1 + \beta_2)^2$ and explain why the
answer is not simply a sum of squares.
(c) Verify that the graded ring $H^*(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is commutative in the graded
sense but not commutative in the ordinary sense, exhibiting a specific pair of
elements that fail to commute.
(d) Write down the additive structure of $H^*(M;\mathbb{Z})$ as a graded abelian
group, and say why $H^*(M;\mathbb{Z})$ is *not* isomorphic as a graded ring to
the exterior algebra $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\beta_1,\beta_2]$
of Example 3.13.

*(Hatcher §3.2, Example 3.7 pp. 207–208; the graded ring and Example 3.13, pp. 212–213)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Expand as you would in any ring, then apply the relation table term by term —
but keep the order of factors, since the ring is not commutative.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), count ranks: the exterior algebra on four odd generators has total rank
$2^4 = 16$, spread over dimensions $0$ to $4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(\alpha_1+\beta_2)\smile(\beta_1+\alpha_2) = \alpha_1\smile\beta_1 + \alpha_1\smile\alpha_2 + \beta_2\smile\beta_1 + \beta_2\smile\alpha_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Expanding, the four terms are $\alpha_1 \smile \beta_1 = \gamma$,
$\alpha_1 \smile \alpha_2 = 0$, $\beta_2 \smile \beta_1 = 0$, and
$\beta_2 \smile \alpha_2 = -(\alpha_2 \smile \beta_2) = -\gamma$. The sum is
$\gamma - \gamma = 0$.
(b) Write $x = \alpha_1+\alpha_2+\beta_1+\beta_2$. Expanding $x \smile x$ gives
sixteen terms. All $\alpha_i\smile\alpha_j$ and $\beta_i\smile\beta_j$ vanish.
The surviving terms pair off: $\alpha_i \smile \beta_i = \gamma$ and
$\beta_i \smile \alpha_i = -\gamma$ cancel for each $i$, and the cross terms
with $i \neq j$ are zero. So $x^2 = 0$. It is not a sum of squares because the
ring is only graded commutative: the cross terms $\alpha_i\smile\beta_i$ and
$\beta_i\smile\alpha_i$ do not combine into $2\alpha_i\smile\beta_i$, they
cancel.
(c) Graded commutativity holds by Theorem 3.11. Ordinary commutativity fails:
$\alpha_1 \smile \beta_1 = \gamma$ while $\beta_1 \smile \alpha_1 = -\gamma$,
and $\gamma \neq -\gamma$ in $H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
(d) $H^0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$, $H^1 \cong \mathbb{Z}^4$, $H^2 \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and
$H^n = 0$ for $n > 2$; total rank $6$. The exterior algebra
$\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\beta_1,\beta_2]$ has rank $2^4 = 16$ and
is non-zero in dimensions up to $4$, so it cannot be isomorphic to
$H^*(M;\mathbb{Z})$ as a graded ring — or even as a graded abelian group. The
relations of Example 3.7 are strictly more than the exterior-algebra relations:
they also kill $\alpha_1\smile\beta_2$ and everything above dimension $2$.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — cup products as an obstruction)

(a) Prove Proposition 3.10: for $f : X \to Y$, the induced map satisfies
$f^*(\alpha \smile \beta) = f^*(\alpha) \smile f^*(\beta)$. Work at cochain
level and say why the cochain identity descends to cohomology.
(b) Deduce that a homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism of graded rings,
so the cup product structure is a homotopy invariant.
(c) Use Example 3.14 together with (b) to prove that $\mathbb{CP}^2$ is not
homotopy equivalent to $S^2 \vee S^4$, being explicit about which cup product is
non-zero and which must vanish.
(d) The mission statement for this unit asserts that $S^2 \times S^1$ and
$S^2 \vee S^1 \vee S^3$ have isomorphic homology groups but non-isomorphic
cohomology rings. Using only what is proved in this unit, say which half of that
assertion you can already justify and which half needs a result from at2-05, and
name the result.

*(Hatcher §3.2, Proposition 3.10 p. 210; Example 3.14, pp. 213–214)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The cochain-level identity is $f^\sharp(\varphi) \smile f^\sharp(\psi) = f^\sharp(\varphi \smile \psi)$;
evaluate both sides on a singular simplex $\sigma$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the wedge-sum ring isomorphism of Example 3.14 forces every product of
two positive-dimensional classes coming from different wedge factors to vanish,
and each factor's own ring has nothing in the right dimension.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^*(\mathbb{CP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$ with
$|\alpha| = 2$, so $\alpha^2 \neq 0$ in $H^4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Evaluate on $\sigma : \Delta^{k+\ell} \to X$:
$(f^\sharp\varphi \smile f^\sharp\psi)(\sigma) = f^\sharp\varphi(\sigma|[v_0,\dots,v_k])\,f^\sharp\psi(\sigma|[v_k,\dots,v_{k+\ell}]) = \varphi(f\sigma|[v_0,\dots,v_k])\,\psi(f\sigma|[v_k,\dots,v_{k+\ell}]) = (\varphi\smile\psi)(f\sigma) = f^\sharp(\varphi\smile\psi)(\sigma)$.
Since $f^\sharp$ is a cochain map it carries cocycles to cocycles and
coboundaries to coboundaries, so the identity descends to classes.
(b) If $f : X \to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence with homotopy inverse $g$, then
$f^*$ and $g^*$ are ring homomorphisms by (a) and mutually inverse additive
isomorphisms by homotopy invariance, hence mutually inverse ring isomorphisms.
(c) $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^2;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$ with
$|\alpha| = 2$, so the square of a generator of $H^2$ is a generator of $H^4$,
in particular non-zero. For the wedge, Example 3.14 gives a ring isomorphism
$\tilde{H}^*(S^2 \vee S^4;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \tilde{H}^*(S^2;\mathbb{Z}) \oplus \tilde{H}^*(S^4;\mathbb{Z})$
with coordinatewise multiplication, so the square of any element of $H^2$ is
computed coordinatewise: in the $S^2$ coordinate it lands in
$\tilde{H}^4(S^2) = 0$, and in the $S^4$ coordinate the element itself is zero.
So every square in $H^2$ vanishes. By (b) the two spaces cannot be homotopy
equivalent, and in particular the attaching map $f : S^3 \to S^2$ of the
$4$-cell is not homotopic to a constant.
(d) The vanishing half is available now: by Example 3.14 the reduced cohomology
ring of $S^2 \vee S^1 \vee S^3$ is a product of the three factors' rings with
coordinatewise multiplication, so every product of two positive-dimensional
classes is zero. The other half — that $H^*(S^2\times S^1;\mathbb{Z})$ has a
non-zero product of a $1$-dimensional and a $2$-dimensional class — needs the
cohomology of a product, which is Theorem 3.15, the Künneth formula of at2-05.
The homology computation is likewise outside this unit.
</details>
