## Problem 1 (easy — the Euclidean algorithm, forwards and backwards)

(a) Use the Euclidean algorithm to compute $\gcd(1234, 288)$, showing every division-with-remainder step.

(b) Then run the algorithm **backwards** to produce integers $m, n$ with $\gcd(1234, 288) = 1234m + 288n$.

(c) Your $m, n$ are not the only pair that works. Find a second pair, and describe all of them.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Each step divides the previous divisor by the previous remainder. The last nonzero remainder is the gcd (Theorem 1.14, p. 11). For (b), start from the equation that produced the gcd and substitute the earlier remainders one at a time.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$1234 = 288\cdot 4 + 82$; $288 = 82\cdot 3 + 42$; $82 = 42\cdot 1 + 40$; $42 = 40\cdot 1 + 2$; $40 = 2\cdot 20 + 0$. For (c), adding $288k$ to $m$ and subtracting $1234k$ from $n$ changes nothing.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The last nonzero remainder is $2$, so $\gcd(1234,288) = 2$. Backwards: $2 = 42 - 40$, and $40 = 82 - 42$, so $2 = 42 - (82 - 42) = 2\cdot 42 - 82$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Continue: $42 = 288 - 3\cdot 82$, so $2 = 2(288 - 3\cdot 82) - 82 = 2\cdot 288 - 7\cdot 82$. And $82 = 1234 - 4\cdot 288$, so $2 = 2\cdot 288 - 7(1234 - 4\cdot 288) = -7\cdot 1234 + 30\cdot 288$. Check: $-8638 + 8640 = 2$. ✓ For (c), the general solution is $m = -7 + 144k$, $n = 30 - 617k$ — note $288/2 = 144$ and $1234/2 = 617$, not $288$ and $1234$, since dividing through by the gcd is what makes the shift minimal.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — reading Definition 1.5 exactly as written)

(a) Prove directly from Definition 1.5 (p. 7) that $\gcd(a, 0) = |a|$ for every integer $a$, including $a = 0$.

(b) Aluffi's second requirement on $d$ is that **every** common divisor $c$ of $a$ and $b$ *divides* $d$ — not merely that $c \le d$. Remark 1.7 (p. 8) explains why. Give the reason in your own words, and say what would go wrong with the $c \le d$ version at $a = b = 0$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a) you must check both requirements: that $|a|$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $0$, and that every common divisor of $a$ and $0$ divides $|a|$. Remember that every integer divides $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $|a| \mid a$ always, and $|a| \mid 0$ since $0 = |a|\cdot 0$. If $c \mid a$ and $c \mid 0$, then $c \mid a$, and $c \mid a$ gives $c \mid |a|$. Nonnegativity pins the answer to $|a|$ rather than $-|a|$. (b) Ask what the common divisors of $0$ and $0$ are.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) At $a = 0$: every integer divides $0$, so the common divisors of $0$ and $0$ are all of $\mathbb{Z}$, and the nonnegative integer divisible by all of them is $0$. So $\gcd(0,0) = 0 = |0|$, consistent with the general argument.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) The divisibility form is the one that survives the move to other rings: "$\le$" needs an ordering, and a general ring has none, while "divides" is written in the ring's own multiplication. The concrete damage is at $a = b = 0$: every integer is a common divisor, so there is no largest one and the $c \le d$ version defines nothing, whereas the divisibility version gives $\gcd(0,0) = 0$ without a special case. This is the first place in the book where a definition is chosen for its portability rather than its familiarity — and the same choice is made again for prime and irreducible in §1.4.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — Bézout does the work)

(a) Prove Corollary 1.11 (p. 10) from Theorem 1.8 (p. 8): if $a \mid bc$ and $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, then $a \mid c$.

(b) Remark 1.12 (p. 11) points out that the coprimality hypothesis cannot be dropped. Give an explicit triple $a, b, c$ with $a \mid bc$, $a \nmid b$ and $a \nmid c$, and identify exactly which line of your proof in (a) fails for it.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
By Theorem 1.8 there are $m, n$ with $ma + nb = 1$. Multiply that equation by $c$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
After multiplying by $c$ you have $mac + nbc = c$. Both terms on the left are divisible by $a$ — the first visibly, the second by hypothesis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$a \mid mac$ trivially. Since $a \mid bc$, write $bc = ak$; then $nbc = nak$, so $a \mid nbc$. Hence $a$ divides the sum, which is $c$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Take $a = 10$, $b = 6$, $c = 5$: $10 \mid 30$, but $10 \nmid 6$ and $10 \nmid 5$. The line that fails is the first one: $\gcd(10,6) = 2$, so Theorem 1.8 gives $10m + 6n = 2$, never $1$, and there is no equation to multiply by $c$. Notice the hypothesis is used exactly once, at the very start, and everything after it is bookkeeping — which is a good sign that coprimality is the real content of the statement.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — two divisors that do not overlap)

Suppose $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, and let $c$ be an integer with $a \mid c$ and $b \mid c$. Prove that $ab \mid c$.

Then show by example that the conclusion genuinely needs $\gcd(a,b) = 1$: exhibit $a, b, c$ with $a \mid c$ and $b \mid c$ but $ab \nmid c$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Write $c = ak$ using $a \mid c$, then use $b \mid c$ to say something about $k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
From $c = ak$ and $b \mid ak$ with $\gcd(b,a) = 1$, Corollary 1.11 (p. 10) applies with the roles $b \mid a\cdot k$: it gives $b \mid k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
So $k = b\ell$ for some integer $\ell$, and therefore $c = ak = ab\ell$, i.e. $ab \mid c$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For the example take $a = 4$, $b = 6$, $c = 12$: $4 \mid 12$ and $6 \mid 12$, but $ab = 24 \nmid 12$. Here $\gcd(4,6) = 2$, and the step that breaks is the appeal to Corollary 1.11: $6 \mid 4\cdot 3$ does not force $6 \mid 3$. The general repair is $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \mid c$, and $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = |ab|/\gcd(a,b)$ — which is $|ab|$ exactly when the two are coprime.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — prime and irreducible are not the same idea)

Theorem 1.21 (p. 14) says that for $p \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, $p$ is prime (Definition 1.19, p. 14) if and only if $p$ is irreducible (Definition 1.17, p. 13).

(a) Prove the direction **prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible**.

(b) Prove the direction **irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime**, using Lemma 1.18 (p. 13).

(c) One of the two directions uses Theorem 1.8 and one does not. Say which, and state precisely where it enters.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), suppose $p$ is prime and $d \mid p$; write $p = de$ and apply the prime property to the product $de$. For (b), suppose $p$ is irreducible and $p \mid bc$; if $p \nmid b$, what does Lemma 1.18 give you?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) From $p \mid de$ get $p \mid d$ or $p \mid e$; combine with $d \mid p$ (or $e \mid p$) to force $d = \pm 1$ or $d = \pm p$. (b) Lemma 1.18 gives $\gcd(p,b) = 1$; then Corollary 1.11 finishes it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Say $p \mid d$. With $d \mid p$ this gives $|d| = |p|$, so $d = \pm p$. If instead $p \mid e$, then $e = \pm p$ and $d = \pm 1$. Either way the only divisors of $p$ are $\pm 1, \pm p$, and $p \ne \pm 1$ is part of both definitions.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Let $p$ be irreducible and suppose $p \mid bc$. If $p \mid b$ we are done, so assume $p \nmid b$. Lemma 1.18 then gives $\gcd(p,b) = 1$, and Corollary 1.11 applied to $p \mid bc$ yields $p \mid c$. So $p$ is prime.

(c) Direction (a) is pure divisibility bookkeeping and uses nothing beyond the two definitions. Direction (b) uses Theorem 1.8 twice over, though at one remove: Lemma 1.18 is proved from the definition of gcd, but Corollary 1.11 — the step that converts $\gcd(p,b) = 1$ into $p \mid c$ — is a corollary of Bézout. This is why Aluffi keeps the two words apart: irreducibility is about factorisations, primality is about products, and it takes a theorem to identify them. In rings where Bézout fails, so does this direction.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — which half of the FTA needs which idea)

Theorem 1.22 (p. 15) has two halves: **existence** of a factorisation into irreducibles, and **uniqueness** up to order and sign.

(a) Prove the existence half for integers $n \ge 2$ by well-ordering, using only Definition 1.17. Note explicitly that primality is never invoked.

(b) Now let $S = \{1, 5, 9, 13, 17, \dots\}$ be the positive integers leaving remainder $1$ on division by $4$. Check that $S$ is closed under multiplication, so "divisor" and "irreducible" make sense inside $S$.

(c) Show that $9$, $21$ and $49$ are all irreducible **as elements of $S$**, and deduce that $441$ has two genuinely different factorisations into $S$-irreducibles. Which of the two halves of Theorem 1.22 fails in $S$, and which result from §1.4 is unavailable there?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Multiply $(4j+1)(4k+1)$ and look at the remainder. (c) A factorisation inside $S$ may only use factors that are themselves in $S$ — the usual factors of $9$ and $49$ are not.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Let $T$ be the set of integers $\ge 2$ with no such factorisation and take its least element by well-ordering; split on irreducible or composite. (c) $9 = 3\cdot 3$ and $49 = 7\cdot 7$ and $21 = 3\cdot 7$, and $3, 7 \notin S$ because both leave remainder $3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $T \ne \varnothing$, let $m = \min T$. If $m$ is irreducible it is a one-factor product, so $m \notin T$. Otherwise $m = de$ with $1 < d, e < m$; both are below the minimum, so both factor, and concatenating gives a factorisation of $m$. Contradiction either way, so $T = \varnothing$. Nowhere does this argument ask whether a divisor is prime.

(b) $(4j+1)(4k+1) = 16jk + 4j + 4k + 1 = 4(4jk + j + k) + 1 \in S$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Inside $S$, a divisor of $9$ must be an element of $S$ dividing $9$, and the only candidates in $S$ are $1$ and $9$ — because $3 \notin S$. So $9$ is $S$-irreducible, and the same argument makes $21$ and $49$ $S$-irreducible. But
$$441 = 21 \cdot 21 = 9 \cdot 49,$$
two factorisations into $S$-irreducibles that are not rearrangements of each other.

So **existence survives and uniqueness fails**. Existence needed only well-ordering and Definition 1.17, both of which $S$ still has. Uniqueness needed Theorem 1.21 — irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime — and that is exactly what $S$ lacks: $9$ is $S$-irreducible but not $S$-prime, since $9 \mid 21\cdot 21$ while $9 \nmid 21$. And Theorem 1.21 was proved from Bézout, which $S$ has no version of, since $S$ is not closed under subtraction. The moral is the one stated at the end of §1.4: in $\mathbb{Z}$ the two words agree, and it is a theorem that they do, not a definition.
</details>
