<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) The space of persistence diagrams equipped with the bottleneck distance (or
the $p$-th Wasserstein distance) lacks inner product structure, which poses
challenges inside a machine learning framework. Their parenthetical: a Hilbert
space is a vector space equipped with an inner product which is a complete metric
space with respect to the distance induced by that inner product.
(b) Definition 13.1: for a finite diagram $D = \{(b_i,d_i)\}_{i \in [1,n]}$ from
$\mathbb{D}$, the *persistence landscape* is
$\lambda_D : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with
$$\lambda_D(k,t) := k\text{-th largest value of } [\min\{t - b_i,\ d_i - t\}]_+ \text{ for } i \in [1,n],$$
where $[c]_+ = \max(c,0)$. Each point $(b_i,d_i)$ traces the upper boundary of a
triangle, namely the curve
$\{(t, [\min\{t-b_i, d_i-t\}]_+) \mid t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ — a tent rising from
$(b_i,0)$ to the apex at the midpoint and back down to $(d_i,0)$. There are $n$
such triangles, and $\lambda_D(k,\cdot)$ is the $k$-th upper envelope in their
arrangement, that is, the boundary of the $k$-th layer. For $k > n$ there are
fewer than $k$ triangles covering any $t$, so the $k$-th largest value is $0$ and
$\lambda_D(k,\cdot) \equiv 0$.
(c) $\|\lambda_D\|_p^p = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \|\lambda_D(k,\cdot)\|_p^p$ and
$\Lambda_p(D_1,D_2) = \|\lambda_{D_1} - \lambda_{D_2}\|_p$. Landscapes of finite
diagrams lie in the $L^p$-space $\mathcal{L}^p(\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{R})$,
which is a Hilbert space when $p = 2$.
(d) Claim 13.1: given a diagram $D$ with landscape $\lambda_D$, one can uniquely
recover $D$ from $\lambda_D$ — the map is injective and lossless. Theorem 13.1:
for diagrams $D$ and $D'$, $\Lambda_\infty(D,D') \leq d_B(D,D')$, so the map is
1-Lipschitz with respect to the bottleneck distance. The mean landscape of
$D_1,\ldots,D_\ell$ is
$\overline{\lambda}(k,t) = \frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{D_i}(k,t)$, and
the warning is that a function $\lambda : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$
need not be the image of any valid persistence diagram — in particular the mean
landscape may not be. There is no tension: Claim 13.1 asserts injectivity, so the
map has a left inverse on its image, and says nothing about the image being all
of the function space. Averaging leaves that image.
</details>

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 13.2: for a topological space $X$, a function
$\mathbf{k} : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a *positive semidefinite kernel* if it
is symmetric and for every integer $n > 0$, all $x_1,\ldots,x_n \in X$ and all
$a_1,\ldots,a_n \in \mathbb{R}$ with $\sum_i a_i = 0$ one has
$\sum_{i,j} a_i a_j \mathbf{k}(x_i,x_j) \geq 0$; it is *negative semidefinite* if
under the same conditions $\sum_{i,j} a_i a_j \mathbf{k}(x_i,x_j) \leq 0$. Given a
set $X$ and a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ of real-valued functions on $X$, the
*evaluation functional* at $x$ is the linear functional
$L_x : \mathcal{H} \to \mathbb{R}$, $L_x(f) = f(x)$; $\mathcal{H}$ is a
*reproducing kernel Hilbert space* if $L_x$ is continuous for every $x \in X$.
Given a positive semidefinite kernel $\mathbf{k}$ there is a unique RKHS
$\mathcal{H}_\mathbf{k}$ with
$\mathbf{k}(x,y) = \langle \mathbf{k}(\cdot,x), \mathbf{k}(\cdot,y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_\mathbf{k}}$,
and $\mathbf{k}$ is called its *reproducing kernel*.
(b) Equivalently $k(x,y) = \langle \Phi(x), \Phi(y)\rangle_\mathcal{H}$ for a
feature map $\Phi : X \to \mathcal{H}$, and the induced pseudometric is
$\mathsf{d}_\mathbf{k}^2(x,y) := \mathbf{k}(x,x) + \mathbf{k}(y,y) - 2\mathbf{k}(x,y)$,
equivalently $\mathsf{d}_\mathbf{k}(x,y) = \|x - y\|_\mathcal{H}$ after the
embedding. It is only a pseudometric because $\mathsf{d}(x,y) = 0$ need not imply
$x = y$ — the feature map need not be injective — while all other metric axioms
hold.
(c) Definition 13.3: for $\sigma > 0$ define
$\Phi_\sigma : \mathbb{D} \to \mathcal{L}^2(\Omega)$ by
$$\Phi_\sigma(D)(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\sum_{y \in D}\left[e^{-\|x-y\|^2/4\sigma} - e^{-\|x-\bar y\|^2/4\sigma}\right],$$
where $\bar y = (y_2,y_1)$. The induced *persistence scale space kernel* is
$$k_\sigma(D,E) = \langle \Phi_\sigma(D), \Phi_\sigma(E)\rangle_{\mathcal{L}^2(\Omega)} = \frac{1}{8\pi\sigma}\sum_{y \in D;\, z \in E}\left[e^{-\|y-z\|^2/8\sigma} - e^{-\|y-\bar z\|^2/8\sigma}\right].$$
The reflected term subtracts a mirror-image Gaussian, which makes the feature
function vanish on the diagonal — points of low persistence, which sit near the
diagonal, therefore contribute almost nothing, and adding or removing them barely
moves the feature. By construction, being an inner product of feature vectors,
the PSSK is positive definite.
(d) Theorem 13.2: for $D, E \in \mathbb{D}$,
$\|\Phi_\sigma(D) - \Phi_\sigma(E)\|_{\mathcal{L}^2(\Omega)} \leq \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma}\mathsf{d}_{W,1}(D,E)$.
The stability is against the 1-Wasserstein distance, with Lipschitz constant
$1/(2\pi\sigma)$. Since that constant blows up as $\sigma \to 0$, a sharper scale
parameter buys resolution at the cost of stability: the sharper the Gaussians, the
more violently the feature map can react to a small perturbation of the diagram.
</details>

the persistence $|b - d|$ of the point $u = (b,d)$.

of variance $\sigma^2$ and weight function $\omega$, then for finite bounded
diagrams $D, E$,
$$\|\mathrm{I}_D - \mathrm{I}_E\|_1 \leq \left(\sqrt{5}\,|\nabla\omega| + \sqrt{\tfrac{10}{\pi}}\frac{\|\omega\|_\infty}{\sigma}\right)\mathsf{d}_{W,1}(D,E),$$

$\|\cdot\|_2$ and $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ norms. The constant depends on the weight

scale $\sigma$, again degrading as $\sigma \to 0$.

on $\Omega$ and $\omega : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^+$ strictly positive. The feature
map $\Psi_{k,\omega} : \mathbb{D} \to \mathcal{H}_k$ is
$\Psi_{k,\omega}(D) = \sum_{x \in D}\omega(x)k(\cdot,x)$, inducing the
*persistence weighted kernel*
$$K_{k,\omega}(D,E) = \langle\Psi_{k,\omega}(D),\Psi_{k,\omega}(E)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = \sum_{x\in D;\,y\in E}\omega(x)\omega(y)k(x,y).$$
A kernel $k$ on a locally compact Hausdorff $X$ is a $C_0$-*kernel* if
$k(\cdot,x) \in C_0(X)$ for every $x$, where $C_0(X)$ is the space of continuous
functions vanishing at infinity; it is $C_0$-*universal* if in addition
$\mathcal{H}_k$ is dense in $C_0(X)$. The $d$-dimensional Gaussian kernel
$k_G(x,y) = (1/(2\pi\tau^2))e^{-\|x-y\|^2/2\tau^2}$ is $C_0$-universal on
$\mathbb{R}^d$. What universality buys: the kernel mean embedding of measures is
then injective, so the induced distance
$\|\Psi_{k,\omega}(D) - \Psi_{k,\omega}(E)\|_{\mathcal{H}_k}$ is a proper metric
rather than only a pseudometric.
(d) Treat $D$ as the unweighted measure $\mu_D = \sum_{x\in D}\delta_x$ and set
$k^\omega(x,y) := \omega(x)\omega(y)k(x,y)$, which remains positive semidefinite
for strictly positive $\omega$; with $\mathcal{H}_{k^\omega}$ its RKHS, the map
$\Psi_{k^\omega}(D) := \sum_{x\in D}\omega(x)\omega(\cdot)k(\cdot,x)$ is a valid
feature map, and Eq. (13.5) asserts that the inner product it induces equals the
one of Eq. (13.4): $K_{k^\omega}(D,E) = K_{k,\omega}(D,E)$. Taking
$\omega_{arc}(x) = \arctan(C \cdot pers(x)^p)$ with $pers(x) = |d-b|$, and $k$ the
2D unnormalised Gaussian $k_G(x,y) = e^{-\|x-y\|^2/2\tau^2}$, the weighted kernel
$k_G^{\omega_{arc}}$ is the *persistence weighted Gaussian kernel*. Putting a
Gaussian on top of the RKHS representation instead of a linear kernel gives the
$(k,\omega)$-Gaussian kernel
$K^G_{k,\omega}(D,E) = \exp(-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\Psi_{k,\omega}(D) - \Psi_{k,\omega}(E)\|^2_{\mathcal{H}_k})$.

results for the PWGK-induced distance with respect to both $d_B$ and
$\mathsf{d}_{W,1}$ are shown in the cited work, with bounds depending on $\omega$
and $k_G$, and then write that the precise statements are somewhat involved and
they omit the details. They add one remark worth keeping: stability with respect
to the *bottleneck* distance is provided, which is usually harder to obtain than
Wasserstein stability for such vectorizations.
</details>

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Theorem 13.4: given $X$ and $\phi : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$, the kernel
$\phi$ is negative semidefinite if and only if $e^{-t\phi}$ is positive
semidefinite for all $t > 0$. It converts a distance into a kernel by
exponentiation: if one can show a candidate distance on diagrams is negative
semidefinite, then $e^{-t\phi}$ is a legitimate positive semidefinite kernel and
the whole RKHS apparatus applies. The strategy is useful because it lets one work
directly with a distance designed to respect the geometry of diagrams, instead of
inventing a feature map and hoping the induced distance is meaningful.
(b) Definition 13.6: for a unit vector $\theta \in \mathbb{S}^1$, let $L(\theta)$
be the line $\{\lambda\theta\}$ and $\pi_\theta$ the orthogonal projection onto
it; for diagrams $D, E$ set $\mu_D^\theta := \sum_{p\in D}\delta_{\pi_\theta(p)}$
and $\bar\mu_D^\theta := \sum_{p\in D}\delta_{\pi_\theta \circ \pi_\Delta(p)}$
with $\pi_\Delta$ the projection onto the diagonal, and symmetrically for $E$.
Then
$$\mathsf{d}_{SW}(D,E) := \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^1}\mathcal{W}(\mu_D^\theta + \bar\mu_E^\theta,\ \mu_E^\theta + \bar\mu_D^\theta)\,d\theta,$$
where $\mathcal{W}$ is the 1-Wasserstein distance between measures of Eq. (13.6).
The sums are used because $\mathcal{W}$ is defined only for measures of equal
total mass, and $D$ and $E$ may have different cardinalities; adding the
diagonal projections of the *other* diagram equalises the masses.
(c) Proposition 13.5: $\mathsf{d}_{SW}$ is negative semidefinite on $\mathbb{D}$,
the space of bounded and finite persistence diagrams. With Theorem 13.4 this
gives the positive semidefinite *sliced Wasserstein kernel*
$k_{SW}(D,E) := e^{-\mathsf{d}_{SW}(D,E)/2\sigma^2}$ for $\sigma > 0$.
Theorem 13.6: for $\mathbb{D}_N$ the bounded diagrams of cardinality at most $N$
and $D, E \in \mathbb{D}_N$,
$$\frac{\mathsf{d}_{W,1}(D,E)}{4N(4N-1)+2} \leq \mathsf{d}_{SW}(D,E) \leq 2\sqrt{2}\,\mathsf{d}_{W,1}(D,E).$$
"Strongly equivalent" means each distance bounds the other up to a
multiplicative constant, so they induce the same topology and a statement in one
is a statement in the other up to those constants — this is more than stability,
which is one-sided. The parameter $N$ sits in the lower bound only, and it
degrades quadratically: the larger the diagrams one allows, the weaker the
guarantee that $\mathsf{d}_{SW}$ cannot be much smaller than
$\mathsf{d}_{W,1}$.
(d) Definition 13.7: map $D$ to $\mu_D(x) := \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{u\in D}\phi_{G,\sigma}(x,u)$
with $\phi_{G,\sigma}(x,u) = e^{-\|x-u\|^2/2\sigma}$ and $Z$ the normalising
integral; with $D_\Delta := \{\pi_\Delta(u) \mid u \in D\}$, the *Fisher
information metric* is
$$\mathsf{d}_{FIM}(D,E) := \mathsf{d}_{FIM}(\mu_{D\cup E_\Delta}, \mu_{E \cup D_\Delta}) = \arccos\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\sqrt{\mu_{D\cup E_\Delta}(x)\,\mu_{E\cup D_\Delta}(x)}\,dx\right),$$
and the *persistence Fisher kernel* is $k_{PF}(D,E) := e^{-t\,\mathsf{d}_{FIM}(D,E)}$
for some $t > 0$. Proposition 13.7: $(\mathsf{d}_{FIM} - \tau)$ is negative
definite on $\mathbb{D}$ for any $\tau \geq \pi/2$. Corollary 13.8: $k_{PF}$ is
positive definite on $\mathbb{D}$. Derivation: by Proposition 13.7 and
Theorem 13.4, $e^{-t(\mathsf{d}_{FIM}-\tau)}$ is positive definite for $t > 0$ and
$\tau \geq \pi/2$; and
$k_{PF}(D,E) = e^{-t\,\mathsf{d}_{FIM}(D,E)} = \alpha\, e^{-t(\mathsf{d}_{FIM}(D,E)-\tau)}$
with $\alpha = e^{-t\tau} > 0$ a fixed constant, and scaling a positive definite
kernel by a positive constant leaves it positive definite. The property the SW and
PF kernels have and the PSSK and PWGK lack is *infinite divisibility*, which Dey
and Wang note can bring computational advantages when used in kernel machines.
</details>

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) A classifier is a smooth scalar $f : \mathbf{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ on a
$d$-dimensional hypercube $\mathbf{X}$, predicting by $\mathrm{sign}(f(x))$; the
*classification boundary* is the zero level set
$S_f = f^{-1}(0) = \{x \in \mathbf{X} \mid f(x) = 0\}$. Letting $\mathrm{Dgm}f$ be
the levelset zigzag persistence diagram of $f$, set
$\Pi_{S_f} := \{(b,d) \in \mathrm{Dgm}f \mid b \leq 0,\ d \geq 0\}$ — the features
whose lifetime passes through the zero level set, in one-to-one correspondence
with the topological features of $S_f$. For $c = (b,d) \in \Pi_{S_f}$ the
*robustness* is $\rho(c) = \min\{|b|,|d|\}$, the least $L_\infty$ perturbation of
$f$ needed to push $c$ out of $\Pi_{S_f}$ and so remove the feature from $S_f$.
The topological complexity is $L_{topo}(f) := \sum_{c \in \Pi_{S_f}}\rho^2(c)$.
Robustness needs more than $S_f$ because two components of $S_f$ of similar size
can require very different perturbations to destroy: in Dey and Wang's figure,
pushing down the saddle $q_1$ merges the left component into the large one,
whereas removing the right component requires moving $q_2$ or $p_2$ further. The
size of a feature within $S_f$ says nothing about how deep the surrounding
function must be moved; the persistence pairing does. Combined with any standard
loss $\ell$ and parameterising $f_\omega$ by $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^m$,
$$L(f_\omega, X_n) = \sum_{x_i \in X_n}\ell(f_\omega(x_i), y_i) + \lambda L_{topo}(f_\omega).$$
(b) $\mathcal{T}(\omega) := \mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Dgm}f_\omega) = \mathcal{T}(\{(b_i,d_i)\})$
depends on $\omega$ only through the diagram, so the chain rule needs
$\partial b_i/\partial\omega$ and $\partial d_i/\partial\omega$; the terms
$\partial\mathcal{T}/\partial b_i$ are easy once the analytic form of
$\mathcal{T}$ in the $b_i, d_i$ is given, as it is for $L_{topo}$. Intuitively one
would want to invert the map sending $f_\omega$ to $\mathrm{Dgm}f_\omega$, and
that inverse does not exist in general — many functions share a diagram. What is
used in its place is a map back to *vertices*: for a PL-function on $K$,
Theorem 3.16 says each persistent point is of the form
$(b_i,d_i) = (f(v_{\ell_i}), f(v_{r_i}))$ with $v_{\ell_i}, v_{r_i}$ homological
critical points, giving $\rho : \mathrm{Dgm}f \to V \times V$ with
$\rho(b_i,d_i) = (v_{\ell_i}, v_{r_i})$.
(c) Proposition 13.9: suppose $f_\omega : |K| \to \mathbb{R}$ is a PL-function
with distinct values on all vertices $V$ of $K$, and $K$ is a finite simplicial
complex. Then there is a neighbourhood of $\omega$ in the parameter space on
which $\xi(\omega) = \rho_\omega(\mathrm{Dgm}f_\omega)$ remains constant.
Derivation: $b_i = f_\omega(\rho_\omega(b_i)) = f_\omega(v_{\ell_i})$ always; by
Proposition 13.9 the identity of $v_{\ell_i}$ does not change within a small
enough neighbourhood of $\omega$, so within that neighbourhood $b_i$ is literally
the function $\omega \mapsto f_\omega(v_{\ell_i})$ evaluated at a *fixed* vertex.
Differentiating,
$$\frac{\partial b_i}{\partial\omega} = \frac{\partial f_\omega(\rho_\omega(b_i))}{\partial\omega} = \frac{\partial f_\omega(v_{\ell_i})}{\partial\omega},$$
and $\partial d_i/\partial\omega = \partial f_\omega(v_{r_i})/\partial\omega$
analogously; the chain rule then gives $\partial\mathcal{T}/\partial\omega$. The
hypotheses: $K$ finite and $f_\omega$ PL are what make the vertex map $\rho$
available at all, via Theorem 3.16 and the lower-star filtration; *distinct values
on all vertices* is what makes the pairing locally rigid, since a tie between two
vertex values is exactly where the sorted order — and hence the pairing — can
switch.
(d) Definition 13.8: the $(p,q)$-Wasserstein distance between diagrams $P, Q$ is
$\mathbf{d}_{W,q}^p(P,Q) := \inf_{\Pi : P \to Q}\left[\sum_{x\in P}\|x - \Pi(x)\|_p^q\right]^{1/q}$
over bijections $\Pi$; the $q$-Wasserstein distance of Definition 3.10 is
$\mathbf{d}_{W,q}^\infty$. Definition 13.9: with $D_\emptyset$ the trivial diagram
of infinitely many diagonal copies, $\mathbb{D}_q^p := \{P \mid \mathbf{d}_{W,q}^p(P, D_\emptyset) < \infty\}$.
Definition 13.10: for a probability distribution $\rho$ on
$(\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{D}))$, the *Fréchet function* is
$\mathcal{F}_\rho(X) := \int_\mathcal{D}\mathbf{d}_\mathcal{D}^2(X,Y)\,d\rho(Y)$;
the *Fréchet variance* is $Var_\rho := \inf_{X\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{F}_\rho(X)$
and the *Fréchet expectation* (mean set) is
$\mathbb{E}(\rho) = \{X \mid \mathcal{F}_\rho(X) = Var_\rho\}$. Theorem 13.10: if
$\rho$ is a probability measure on $(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{D}))$ with
finite second moment and compact support, then $\mathbb{E}(\rho) \neq \emptyset$.
What is known about the spaces: $\mathbb{D}_q^\infty$ is Polish — complete and
separable — so probability measures can be defined on it; and $\mathbb{D}_2^2$ is
a nonnegatively curved Alexandrov space, a geodesic space with curvature bounded
below by zero. For a practitioner: optimising a persistence-based loss by gradient
descent is licensed at a parameter $\omega$ where $f_\omega$ is PL on a finite
complex with all vertex values distinct — there the diagram's birth and death
values are honest differentiable functions of $\omega$, being $f_\omega$ evaluated
at fixed vertices — and Proposition 13.9 says nothing at parameters where two
vertex values coincide. Reporting a "mean persistence diagram" is licensed only in
the Fréchet sense: Theorem 13.10 guarantees the mean set is non-empty under finite
second moment and compact support, but the mean is in general *not unique*, so
"the" mean is a choice from a set; an iterative algorithm computing a local
minimum exists for $\mathbb{D}_2^2$, the computational question remains open, and
where uniqueness matters — tracking a mean across time, for instance — the
probabilistic Fréchet mean is the proposed repair. Note also the contrast with
landscapes: the mean landscape of Problem 1 always exists and is easy to compute,
but need not be the landscape of any diagram at all.
