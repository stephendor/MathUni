(a) Write down the Čech complex $\mathbb{C}^r_Z(P)$ and the Vietoris–Rips complex

(b) State Corollary 6.1 and Proposition 6.2, and say which hypothesis each needs.
What does Proposition 6.2 add that Corollary 6.1 does not?
(c) State the interleaving relation between the Čech and Rips filtrations and the
bottleneck consequence Dey and Wang record at Eq. (6.3).
(d) Now suppose the ambient space is dropped and $P$ is treated as a discrete
metric space $(P, \mathsf{d}_P)$. Write the relation (6.5) between
$\mathbb{C}^r_P(P)$ and $\mathbb{C}^r_Z(P)$, explain where the factor 2 comes
from, and say why the two *Rips* complexes are nevertheless identical. Then give
the size of the $d$-skeleton of either complex once $r$ exceeds the diameter of
$P$, and say in one sentence why that number is the subject of this unit.

(a) $\mathbb{C}^r_Z(P) = \{\sigma = \{p_0,\dots,p_k\} \mid \bigcap_{i}B_Z(p_i,r) \neq \emptyset\}$
and $\mathbb{VR}^r(P) = \{\sigma = \{p_0,\dots,p_k\} \mid \mathsf{d}_Z(p_i,p_j) \leq 2r \text{ for all } i,j\}$.
The Čech complex is the nerve of the union of balls $P^r = \bigcup_{p\in P}B_Z(p,r)$.
(b) Corollary 6.1: for fixed $r \geq 0$, if $B_Z(x,r)$ is convex for every
$x \in P$, then $\mathbb{C}^r(P)$ is homotopy equivalent to $P^r$, hence
$\mathsf{H}_k(\mathbb{C}^r(P)) \cong \mathsf{H}_k(P^r)$ for every $k$. It follows
from the Nerve Theorem. Proposition 6.2: if $B(x,r)$ is convex for every $x\in P$
and *all* $r \geq 0$, then the persistence module $\mathsf{H}_k\mathcal{P}$ is
isomorphic to $\mathsf{H}_k\mathcal{C}(P)$, and the diagrams are equal,
$\mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{P} = \mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{C}(P)$. Corollary 6.1 is one
scale at a time; Proposition 6.2 upgrades it to the whole filtration, which is
what a persistence statement needs — and it pays for that with convexity at every
radius rather than one.
(c) The Čech and Rips filtrations are multiplicatively 2-interleaved, so their
persistence modules are $\log 2$-interleaved at the log scale, and
$\mathsf{d}_b(\mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathcal{C}(P), \mathrm{Dgm}_{\log}\mathcal{R}(P)) \leq \log 2$
by Corollary 4.4.
(d) $\mathbb{C}^r_P(P) \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r_Z(P) \subseteq \mathbb{C}^{2r}_P(P)$.
The two Čech complexes differ because the metric balls differ: $B_P(p,r)$
contains only points of $P$, while $B_Z(p,r)$ is a ball of the ambient space, so
a common point of ambient balls need not be a point of $P$. The Rips complexes
coincide because the definition uses only pairwise distances among points of $P$,
and those are the same in both readings. Once $r$ exceeds the diameter of $P$,
every subset of $P$ spans a simplex, so the $d$-skeleton of either complex has
size $\Theta(n^{d+1})$ for $n = |P|$ — a polynomial of degree $d+1$ in the sample
size is precisely what makes the exact filtration unbuildable, and everything
that follows is an attempt to replace it by something of size $O(n)$ with a
stated error.
</details>

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 6.1: for finite point sets $P$ and $Q$ with appropriate metrics,
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Cech}}(P,Q) = \max_k \mathsf{d}_B(\mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{C}(P), \mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{C}(Q))$
and
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Rips}}(P,Q) = \max_k \mathsf{d}_B(\mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{R}(P), \mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{R}(Q))$.
They are pseudodistances because two different point sets can have identical
persistence diagrams in every dimension, making the distance zero without the
sets being equal — the diagrams are a summary, not a complete invariant.
(b) Definition 6.2: for compact $A, B \subseteq (Z,\mathsf{d}_Z)$,
$\mathsf{d}_H(A,B) = \max\{\max_{a\in A}\mathsf{d}_Z(a,B), \max_{b\in B}\mathsf{d}_Z(b,A)\}$
— this needs a common ambient space. Definition 6.3: for metric spaces
$(X,\mathsf{d}_X)$ and $(Y,\mathsf{d}_Y)$, a correspondence is
$C \subseteq X \times Y$ meeting every $x$ and every $y$; its distortion is
$\mathrm{distort}_C = \frac{1}{2}\sup_{(x,y),(x',y') \in C}|\mathsf{d}_X(x,x') - \mathsf{d}_Y(y,y')|$;
and $\mathsf{d}_{GH}(X,Y) = \inf_C \mathrm{distort}_C(X,Y)$. It needs no ambient
space: it measures how far the two are from being isometric.
(c) Theorem 6.3: (a) for finite $P, Q \subseteq (Z,\mathsf{d}_Z)$,
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Cech}}(P,Q) \leq \mathsf{d}_H(P,Q)$ and
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Rips}}(P,Q) \leq \mathsf{d}_H(P,Q)$; (b) for finite metric
spaces $(P,\mathsf{d}_P)$ and $(Q,\mathsf{d}_Q)$,
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Cech}}(P,Q) \leq 2\mathsf{d}_{GH}((P,\mathsf{d}_P),(Q,\mathsf{d}_Q))$
and
$\mathsf{d}_{\mathrm{Rips}}(P,Q) \leq \mathsf{d}_{GH}((P,\mathsf{d}_P),(Q,\mathsf{d}_Q))$.
(d) The extra 2 comes from the difference in metric balls recorded at Eq. (6.5):
without an ambient space the Čech complex must be built with balls internal to
the point set, and $\mathbb{C}^r_Z(P) \subseteq \mathbb{C}^{2r}_P(P)$ is the price.
The Rips bound has no such factor because the Rips complex does not see balls at
all. A metric space $(Z,\mathsf{d}_Z)$ is *totally bounded* if it has a finite
$\varepsilon$-sample for every $\varepsilon > 0$ — where an $\varepsilon$-sample
(Definition 2.17) is a finite $S \subseteq Z$ with $\mathsf{d}_Z(z,S) \leq \varepsilon$
for every $z$ — that is, it can be approximated by a finite metric space at any
resolution. Dey and Wang remark that Theorem 6.3(b) extends to such spaces, which
need not be finite.
</details>

(a) Definition 2.11: for finite $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, a $k$-simplex $\sigma$
is *Delaunay* if its vertices lie in $P$ and there is an open $d$-ball whose
boundary contains its vertices and which is empty of points of $P$; a *Delaunay
complex* $\mathrm{Del}\,P$ is a geometric simplicial complex with vertices in $P$
all of whose simplices are Delaunay and whose underlying space is the convex hull
of $P$. Fact 2.3: every nondegenerate point set — no $d+2$ points cospherical —
admits a unique Delaunay complex. Definition 2.12: the *Voronoi diagram*
$\mathrm{Vor}(P)$ tessellates $\mathbb{R}^d$ into convex cells
$V_p = \{x \mid \mathsf{d}(x,p) \leq \mathsf{d}(x,q)\ \forall q \in P\}$, a
$k$-face being an intersection of $(d-k+1)$ cells. Fact 2.4: $\mathrm{Del}(P)$ is
the nerve of the closed cover $\{V_p\}_{p\in P}$ of $\mathbb{R}^d$ — that is the
sense in which it is a nerve, and it gives the duality sending a Delaunay
$k$-simplex to a Voronoi $(d-k)$-face. Fact 2.5: among all triangulations of $P$,
$\mathrm{Del}\,P$ (a) maximises the minimum angle in $\mathbb{R}^2$; (b) minimises
the largest circumcircle in $\mathbb{R}^2$; (c) minimises the largest min-ball in
all dimensions. Sizes: $\Theta(n)$ and $\Theta(n\log n)$ time in $\mathbb{R}^2$;
$\Theta(n^2)$ and $\Theta(n^2)$ time in $\mathbb{R}^3$; $\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2\rceil})$
and optimal time $\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2\rceil})$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, $d \geq 3$.
(b) The alpha complex consists of all simplices of $\mathrm{Del}(P)$ with a
circumscribing ball of radius at most $\alpha$. As a nerve: put
$D_p^\alpha = \{x \in B(p,\alpha) \mid \mathsf{d}(x,p) \leq \mathsf{d}(x,q)\ \forall q\}$,
the part of the Voronoi cell of $p$ inside the $\alpha$-ball; then
$\mathrm{Del}^\alpha(P)$ is the nerve of $\{D_p^\alpha\}$. Equivalently
$\sigma = \{p_0,\dots,p_k\}$ is in it iff $\bigcup_p B(p,\alpha)$ meets
$V_{p_0}\cap\cdots\cap V_{p_k}$.
(c) Definition 2.13: with witnesses $P$ and landmarks $Q \subseteq P$, a simplex
$\sigma = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ is *weakly witnessed* by $x \in P\setminus Q$ if
$\mathsf{d}(q,x) \leq \mathsf{d}(p,x)$ for every $q \in \sigma$ and every
$p \in Q\setminus\sigma$. Definition 2.14: the *witness complex*
$\mathcal{W}(Q,P)$ is the collection of simplices all of whose faces are weakly
witnessed by a point of $P\setminus Q$. The "every face" clause is needed because
a weakly witnessed simplex need not have all its faces weakly witnessed, so
without it the collection would not be closed under taking faces and would not be
a simplicial complex. Definition 2.15: for finite $Q \subset \mathbb{R}^d$,
$\sigma$ is *strongly witnessed* by $x$ if the same inequality holds and in
addition $\mathsf{d}(q_1,x) = \cdots = \mathsf{d}(q_d,x)$. Proposition 2.3:
$\sigma$ is strongly witnessed if and only if every face of $\sigma$ is weakly
witnessed. Fact 2.6: for finite $Q \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, $\sigma \in \mathrm{Del}\,Q$
if and only if $\sigma$ is strongly witnessed by points of $\mathbb{R}^d$.
Proposition 2.4: for finite $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and $Q \subseteq P$,
$\mathcal{W}(Q,P) \subseteq \mathrm{Del}\,Q$ — so a witness complex of a Euclidean
sample embeds in that Euclidean space. Proposition 2.5: (a)
$\mathcal{W}(Q,\mathbb{R}^d) = \mathrm{Del}|_{\mathbb{R}^d}Q = \mathrm{Del}\,Q$;
(b) $\mathcal{W}(Q,M) = \mathrm{Del}|_M Q$ if $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is a
smooth 1- or 2-manifold; (c) $\mathcal{W}(Q,P) = \mathrm{Del}|_M Q$ when $P$ and
$Q$ are sufficiently dense samples of a 1-manifold $M$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the
result does not extend to other submanifolds.
(d) Definition 2.16: for a finite metric space $(P,\mathsf{d})$, a graph $G(P)$
on $P$, a subset $Q \subseteq P$ and a nearest-point map
$\nu : P \to Q$ with $\nu(p) \in \operatorname{argmin}\mathsf{d}(p,Q)$, the
*graph induced complex* $\mathcal{G}(G(P),Q,\mathsf{d})$ contains the $k$-simplex
$\{q_1,\dots,q_{k+1}\}$ if and only if there is a $(k+1)$-clique
$\{p_1,\dots,p_{k+1}\}$ in $G(P)$ with $q_i \in \nu(p_i)$ for each $i$; it is a
complex because a subset of a clique is a clique. Definition 2.17: $Q \subseteq P$
is a *$\delta$-sample* of $(P,\mathsf{d})$ if every $p \in P$ has some $q\in Q$
with $\mathsf{d}(p,q) \leq \delta$, and *$\delta$-sparse* if
$\mathsf{d}(q,r) \geq \delta$ for all distinct $q,r \in Q$. Theorem 2.6: for a
smooth compact connected surface $M \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, if
$8\varepsilon \leq \delta \leq \frac{2}{27}\rho$, $\alpha \geq 8\varepsilon$, $P$
is an $\varepsilon$-sample of $(M,\mathsf{d}_E)$ and $Q \subseteq P$ is a
$\delta$-sparse $\delta$-sample of $(P,\mathsf{d}_E)$, then a triangulation $T$ of
$M$ exists as a subcomplex of $\mathcal{G}^\alpha(P,Q,\mathsf{d}_E)$ and can be
computed efficiently. Theorem 2.7: for $P$ an $\varepsilon$-sample of an embedded
smooth compact manifold $M$ with reach $\rho$ and $Q$ a $\delta$-sample of
$(P,\mathsf{d}_G)$, if $4\varepsilon \leq \alpha$ and
$\delta \leq \frac{1}{3}\sqrt{3/5}\,\rho$, then
$h_* : \mathsf{H}_1(\mathbb{VR}^\alpha(P)) \to \mathsf{H}_1(\mathcal{G}^\alpha(P,Q,\mathsf{d}_G))$
is an isomorphism, $h$ being the simplicial map induced by the nearest-point map.
Advantage: a GIC is not necessarily a subcomplex of the Delaunay complex, so it
carries a few extra simplices that aid topology inference — and the witness
complex fails to capture the topology of a manifold even for dense input, except
for smooth curves in the plane. Disadvantage: for the same reason a GIC may not
embed in the Euclidean space its vertices live in. On size: §2.3 proves *no*
bound on the number of simplices of either complex. What it offers instead is a
qualitative claim — these complexes are "much sparser" and the GIC "retains the
simplicity of the Rips complex as well as the sparsity of the witness complex" —
together with the empirical comparison of Figure 2.8, where the 2-skeletons of
the Rips complexes on a Klein bottle sample in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and a primary-circle
sample in $\mathbb{R}^{25}$ have 608 200 and 1 329 672 867 simplices. That is
evidence, not a theorem.

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 6.4: for finite $P \subset (\mathbb{R}^d,\mathsf{d})$ and
$\gamma,\gamma' \geq 0$, a subset $Q \subseteq P$ is a *$(\gamma,\gamma')$-net* of
$P$ if it satisfies the covering condition ($Q$ is a $\gamma$-sample: every
$p \in P$ has $\mathsf{d}(p,Q) \leq \gamma$) and the packing condition ($Q$ is
$\gamma'$-sparse: $\mathsf{d}(q,q') \geq \gamma'$ for distinct $q,q' \in Q$); when
$\gamma = \gamma'$ it is a $\gamma$-net. A family $\{N_\gamma\}_\gamma$ is a
*net-tower* if (i) there is a constant $c > 0$ with $N_\gamma$ a
$(\gamma,\gamma/c)$-net for all $\gamma$, and (ii) $N_\gamma \supseteq N_{\gamma'}$
for $\gamma \leq \gamma'$. Farthest point sampling: pick $p_1$ arbitrarily, set
$P_1 = \{p_1\}$, and recursively pick
$p_i \in \operatorname{argmax}_{p \in P\setminus P_{i-1}}\mathsf{d}(p,P_{i-1})$
with $P_i = P_{i-1}\cup\{p_i\}$; the *exit-time* is
$\mathsf{t}_{p_i} = \mathsf{d}(p_i,P_{i-1})$. Then the open net-tower is
$\mathcal{N} = \{N_\gamma\}$, $N_\gamma = \{p \in P \mid \mathsf{t}_p > \gamma\}$,
and the closed net-tower is $\overline{\mathcal{N}} = \{\overline N_\gamma\}$,
$\overline N_\gamma = \{p \in P \mid \mathsf{t}_p \geq \gamma\}$.
(b) For a fixed $0 < \varepsilon < 1$,
$$w_p(\alpha) = \begin{cases} 0 & \mathsf{t}_p/\varepsilon \geq \alpha,\\ \alpha - \mathsf{t}_p/\varepsilon & \mathsf{t}_p/\varepsilon < \alpha < \mathsf{t}_p/(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)),\\ \varepsilon\alpha & \mathsf{t}_p/(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)) \leq \alpha.\end{cases}$$
Claim 6.1: $w_p$ is continuous, 1-Lipschitz and nondecreasing. The net-induced
distance at scale $\alpha$ is
$\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q) := \mathsf{d}(p,q) + w_p(\alpha) + w_q(\alpha)$.
Definition 6.5: the open sparse Rips complex is
$\mathbf{Q}^\alpha = \{\sigma \subseteq N_{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\alpha} \mid \widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q) \leq 2\alpha\ \forall p,q \in \sigma\}$
and the closed one $\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\alpha$ is the same with
$\overline N_{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}$. The cumulative complex is
$\mathbb{S}^\alpha := \bigcup_{\beta\leq\alpha}\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\beta$, and
the $\varepsilon$-sparse Rips filtration is
$\mathbb{S} = \{\mathbb{S}^\alpha \hookrightarrow \mathbb{S}^\beta\}_{\alpha\leq\beta}$.
Only the cumulative complexes form a filtration: for $\alpha < \beta$ neither
$\mathbf{Q}^\alpha \subseteq \mathbf{Q}^\beta$ nor
$\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\alpha \subseteq \overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\beta$ need hold,
because the vertex set shrinks as $\alpha$ grows, while
$\mathbb{S}^\alpha \subseteq \mathbb{S}^\beta$ holds by construction.
(c) Claim 6.2: if $\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q) \leq 2\alpha \leq 2\beta$ then
$\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\beta(p,q) \leq 2\beta$. Proof: $w_p$ and $w_q$ are
1-Lipschitz (Claim 6.1), so
$w_p(\beta) \leq w_p(\alpha) + (\beta-\alpha)$ and likewise for $q$; hence
$\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\beta(p,q) \leq \mathsf{d}(p,q) + w_p(\alpha) + w_q(\alpha) + 2(\beta-\alpha) = \widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q) - 2\alpha + 2\beta \leq 2\beta$.
This is what makes the *relaxed* Rips complexes
$\widehat{\mathbb{VR}}^\alpha(P) := \{\sigma \subseteq P \mid \widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q) \leq 2\alpha\ \forall p,q\in\sigma\}$
a genuine filtration under inclusion. Proposition 6.5: with $C = 1/(1-\varepsilon)$,
$\mathbb{VR}^{\alpha/C} \subseteq \widehat{\mathbb{VR}}^\alpha \subseteq \mathbb{VR}^\alpha$.
The right inclusion holds because the weights are non-negative, so
$\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha \geq \mathsf{d}$; the left holds because the weights
are bounded by $\varepsilon\alpha$ each, so $\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha$ exceeds
$\mathsf{d}$ by at most $2\varepsilon\alpha$. The relaxed complex is therefore
squeezed between two ordinary Rips complexes whose scales differ by the factor
$C$, and that factor is the whole approximation error.
(d) Theorem 6.4: for $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with $|P| = n$ and $d$ constant,
$\mathcal{R}(P)$ the Rips filtration, $\mathbb{S}(P)$ the $\varepsilon$-sparse
Rips filtration of Definition 6.5, and fixed $0 < \varepsilon < 1/3$: (a)
$\mathbb{S}(P)$ and $\mathcal{R}(P)$ are multiplicatively
$1/(1-\varepsilon)$-interleaved at the homology level, so
$\mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathbb{S}(P)$ is a $\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))$-approximation of
$\mathrm{Dgm}_k\mathcal{R}(P)$ at log scale; (b) for fixed $k \geq 0$ the total
number of $k$-simplices ever appearing in $\mathbb{S}(P)$ is
$\Theta((1/\varepsilon)^{kd}n)$. Proof outline for (a): Proposition 6.6 says the
inclusion $\mathbf{Q}^\alpha \hookrightarrow \widehat{\mathbb{VR}}^\alpha$ induces
an isomorphism on homology — proved by showing the projection $\pi_\alpha$ onto
the net is a simplicial retraction and that $i \circ \pi_\alpha$ is contiguous to
the identity. Fact 6.2, applied twice, is what makes $\pi_\alpha$ simplicial
($\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(\pi_\alpha(p),\pi_\alpha(q)) \leq \widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,\pi_\alpha(q)) \leq \widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,q)$),
and Fact 6.1, with $w_p(\alpha) \leq \varepsilon\alpha$, is what bounds
$\widehat{\mathsf{d}}_\alpha(p,\pi_\alpha(p)) \leq 2w_p(\alpha) \leq 2\varepsilon\alpha < 2\alpha$
and so supplies the contiguity. The same argument covers
$\mathbf{Q}^\alpha \hookrightarrow \overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\alpha$. Proposition 6.7
then says the inclusion
$\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^\alpha \hookrightarrow \mathbb{S}^\alpha$ also induces an

(a) Eq. (6.12) is the geometric subsequence of the standard Rips filtration
$\mathbb{VR}^\alpha(P) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{VR}^{\alpha(1+\varepsilon)}(P) \hookrightarrow \cdots \hookrightarrow \mathbb{VR}^{\alpha(1+\varepsilon)^m}(P)$.
For the sparsified version set $P_0 = P$ and let $P_{k+1}$ be an
$(\alpha\varepsilon^2)(1+\varepsilon)^{k-1}$-net of $P_k$, stopping when $P_m$ has
constant size; let $\pi_k : P_k \to P_{k+1}$ send $v$ to its nearest neighbour in
$P_{k+1}$. Being a net makes $\pi_k$ induce a simplicial map
$\mathbb{VR}^{\alpha(1+\varepsilon)^k}(P_k) \to \mathbb{VR}^{\alpha(1+\varepsilon)^{k+1}}(P_{k+1})$,
giving the tower of Eq. (6.13). Claim 6.3: for any fixed $\alpha,\varepsilon \geq 0$
and integer $k \geq 0$, each triangle in the diagram relating the two sequences
through the inclusions $i_k$, $j_k$ and the maps $\widehat\pi_k$, $\pi_k$
commutes at the homology level. Definition 6.6 (weak interleaving of vector space
towers): $\mathbb{U}$ and $\mathbb{V}$ over the index set $\{a \geq a_0\}$ are
*weakly $\varepsilon$-interleaved* if there are linear maps
$\phi_i : \mathrm{U}_{a_0+i\varepsilon} \to \mathrm{V}_{a_0+(i+1)\varepsilon}$ and
$\psi_i : \mathrm{V}_{a_0+i\varepsilon} \to \mathrm{U}_{a_0+(i+1)\varepsilon}$ for
every integer $i \geq 0$ making the resulting diagram commute — the relations hold
only at *discrete* index values, unlike Definition 4.4. Theorem 6.8: for $n$
points $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, the persistence diagram of Eq. (6.12) can be
$3\log(1+\varepsilon)$-approximated by that of Eq. (6.13) at log scale, and the
number of $k$-simplices ever appearing in Eq. (6.13) is
$O((1/\varepsilon)^{O(kd)}n)$. The weak interleaving costs a factor 3 relative to
the strong notion: weakly $\varepsilon$-interleaved modules satisfy
$\mathsf{d}_b \leq 3\varepsilon$ rather than $\varepsilon$.
(b) Definition 6.7: for compact $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ the *distance field* is
$\mathsf{d}_X : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$, $x \mapsto \mathsf{d}(x,X)$, and the
*$\alpha$-offset* is $X^\alpha = \mathsf{d}_X^{-1}((-\infty,\alpha])$.
Definition 6.8: the *local feature size* at $x \in X$ is its distance to the
medial axis, and the *reach* $\rho(X)$ is the minimum local feature size over
$X$. Definition 6.9: with $c(x)$ the centre of the minimal enclosing ball of the
set $\Pi(x)$ of closest points of $x$ in $X$, the *generalized gradient* is
$\nabla_\mathsf{d}(x) = (x - c(x))/\mathsf{d}_X(x)$ for $x \notin X$, and the
critical points are those with $\nabla_\mathsf{d}(x) = 0$. Definition 6.10: with
$C$ the set of critical points, the *weak feature size* is
$\mathrm{wfs}(X) = \min_{x\in X}\inf_{c\in C}\mathsf{d}(x,c)$. Dey and Wang
introduce it because the reach is too fragile outside the smooth setting: the
local feature size can be zero at a nonsmooth point — a polygon vertex, where the
medial axis touches the boundary — and even a smoothly embedded manifold can have
reach zero. The reach is used for smoothly embedded manifolds, the weak feature
size for general compact sets.
(c) Proposition 6.9: if $0 < \alpha < \alpha'$ and $\mathsf{d}_X$ has no critical
value in $[\alpha,\alpha']$, then $X^{\alpha'}$ deformation retracts onto
$X^\alpha$, so $\mathsf{H}_*(X^\alpha) \cong \mathsf{H}_*(X^{\alpha'})$.
Fact 6.3: given $A \to B \to C \to D \to E \to F$ of linear maps between
finite-dimensional vector spaces, if $\mathrm{rank}(A\to F) = \mathrm{rank}(C\to D)$
then that quantity also equals $\mathrm{rank}(B\to E)$; and if
$A \to B \to C \to E \to F$ has $\mathrm{rank}(A\to F) = \dim C$, then
$\mathrm{rank}(B\to E) = \dim C$. Proposition 6.10: if $\mathsf{d}_H(X,P) \leq \varepsilon$
for a smooth manifold $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ of reach $\rho(X)$ and
$3\varepsilon \leq \alpha \leq \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{3/5}\,\rho(X)$, then
$\mathsf{H}_*(P^\alpha) \cong \mathsf{H}_*(X)$. Theorem 6.11: under the same
sampling hypothesis, for $3\varepsilon \leq \alpha \leq \frac{3}{16}\sqrt{3/5}\,\rho(X)$
and $i_* : \mathsf{H}_*(\mathbb{VR}^\alpha) \to \mathsf{H}_*(\mathbb{VR}^{2\alpha})$
the inclusion-induced map,
$\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{im}(i_*)) = \dim(\mathsf{H}_*(\mathbb{C}^\alpha(P))) = \dim(\mathsf{H}_*(X))$.
Eq. (6.15) reads
$\mathsf{H}(X) \leftarrow \mathsf{H}(P^\alpha) \leftarrow \mathsf{H}(\mathbb{C}^\alpha(P)) \leftarrow \mathrm{image}(\mathsf{H}(\mathbb{VR}^\alpha)\to\mathsf{H}(\mathbb{VR}^{2\alpha}))$,
where the first arrow is Proposition 6.10, the second is the Nerve Theorem
(Euclidean balls being convex), and the third is Fact 6.3 applied to the
interleaved chain of Čech and Rips complexes.
(d) Proposition 6.12: for compact $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and finite $P$ with
$\mathsf{d}_H(X,P) < \varepsilon < \frac{1}{4}\mathrm{wfs}(X)$, for all
$\alpha,\alpha' \in (\varepsilon, \mathrm{wfs}(X)-\varepsilon)$ with
$\alpha'-\alpha \geq 2\varepsilon$ and any $\lambda \in (0,\mathrm{wfs}(X))$,
$\mathsf{H}_*(X^\lambda) \cong \mathrm{im}(i_*)$ for
$i_* : \mathsf{H}_*(\mathbb{C}^\alpha(P)) \to \mathsf{H}_*(\mathbb{C}^{\alpha'}(P))$.
Theorem 6.13: under $\mathsf{d}_H(X,P) < \varepsilon < \frac{1}{9}\mathrm{wfs}(X)$,
for $\alpha \in (2\varepsilon, \frac{1}{4}(\mathrm{wfs}(X)-\varepsilon))$ and
$\lambda \in (0,\mathrm{wfs}(X))$, $\mathsf{H}_*(X^\lambda) \cong \mathrm{im}(j_*)$
for $j$ the inclusion $\mathbb{VR}^{\alpha/2}(P) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{VR}^{2\alpha}(P)$.
A pair is needed because for a general compact set there is no $\lambda$ with
$X^\lambda$ homotopy equivalent to $X$ — Dey and Wang cite compact sets for which
$\mathsf{H}_*(X^\lambda) \not\cong \mathsf{H}_*(X)$ for every $\lambda > 0$ — so
one gives up on $X$ and targets an offset $X^\lambda$; and the isomorphism
$\mathsf{H}_*(P^\alpha) \cong \mathsf{H}_*(X)$ that carried the manifold argument
is unavailable, leaving only the image of a map between two scales, which is what
Proposition 6.9 and Fact 6.3 can pin down. For a practitioner with $10^5$ points:
building $\mathcal{R}(P)$ outright is out of the question — the $d$-skeleton is
$\Theta(n^{d+1})$ once the scale passes the diameter — so build the
$\varepsilon$-sparse Rips filtration of Definition 6.5, whose $k$-simplex count is
$\Theta((1/\varepsilon)^{kd}n)$, or the simplicial tower of Eq. (6.13). What must
be assumed for the output to say anything about a hidden $X$ is a sampling
condition: $\mathsf{d}_H(P,X) \leq \varepsilon$ together with a feature-size bound
— reach for a smooth manifold via Theorem 6.11, weak feature size for a compact
set via Theorem 6.13 — and a scale $\alpha$ inside the stated window, which is not
checkable from $P$ alone. The sparsification contributes its own separate error:
$\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))$ at log scale for Definition 6.5 by Theorem 6.4(a), or
$3\log(1+\varepsilon)$ for the tower by Theorem 6.8. Those two errors are of
different kinds and both must be reported.
