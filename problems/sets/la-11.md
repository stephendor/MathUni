## Problem 1 (easy — proof, two easy invariances) [interleaves la-06]

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $U$ is a subspace of $V$.

(a) Prove that if $U\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$, then $U$ is invariant under $T$.

(b) Prove that if $\operatorname{range}T\subseteq U$, then $U$ is invariant under $T$.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 1, p. 138)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In both parts, start from an arbitrary $u\in U$ and chase where $Tu$ must land. The two hypotheses put the constraint in different places — one on where $u$ starts, one on where every output goes.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $u\in U\subseteq\operatorname{null}T$ makes $Tu$ a specific vector, and that vector belongs to $U$ for a reason you should name. (b) Do not use the hypothesis on $u$ at all: $Tu$ is an output of $T$, so it lies in $\operatorname{range}T$, which the hypothesis places inside $U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $Tu=0$, and $0\in U$ because $U$ is a subspace. (b) $Tu\in\operatorname{range}T\subseteq U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Both give $Tu\in U$ for arbitrary $u\in U$, which is Definition 5.2. Say explicitly in (a) that $0\in U$ needs $U$ to be a subspace and not merely a subset — that is the one place the word "subspace" does any work, and a solution that says "$Tu=0$, so $Tu\in U$" has skipped it. In (b), note that the hypothesis $u\in U$ is never used, so the same argument shows every subspace containing $\operatorname{range}T$ is invariant, however large.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — computation, and a field-dependent answer)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is defined by $T(x,y)=(-3y,\,x)$. Find the eigenvalues of $T$, with proof. Then say what changes if the same formula is used to define an operator on $\mathbb{C}^2$.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 7, p. 139)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Write out $T(x,y)=\lambda(x,y)$ as two scalar equations and eliminate one variable. Remember that the definition of an eigenvalue requires a *nonzero* vector, so the case $y=0$ needs its own sentence.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The equations are $-3y=\lambda x$ and $x=\lambda y$. Substituting the second into the first gives a single equation in $y$ and $\lambda$. Before dividing by $y$, dispose of $y=0$: show it forces $x=0$ too, so the vector would be zero and cannot be an eigenvector.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$-3y=\lambda^2y$. If $y=0$ then $x=\lambda\cdot0=0$, so $(x,y)=(0,0)$, excluded. Hence $y\ne0$ and $\lambda^2=-3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the equation $\lambda^2=-3$ has no solution, so $T$ has **no** eigenvalues. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it has two, $\lambda=\pm i\sqrt3$, and you should produce a corresponding eigenvector for each to confirm they really are eigenvalues rather than merely candidates — the algebra above shows only that any eigenvalue must satisfy $\lambda^2=-3$.

State clearly that the operator's formula is identical in the two cases and only the available scalars differ, exactly as in the lesson's prediction gate. Mark the difference from that example: there the eigenvalues were $\pm i$, here $\pm i\sqrt3$, and the geometric reading over $\mathbb{R}$ is a rotation combined with a stretch rather than a pure rotation.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, commuting operators preserve the range) [interleaves la-06]

Suppose $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ are such that $ST=TS$. Prove that $\operatorname{range}S$ is invariant under $T$.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 3, p. 139)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The lesson's guided proof did the same thing for $\operatorname{null}S$. This one differs in a way la-06 warned about: membership of a range is an *existence* claim, so you must produce a witness rather than assume an equation.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take $u\in\operatorname{range}S$ and name a $v$ with $Sv=u$ — that naming is the step the null-space version does not need. Then compute $Tu$ and rearrange with the hypothesis until the result is visibly an output of $S$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$Tu=T(Sv)=(TS)v=(ST)v=S(Tv)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The last expression is $S$ applied to the vector $Tv$, so $Tu\in\operatorname{range}S$, and $\operatorname{range}S$ is invariant under $T$ by Definition 5.2.

Compare the two proofs side by side in a closing sentence, because the contrast is the point. For $\operatorname{null}S$ you *assume* $Su=0$ and compute forwards to $S(Tu)=0$. For $\operatorname{range}S$ you *introduce* a preimage $v$ and compute until a preimage of $Tu$ appears. Same hypothesis, same three-line computation, opposite direction of travel — which is exactly the asymmetry la-06 identified between null spaces and ranges.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — computation, the eigenvalues of a projection) [interleaves la-02]

Suppose $V=U\oplus W$, where $U$ and $W$ are nonzero subspaces of $V$. Define $P\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ by $P(u+w)=u$ for $u\in U$ and $w\in W$. Find all eigenvalues and all eigenvectors of $P$, with proof.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 14, p. 140)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Before hunting, check that $P$ is well defined at all: why does every $v\in V$ have exactly one expression $u+w$? Then try the two most obvious kinds of vector — those in $U$ and those in $W$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Directness of the sum gives each $v$ a unique decomposition, which is what makes the formula a function. For $u\in U$ nonzero, $Pu=u$, so $1$ is an eigenvalue; for $w\in W$ nonzero, $Pw=0$, so $0$ is an eigenvalue — and both $U$ and $W$ being nonzero is what supplies the nonzero vectors. To show there are no others, take a general $v=u+w$ with $Pv=\lambda v$ and compare the $U$- and $W$-parts.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$Pv=\lambda v$ reads $u=\lambda u+\lambda w$. Since $u\in U$ and $\lambda w\in W$ and the sum is direct, matching parts gives $u=\lambda u$ and $0=\lambda w$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
From $0=\lambda w$: either $\lambda=0$ or $w=0$. If $\lambda=0$ then $u=0$, so $v=w$ and $v$ is a nonzero element of $W$. If $w=0$ then $v=u\ne0$ and $u=\lambda u$ forces $\lambda=1$. So the eigenvalues are exactly $0$ and $1$; the eigenvectors for $1$ are the nonzero elements of $U$, and those for $0$ are the nonzero elements of $W$.

Two things for the write-up. The "matching parts" step is uniqueness of decomposition in a direct sum and should be cited as such rather than treated as obvious. And note that this operator is as well behaved as an operator gets — $V$ splits into two invariant subspaces on which $P$ is multiplication by a scalar — which is precisely the situation la-12 will call diagonalisable.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — proof, a sum of eigenvectors)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $u,v$ are eigenvectors of $T$ such that $u+v$ is also an eigenvector of $T$. Prove that $u$ and $v$ are eigenvectors of $T$ corresponding to the same eigenvalue.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 25, p. 141)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Give the three eigenvalues names — say $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ — write out what each hypothesis says, and combine them into a single equation in $u$ and $v$. Then ask what would follow if $\alpha\ne\beta$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
From $Tu=\alpha u$, $Tv=\beta v$ and $T(u+v)=\gamma(u+v)$, expand the third using linearity and subtract. You will reach $(\alpha-\gamma)u+(\beta-\gamma)v=0$. Now argue by contradiction: if $\alpha\ne\beta$ then $u$ and $v$ are eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues, and Theorem 5.10 makes them independent — which forces both coefficients to vanish.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\alpha u+\beta v=T(u+v)=\gamma u+\gamma v$, so $(\alpha-\gamma)u+(\beta-\gamma)v=0$. If $\alpha\ne\beta$, independence gives $\alpha=\gamma$ and $\beta=\gamma$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
But $\alpha=\gamma$ and $\beta=\gamma$ give $\alpha=\beta$, contradicting $\alpha\ne\beta$. Hence $\alpha=\beta$ and $u,v$ correspond to the same eigenvalue.

Two remarks belong in the answer. Note that the hypothesis "$u+v$ is an eigenvector" silently includes $u+v\ne0$, since eigenvectors are nonzero by Definition 5.5 — without that the statement is false, as $v=-u$ would show. And note where Theorem 5.10 is used: only to rule out $\alpha\ne\beta$, and nowhere else. Once $\alpha=\beta$ is known, the conclusion needs no further argument.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof, eigenvalues are bounded by the rank)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\dim\operatorname{range}T=k$. Prove that $T$ has at most $k+1$ distinct eigenvalues.

*(Axler §5.A, Exercise 29, p. 141)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Theorem 5.13 bounds the number of eigenvalues by $\dim V$, which may be far larger than $k$. To do better, notice that an eigenvector for a *nonzero* eigenvalue cannot be just anywhere — it has to lie in the range.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Split the eigenvalues into the nonzero ones and the possible zero one. For $\lambda\ne0$ with $Tv=\lambda v$, rewrite $v$ as $T$ applied to something, so that $v\in\operatorname{range}T$. The eigenvectors for distinct nonzero eigenvalues are then an independent list *inside* $\operatorname{range}T$, and la-03's squeeze bounds its length by $k$. Add one for the eigenvalue $0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $Tv=\lambda v$ with $\lambda\ne0$ then $v=T(v/\lambda)$, so $v\in\operatorname{range}T$. Let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r$ be the distinct nonzero eigenvalues with eigenvectors $v_1,\dots,v_r$; these are independent by Theorem 5.10 and all lie in $\operatorname{range}T$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
A basis of $\operatorname{range}T$ is a spanning list of that space with length $k$, and $v_1,\dots,v_r$ is an independent list inside it, so la-03's Theorem 2.23 gives $r\le k$ directly. At most one further eigenvalue is possible, namely $0$, giving at most $k+1$ in total.

Three points earn the marks. The division by $\lambda$ is legal only because the case $\lambda=0$ was separated out first — say so. The bound is attained for every $k$, so it cannot be improved: the operator $\operatorname{diag}(0,1,2,\dots,k)$ on $\mathbb{F}^{k+1}$ has $k$ nonzero diagonal entries and so rank $k$, and its $k+1$ distinct eigenvalues $0,1,\dots,k$ meet the bound exactly. A projection will not serve here: it has only the eigenvalues $0$ and $1$, so it attains $k+1$ only when $k=1$. And note that the result is sharper than Theorem 5.13 whenever $T$ is not injective, since then $k<\dim V$.
</details>
