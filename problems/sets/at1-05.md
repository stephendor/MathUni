## Problem 1 (easy — the algebra first)

Van Kampen's theorem is a statement about free products, so the free product
has to be solid before the topology can be.

(a) In $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ with generators $a$ and $b$, reduce the product
$(b^4a^5b^2a^{-3})(a^4b^{-1}ab^3)$, showing each cancellation. Then write down
the inverse of $ab^2a^{-3}b^{-4}$.
(b) State the universal property of the free product: given homomorphisms
$\varphi_\alpha : G_\alpha \to H$, there is a unique homomorphism
$\varphi : *_\alpha G_\alpha \to H$ restricting to each $\varphi_\alpha$. Prove
it, and say exactly which step needs the fact that reduction of an unreduced
word does not change its image under $\varphi$.
(c) The inclusions $G \hookrightarrow G \times H$ and $H \hookrightarrow G \times H$
induce a homomorphism $G * H \to G \times H$ by (b). Show it is surjective.
Show it is *not* injective when $G$ and $H$ are both non-trivial, by exhibiting
a non-trivial element of the kernel.
(d) Let $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}_2 * \mathbb{Z}_2 \to \mathbb{Z}_2$ send each word
to its length mod 2. Show $\varphi$ is a well-defined surjective homomorphism
and that its kernel is infinite cyclic, generated by $ab$.

*(Hatcher §1.2, pp. 40–42: the $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ computations on p. 40,
the definition of reduced words and the free product on p. 41, the
infinite dihedral group $\mathbb{Z}_2 * \mathbb{Z}_2$ and the universal property
on p. 42)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The two inner letters $a^{-3}$ and $a^4$ are in the same $\mathbb{Z}$, so
they combine before anything else can happen. Watch what that exposes.
(d) "Length" means the length of the *reduced* word; check first that this is
well defined, i.e. that reduction changes length by an even number.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), the formula for $\varphi$ is forced: on $g_1 \cdots g_n$ it must be
$\varphi_{\alpha_1}(g_1) \cdots \varphi_{\alpha_n}(g_n)$. That gives uniqueness
immediately. Existence is the work, and the work is well-definedness.
For (c), think about what $G * H$ knows that $G \times H$ has forgotten — the
free product is deliberately the *non*-commuting version of the direct sum.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(b^4a^5b^2a^{-3})(a^4b^{-1}ab^3)$: the junction letters are $a^{-3}$ and
$a^4$, both in $\langle a \rangle$, so they combine to $a$. The word is now
$b^4a^5b^2 \cdot a \cdot b^{-1}ab^3$, which is already reduced —
$b^4a^5b^2ab^{-1}ab^3$ — since no two adjacent letters lie in the same factor
and none is trivial. Hatcher gives exactly this product on p. 40.
(c) Surjectivity: $(g, h)$ is the image of the two-letter word $gh$.
⟨your step: find the kernel element. Ask what relation holds in $G \times H$
between the images of $g$ and $h$ that does *not* hold in $G * H$.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) *Uniqueness.* Suppose $\varphi$ restricts to $\varphi_\alpha$ on each
$G_\alpha$. Every element of $*_\alpha G_\alpha$ is a reduced word
$g_1 \cdots g_n$, which is the product of the one-letter words $g_i$, and each
one-letter word lies in the subgroup $G_{\alpha_i}$. Since $\varphi$ is a
homomorphism, $\varphi(g_1 \cdots g_n) = \varphi(g_1)\cdots\varphi(g_n)
= \varphi_{\alpha_1}(g_1)\cdots\varphi_{\alpha_n}(g_n)$. So $\varphi$ is
determined on every element, hence unique.

*Existence.* Define $\varphi$ by that formula, applied to any — not necessarily
reduced — expression $g_1 \cdots g_n$. ⟨your step: check this is well defined.
There are exactly two reduction operations, combining two adjacent letters of
the same $G_\alpha$ and deleting an identity letter; check that neither changes
the value of the formula, using that each $\varphi_\alpha$ is a homomorphism.
Then check the result is a homomorphism, which is now immediate from
juxtaposition.⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy/medium — wedges and graphs)

(a) State Theorem 1.20 precisely from memory, then check your statement against
the lesson. Mark which hypothesis is used for surjectivity and which for the
kernel.
(b) Example 1.21 computes $\pi_1(\bigvee_\alpha X_\alpha)$ as
$*_\alpha \pi_1(X_\alpha)$, but only under a hypothesis on the basepoints.
State that hypothesis. Then explain why $\pi_1(\bigvee_\alpha S^1_\alpha)$ is
free, quoting Theorem 1.7 for the factor groups.
(c) Example 1.25's shrinking wedge of circles $X \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ — the
circles of radius $1/n$ centred at $(1/n, 0)$ — has an *uncountable*
fundamental group, so it is certainly not the wedge sum of countably many
circles. Identify precisely which hypothesis of Example 1.21 the shrinking
wedge fails at its basepoint, the origin.
(d) Example 1.22 computes $\pi_1$ of the graph of the twelve edges of a cube as
free of rank 5, using a maximal tree with seven edges. Verify $5 = E - V + 1$
for this graph, and then prove that formula for any finite connected graph $X$
with $V$ vertices and $E$ edges, by counting the edges of a maximal tree.

*(Hatcher §1.2: Theorem 1.20 and Example 1.21, p. 43; Example 1.22, pp. 43–44;
Example 1.25, p. 49. Theorem 1.7 is at1-04's, p. 29.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) Look at the phrase "if each $x_\alpha$ is a deformation retract of an open
neighborhood $U_\alpha$ in $X_\alpha$" and ask what an open neighbourhood of
the origin in the shrinking wedge must contain.
(d) A tree with $V$ vertices has $V - 1$ edges. Prove that by induction on $V$
if you have not seen it.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) and (c) are the same question asked twice, once where the answer is yes and
once where it is no. Example 1.21 is not a theorem about wedge sums; it is a
theorem about wedge sums *whose basepoints are nice*, and the whole content of
Example 1.25 is that this is a real restriction rather than a technicality.
(d) The maximal tree $T$ contains every vertex, so it has $V - 1$ edges; the
generators are indexed by $E - (V-1)$ edges outside it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) In the shrinking wedge, every open neighbourhood of the origin contains all
but finitely many of the circles $C_n$ *entirely* — this is exactly the fact
Hatcher uses on p. 49 to prove his infinite loop is continuous at time 1. So
the origin has no neighbourhood deformation retracting to it inside any single
$C_n$, and the sets $A_\alpha$ of Example 1.21 cannot be built. ⟨your step: say
what goes wrong if you try to build them anyway.⟩
(d) Cube: $V = 8$, $E = 12$, so $E - V + 1 = 12 - 8 + 1 = 5$, matching the five
edges outside the seven-edge tree. Note $7 = V - 1$, as a maximal tree must be.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) *The hypothesis.* Example 1.21 assumes each basepoint $x_\alpha$ is a
deformation retract of some open neighbourhood $U_\alpha \subset X_\alpha$. It
is used to build the open cover: $A_\alpha = X_\alpha \vee \bigvee_{\beta \neq \alpha} U_\beta$
is open, deformation retracts onto $X_\alpha$, and any two or more of the
$A_\alpha$ intersect in $\bigvee_\alpha U_\alpha$, which deformation retracts to
the point. Without a neighbourhood to expand into, there is no open cover to
feed the theorem.

Now the intersections are contractible, so all the $\pi_1(A_\alpha \cap A_\beta)$
vanish, $N$ is trivial, and Theorem 1.20 gives
$\pi_1(\bigvee_\alpha X_\alpha) \cong *_\alpha \pi_1(X_\alpha)$.
⟨your step: specialise to circles and quote Theorem 1.7.⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the hypotheses are not decoration)

Hatcher gives one counterexample for each hypothesis of Theorem 1.20. Work both.

(a) Cover $S^1$ by two open arcs $A$ and $B$, each a proper arc, with
$A \cup B = S^1$. Show that $\pi_1(A) = \pi_1(B) = 0$, so that
$\pi_1(A) * \pi_1(B) = 0$, while $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ by Theorem 1.7.
Conclude that $\Phi$ is not surjective, and identify which hypothesis of
Theorem 1.20 fails.
(b) Let $X$ be the suspension of the three-point space $\{a, b, c\}$, and let
$A_\alpha$, $A_\beta$, $A_\gamma$ be the complements in $X$ of the three points
$a$, $b$, $c$. Draw $X$. Show that each $A_\alpha$ is homotopy equivalent to a
circle, that each twofold intersection is contractible, and that the threefold
intersection $A_\alpha \cap A_\beta \cap A_\gamma$ is *not* path-connected.
(c) Applying Theorem 1.20 to the cover $\{A_\alpha, A_\beta\}$ gives
$\pi_1(X) \cong \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$. Show that applying it illegally to
$\{A_\alpha, A_\beta, A_\gamma\}$ would give $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$,
and prove these two groups are non-isomorphic by comparing abelianisations.
(d) Explain, in one sentence each, what goes wrong in the *proof* in each case:
which step of the argument sketched in the lesson consumes the
path-connectedness of $A_\alpha \cap A_\beta$, and which consumes the
path-connectedness of $A_\alpha \cap A_\beta \cap A_\gamma$.

*(Hatcher §1.2, p. 44 for both counterexamples; the proof of Theorem 1.20,
pp. 44–46; Theorem 1.7, p. 29)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) The suspension of three points is two cone points joined by three arcs. The
three points $a$, $b$, $c$ are the midpoints of those arcs, so deleting one
deletes the interior of one arc.
(d) The first hypothesis is what lets you choose the connecting paths $g_i$ in
Lemma 1.15; the second is what lets you choose the paths $g_v$ at the vertices
of the subdivided square in the kernel argument.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the abelianisation of a free group of rank $r$ is $\mathbb{Z}^r$, and
the rank of a free abelian group is well defined — Hatcher makes this point on
p. 42 precisely so that it can be used as an invariant. This is the same trick
as Corollary 1.27, met again in Problem 4.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Two proper open arcs are each homeomorphic to an interval, hence
contractible, so $\pi_1(A) * \pi_1(B)$ is trivial while $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$;
no homomorphism from the trivial group is onto $\mathbb{Z}$. The failing
hypothesis is path-connectedness of $A \cap B$: two proper arcs covering the
circle must overlap in *two* disjoint arcs. ⟨your step: say why the overlap
cannot be a single arc.⟩
(b) $X$ is the theta-shaped graph: two vertices (the suspension points) joined
by three arcs. $A_\alpha = X - \{a\}$ deformation retracts onto the union of
the other two arcs, a circle. $A_\alpha \cap A_\beta = X - \{a, b\}$ retracts
onto the remaining whole arc, which is contractible.
$A_\alpha \cap A_\beta \cap A_\gamma = X - \{a,b,c\}$ has each arc cut, leaving
two components, one around each suspension point.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Suppose $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$.
Abelianisation is a functor, and isomorphic groups have isomorphic
abelianisations. The abelianisation of a free product is the direct sum of the
abelianisations, so the two sides abelianise to $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and
$\mathbb{Z}^3$. ⟨your step: finish, quoting the well-definedness of the rank of
a free abelian group. Then state the moral: the illegal application of
Theorem 1.20 does not merely fail to be justified, it produces a false answer.⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium/hard — surfaces, from cells to groups)

Proposition 1.26(a) says that attaching 2-cells $e^2_\alpha$ to a
path-connected $X$ along loops $\varphi_\alpha$ kills exactly the normal
subgroup $N$ generated by the classes $[\gamma_\alpha \varphi_\alpha \bar\gamma_\alpha]$,
so $\pi_1(Y) \cong \pi_1(X)/N$.

(a) The closed orientable surface $M_g$ has a cell structure with one 0-cell,
$2g$ 1-cells and one 2-cell attached along
$[a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g]$ — this is the structure you built in at1-02. Derive
$\pi_1(M_g) \cong \langle a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g \mid [a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g] \rangle$
from Proposition 1.26(a), saying where the 1-skeleton computation of Problem
2(b) enters.
(b) Prove Corollary 1.27: $M_g$ is not homotopy equivalent to $M_h$ for
$g \neq h$. Say why the corollary is stated for homotopy equivalence rather
than homeomorphism, and what that buys.
(c) The non-orientable surface $N_g$ is the wedge of $g$ circles with a 2-cell
attached along $a_1^2 \cdots a_g^2$. Compute $\pi_1(N_g)$ and its
abelianisation. Deduce that $N_g \not\simeq N_h$ for $g \neq h$, and that no
$N_g$ is homotopy equivalent to any $M_h$.
(d) Identify $N_1$ and $N_2$ as spaces you already know, and give $\pi_1$ of
each explicitly as a named group.
(e) Proposition 1.26(b) says attaching cells of dimension $n > 2$ changes
nothing. Use it, with Example 1.21, to compute $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^2)$, and then
$\pi_1(\mathbb{R}^3 - A)$ for $A$ an unknotted circle, given Example 1.23's
deformation retraction of $\mathbb{R}^3 - A$ onto $S^1 \vee S^2$.

*(Hatcher §1.2: Proposition 1.26 and its proof, pp. 49–51; the $M_g$
computation and Corollary 1.27, p. 51; the $N_g$ computation, pp. 51–52;
Example 1.23, p. 46)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) In the abelianisation, the relation becomes $2(a_1 + \cdots + a_g) = 0$.
Rechoose the basis as $a_1, \dots, a_{g-1}, a_1 + \cdots + a_g$.
(e) $S^2$ is a 0-cell with a 2-cell attached — so it is *not* covered by (b).
Use Example 1.21 on the wedge and Proposition 1.14 for $\pi_1(S^2)$, or
attach the 2-cell of $S^2$ to a point and note the resulting $N$ is trivial.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Every part of this problem is the same two-step: compute a presentation, then
abelianise to get an invariant you can actually compare. Presentations are hard
to tell apart; finitely generated abelian groups are classified. That gap is
the reason Corollary 1.27's proof is three lines.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) $\pi_1(N_g) \cong \langle a_1, \dots, a_g \mid a_1^2\cdots a_g^2 \rangle$ by
Proposition 1.26(a). Its abelianisation is $\mathbb{Z}^g$ modulo the single
relation $2(a_1 + \cdots + a_g) = 0$. Setting $s = a_1 + \cdots + a_g$ and
taking $a_1, \dots, a_{g-1}, s$ as a new basis, this is
$\mathbb{Z}^{g-1} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$. ⟨your step: read off both conclusions.
For the second, note that the abelianisations of the $\pi_1(M_h)$ are free
abelian, and say what distinguishes them.⟩
(d) $N_1 = \mathbb{R}P^2$, with $\pi_1 = \langle a \mid a^2 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
$N_2$ is the Klein bottle; Hatcher notes on p. 51 that its more usual
presentation comes from the square with sides identified by $aba^{-1}b$, and
that cutting along a diagonal converts one to the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Suppose $M_g \simeq M_h$. Homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms on
$\pi_1$, so $\pi_1(M_g) \cong \pi_1(M_h)$, hence their abelianisations are
isomorphic. Abelianising the presentation in (a) kills every commutator, so the
single relator $[a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g]$ becomes trivial and the abelianisation
is free abelian of rank $2g$. ⟨your step: conclude $g = h$, and then answer the
second question — what is the relationship between "homeomorphic" and
"homotopy equivalent", and which is the stronger conclusion to have ruled out?⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — every group is a fundamental group, and what that costs)

(a) Prove Corollary 1.28: for every group $G$ there is a 2-dimensional cell
complex $X_G$ with $\pi_1(X_G) \cong G$. State clearly the fact about
presentations that the proof consumes, and prove that fact too.
(b) Describe $X_G$ for $G = \mathbb{Z}_n$, and say why $X_G$ is a surface when
$n = 2$ but not when $n > 2$.
(c) Example 1.24 computes $\pi_1$ of the complement of the torus knot
$K_{m,n}$: the complement deformation retracts onto a 2-complex $X_{m,n}$, the
union of the mapping cylinders $X_m$ of $z \mapsto z^m$ and $X_n$ of
$z \mapsto z^n$ glued along the circle $X_m \cap X_n$. Given that all three of
$\pi_1(X_m)$, $\pi_1(X_n)$ and $\pi_1(X_m \cap X_n)$ are $\mathbb{Z}$, and that
a generator of the intersection maps to $m$ times a generator in $X_m$ and $n$
times a generator in $X_n$, apply Theorem 1.20 to obtain
$G_{m,n} = \langle a, b \mid a^m = b^n \rangle$.
(d) Show that the cyclic subgroup $C$ generated by $a^m = b^n$ is central in
$G_{m,n}$, and that $G_{m,n}/C \cong \mathbb{Z}_m * \mathbb{Z}_n$. Taking from
Hatcher's Exercise 1 the fact that a free product of non-trivial groups has
trivial centre, deduce that $C$ is *exactly* the centre.
(e) Now the reflection. Corollary 1.28 says every group whatsoever is the
$\pi_1$ of a 2-complex, while Problem 2(d) says the $\pi_1$ of a graph is always
free. Explain what this pair of facts tells you about how much of a space's
shape $\pi_1$ can be expected to see, and why a *computable* invariant might
need to be coarser than $\pi_1$. Connect this to why the rest of this module
turns to homology.

*(Hatcher §1.2: Corollary 1.28 and its proof, p. 52; Example 1.29, p. 52;
Example 1.24, pp. 47–48. Part (e) is a reflection, not a citation.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The fact is that every group has a presentation. Build it: every group is a
quotient of a free group, namely the free group on the underlying set of $G$.
(d) To show $C$ is central it is enough to show $a^m$ commutes with the
generators $a$ and $b$, which is immediate; the content is the converse
inclusion.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) is a nice piece of pure group theory that runs entirely on one idea: if
$C$ is central then it is normal, so $G_{m,n}/C$ makes sense; killing $a^m$ and
$b^n$ separately leaves $\langle a \mid a^m\rangle * \langle b \mid b^n\rangle$.
Then an element outside $C$ would give a non-trivial central element downstairs,
which Exercise 1 forbids.
(e) There is no trick here and no right answer to memorise. Say what an
invariant is *for*, and then ask whether an invariant that takes every possible
value is doing that job.
</details>
