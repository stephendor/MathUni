## Problem 1 (easy — testing the definition)

Suppose $b,c\in\mathbb{R}$. Define $T:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2$ by
$$T(x,y,z) = (2x-4y+3z+b,\ 6x+cxyz).$$
Show that $T$ is linear if and only if $b=c=0$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 1, p. 57)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction is a routine verification. For the other, you must show that $b\ne0$ breaks linearity and, separately, that $c\ne0$ does — two different offending terms, and they are not broken by the same test.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For $b\ne0$, Theorem 3.11 settles it in one line: compute $T(0,0,0)$. For $c\ne0$ that test is useless, since $0\cdot0\cdot0=0$ — so reach for homogeneity instead and see how the term $cxyz$ scales when $(x,y,z)$ is multiplied by $\lambda$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$T(0,0,0)=(b,0)$, so $b\ne0$ contradicts Theorem 3.11. For the other: $T(\lambda(x,y,z))$ has second slot $6\lambda x + c\lambda^3xyz$, while $\lambda T(x,y,z)$ has second slot $6\lambda x + c\lambda xyz$. Pick a concrete point where $xyz\ne0$ and a concrete $\lambda\ne0,1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
With $(x,y,z)=(1,1,1)$ and $\lambda=2$: the second slots are $12+8c$ and $12+2c$, equal only when $6c=0$, i.e. $c=0$. Conversely, if $b=c=0$ then $T(x,y,z)=(2x-4y+3z,6x)$, and both clauses of Definition 3.2 follow by direct computation — do write that computation out rather than calling it obvious, since the "if" direction carries half the marks. Note that the two failures are detected by two different clauses: the constant $b$ is caught by additivity (via Theorem 3.11) and the cubic $cxyz$ by homogeneity.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, maps on a line) [interleaves la-04]

Show that every linear map from a 1-dimensional vector space to itself is multiplication by some scalar. Precisely: prove that if $\dim V = 1$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,V)$, then there exists $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$ such that $Tv=\lambda v$ for all $v\in V$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 7, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\dim V=1$ means $V$ has a basis consisting of a single vector. Where can that vector's image possibly be?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Let $u$ be a basis of $V$, so $u \ne 0$ and $V=\operatorname{span}(u)$. Then $Tu\in V$, so $Tu=\lambda u$ for some scalar $\lambda$ — that scalar is your candidate, and it is produced by the *codomain* being one-dimensional, not by any property of $T$. Then check the claimed identity at a general $v$, which you may write as $cu$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For $v\in V$ write $v=cu$. Then $Tv=T(cu)=c(Tu)$ by homogeneity $=c(\lambda u)=\lambda(cu)=\lambda v$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
That completes the proof, and the write-up should make two things explicit. First, $\lambda$ is chosen once, before $v$ is quantified — a proof that produces a different $\lambda$ for each $v$ has proved nothing. Second, the only property of $T$ used is homogeneity; additivity is never needed. It is worth asking yourself why the argument collapses when $\dim V=2$: $Tu_1$ is then no longer forced to be a multiple of $u_1$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — additivity alone is not enough)

Give an example of a function $\varphi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ such that
$$\varphi(w+z)=\varphi(w)+\varphi(z)$$
for all $w,z\in\mathbb{C}$, but $\varphi$ is **not** linear. Here $\mathbb{C}$ is regarded as a complex vector space, so the scalars are all of $\mathbb{C}$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 9, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The lesson's prediction gate showed homogeneity without additivity. This is the mirror image, and the phrase "regarded as a complex vector space" is the whole hint: you need a function that respects addition but mishandles multiplication by $i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Complex conjugation, $\varphi(z)=\bar z$, is the standard witness. Additivity is the familiar identity $\overline{w+z}=\bar w+\bar z$. For the failure, test homogeneity at the single scalar $\lambda=i$ and a single convenient $z$ — you do not need to analyse all $\lambda$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\varphi(i\cdot1)=\varphi(i)=\overline{i}=-i$, whereas $i\varphi(1)=i\cdot\overline{1}=i$. Since $-i\ne i$, homogeneity fails.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So $\varphi$ is additive but not linear. Two points to state explicitly. First, why the field matters: over $\mathbb{R}$ the same function *is* linear, because $\overline{av}=a\bar v$ for real $a$, so the counterexample depends entirely on $\mathbb{C}$ being the scalar field — the same sensitivity la-04 met when $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=2$ but $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}=1$. Second, together with the lesson's cube-root example this establishes that Definition 3.2's two clauses are logically independent, so neither can be dropped or derived from the other. **Any correct example, fully verified, earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, every map on $\mathbb{F}^n$ is an array) [interleaves la-04]

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}^n,\mathbb{F}^m)$. Show that there exist scalars $A_{j,k}\in\mathbb{F}$, for $j=1,\dots,m$ and $k=1,\dots,n$, such that
$$T(x_1,\dots,x_n) = (A_{1,1}x_1+\dots+A_{1,n}x_n,\ \dots,\ A_{m,1}x_1+\dots+A_{m,n}x_n)$$
for every $(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in\mathbb{F}^n$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 3, p. 57)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
By Theorem 3.5 the map $T$ is determined by its values on a basis. Use the standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ of $\mathbb{F}^n$ and give a name to the coordinates of each $Te_k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Define $A_{j,k}$ to be the $j$-th coordinate of $Te_k$ — that is, $Te_k=(A_{1,k},\dots,A_{m,k})$. This *defines* the array; nothing is being solved for. Then expand an arbitrary $x$ along the standard basis and push $T$ through the expansion using both clauses of Definition 3.2.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(x_1,\dots,x_n)=x_1e_1+\dots+x_ne_n$, so $T(x_1,\dots,x_n)=x_1Te_1+\dots+x_nTe_n$. Now read off the $j$-th coordinate of the right-hand side.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The $j$-th coordinate is $x_1A_{j,1}+\dots+x_nA_{j,n}$, which is the claimed expression. Say explicitly which axiom does which job: homogeneity converts $T(x_ke_k)$ into $x_kTe_k$, and additivity assembles the $n$ pieces. Note also the direction of the indices — $A_{j,k}$ has its *column* index $k$ naming the basis vector and its *row* index $j$ naming the output slot, so the $k$-th column of the array is $Te_k$. That convention is not arbitrary bookkeeping; la-07 builds the whole matrix formalism on it, and getting it backwards makes matrix multiplication come out transposed.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — proof, a map that is not linear)

Suppose $U$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U\ne V$. Suppose $S\in\mathcal{L}(U,W)$ and $S\ne0$ — meaning $Su\ne0$ for some $u\in U$. Define $T:V\to W$ by
$$Tv = \begin{cases} Sv & \text{if } v\in U,\\ 0 & \text{if } v\in V \text{ and } v\notin U.\end{cases}$$
Prove that $T$ is **not** a linear map on $V$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 10, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
To refute linearity you need one specific pair of vectors on which additivity fails. You have two vectors handed to you by the hypotheses: some $u\in U$ with $Su\ne0$, and — since $U\ne V$ — some $v\in V$ outside $U$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Test additivity on that pair, $u$ and $v$. Everything hinges on deciding whether $u+v$ lies in $U$, and that decision is not a guess: assume it does and derive a contradiction using the fact that $U$ is a subspace.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $u+v\in U$ then $v=(u+v)-u\in U$, since $U$ is closed under addition and under multiplication by $-1$. That contradicts $v\notin U$. Hence $u+v\notin U$, so $T(u+v)=0$ by the second branch of the definition.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Meanwhile $Tu+Tv = Su+0 = Su \ne 0$. So $T(u+v)\ne Tu+Tv$ and additivity fails, hence $T$ is not linear. The write-up should say where each hypothesis is spent: $U\ne V$ supplies $v$, $S\ne0$ supplies $u$, and the subspace property of $U$ is what forces $u+v$ into the second branch. Drop any one of the three and the argument stops — in particular, if $S$ were the zero map then $T$ would be the zero map on all of $V$, which *is* linear.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof, extending a linear map) [interleaves la-04]

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional. Prove that every linear map on a subspace of $V$ can be extended to a linear map on $V$: if $U$ is a subspace of $V$ and $S\in\mathcal{L}(U,W)$, then there exists $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ such that $Tu=Su$ for all $u\in U$.

*(Axler §3.A, Exercise 11, p. 58)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Problem 5 shows that the naive extension — do what $S$ does on $U$, send everything else to $0$ — is not linear. So the extension has to be built by a method that produces a linear map by construction rather than by decree. Which theorem of this unit manufactures linear maps to order?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Theorem 3.5 builds a unique linear map from prescribed values on a basis of the domain. So you need a basis of $V$ whose first vectors form a basis of $U$ — which is exactly what la-04's Theorem 2.33 provides, after la-04's Theorem 2.32 gives $U$ a basis at all. Prescribe values on that basis and let Theorem 3.5 do the rest.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$U$ is finite-dimensional (la-03, Theorem 2.26), so it has a basis $u_1,\dots,u_m$ (Theorem 2.32), which is independent in $V$ and so extends to a basis $u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_n$ of $V$ (Theorem 2.33). By Theorem 3.5 there is a unique $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ with $Tu_j=Su_j$ for each $j$ and $Tw_k=0$ for each $k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
It remains to check $Tu=Su$ for *every* $u\in U$, not merely on the basis: write $u=c_1u_1+\dots+c_mu_m$ and compute $Tu=c_1Tu_1+\dots+c_mTu_m=c_1Su_1+\dots+c_mSu_m=Su$, using linearity of $T$ and then of $S$. Three observations belong in the write-up. The values $Tw_k=0$ are an arbitrary choice — any values would do, so the extension is very far from unique. Compare with Problem 5: there the extension was defined by cases and was not linear; here it is defined on a basis and is linear by construction, and the difference is precisely that Theorem 3.5's recipe respects the coefficient structure while a case split does not. Finally, note that finite-dimensionality of $V$ is used, through Theorem 2.33.
</details>
