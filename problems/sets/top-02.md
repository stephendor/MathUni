## Problem 1 (easy — the two basis conditions, and what generation means)

Munkres's definition (p. 78): a *basis* for a topology on $X$ is a collection
$\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of $X$ such that (1) for each $x \in X$ there is at
least one basis element $B$ containing $x$; and (2) if $x$ belongs to
$B_1 \cap B_2$ then there is a basis element $B_3$ with
$x \in B_3 \subset B_1 \cap B_2$. The topology $\mathcal{T}$ *generated by*
$\mathcal{B}$ is: $U \subset X$ is open if for each $x \in U$ there is
$B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B \subset U$.

(a) For $X = \{a,b,c,d\}$, decide which of these is a basis, naming the failing
condition and the witnessing sets when one fails:
$$\mathcal{B}_1 = \{\{a\},\{b,c\},\{c,d\}\},\qquad
\mathcal{B}_2 = \{\{a\},\{b\},\{c\},\{a,b,c\},\{c,d\}\},\qquad
\mathcal{B}_3 = \{\{a,b\},\{c,d\}\}.$$
(b) For whichever of the three are bases, list the generated topology in full.
(c) Prove Lemma 13.1: $\mathcal{T}$ equals the collection of all unions of
elements of $\mathcal{B}$.
(d) Munkres remarks that the expression of an open set as a union of basis
elements "is not, however, unique", and that this makes "basis" in topology
differ "drastically" from its use in linear algebra. Give an explicit open set
in one of your topologies from (b) with two genuinely different expressions as
a union of basis elements.

*(Munkres §13, p. 78 for both definitions; p. 80 for Lemma 13.1 and for the
remark about linear algebra.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Condition (2) is about the *intersection* of two basis elements, and it is
satisfied vacuously when that intersection is empty. Check $\mathcal{B}_1$'s
two three-element candidates against that.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Condition (1) is a covering condition — does $\bigcup \mathcal{B} = X$? — and
fails loudly. Condition (2) needs checking only on pairs whose intersection is
non-empty. For (c), one inclusion is that $\mathcal{T}$ is closed under unions
and contains $\mathcal{B}$; the other is Munkres's $U = \bigcup_{x \in U} B_x$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathcal{B}_1$ fails (2): $\{b,c\} \cap \{c,d\} = \{c\}$, the point $c$ lies in
it, and no basis element is contained in $\{c\}$. $\mathcal{B}_2$ and
$\mathcal{B}_3$ both satisfy (1) and (2). $\mathcal{B}_3$ generates
$\{\varnothing, \{a,b\},\{c,d\},X\}$ — four open sets — and
$\mathcal{B}_2$ generates twelve. Note that $\{d\}$ is open in neither.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\mathcal{B}_3$. Condition (1): $\{a,b\} \cup \{c,d\} = X$, so every point
lies in a basis element. Condition (2): the only pair is
$\{a,b\} \cap \{c,d\} = \varnothing$, and the condition quantifies over points
of the intersection, of which there are none — so it holds vacuously. Hence
$\mathcal{B}_3$ is a basis.

$\mathcal{B}_1$: ⟨your step — compute $\{b,c\} \cap \{c,d\}$ and test
condition (2) at the point it contains⟩

$\mathcal{B}_2$: ⟨your step — condition (1) is a glance; for condition (2) the
only pairs with non-empty intersection involve $\{a,b,c\}$ or $\{c\}$, so there
are five to check, not ten⟩

(c) ($\supset$) Each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$: for $x \in B$ take
$B$ itself. A topology is closed under unions, so every union of basis elements
is in $\mathcal{T}$.
($\subset$) ⟨your step — Munkres's $U = \bigcup_{x \in U} B_x$; say where each
$B_x$ comes from and why the union is exactly $U$ and not larger⟩

(b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — going backwards, from a topology to a basis)

Lemma 13.2 (p. 80) runs in the reverse direction: let $X$ be a topological
space and $\mathcal{C}$ a collection of **open** sets such that for each open
$U$ and each $x \in U$ there is $C \in \mathcal{C}$ with $x \in C \subset U$;
then $\mathcal{C}$ is a basis for the topology of $X$.

(a) Prove Lemma 13.2. There are three things to check — the two basis
conditions, and that the generated topology is the one you started with — and
Munkres's proof of the last part uses Lemma 13.1 in one direction. Say where.
(b) Why does the hypothesis "$\mathcal{C}$ is a collection of open sets" appear
at all? Show by example that dropping it breaks the conclusion.
(c) Apply Lemma 13.2 to show that
$\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \{(a,b) \mid a < b,\ a,b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is a
basis generating the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ — this is Munkres's own
Exercise 8(a), and he tells you which lemma to apply. Note what you get for
free: the standard topology has a **countable** basis.
(d) Munkres's Exercise 8(b) asserts that
$\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \{[a,b) \mid a < b,\ a,b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$
generates a topology *different* from the lower limit topology
$\mathbb{R}_\ell$. Prove it, by producing a set open in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ that
is not open in the topology generated by $\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}$.

*(Munkres §13, p. 80 for Lemma 13.2; pp. 83–84 for Exercises 8(a) and 8(b),
which are set as exercises and not worked in the text.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), between any two reals there is a rational. For (d), consider
$[\sqrt{2}, 3)$ and ask what a $\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}$-basis element
containing $\sqrt{2}$ must look like.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Given $(a,b)$ open in the standard topology and $x \in (a,b)$, pick
rationals $p, q$ with $a < p < x < q < b$; then $x \in (p,q) \subset (a,b)$.
Lemma 13.2 does the rest. (d) A basis element $[p,q)$ containing $\sqrt{2}$ has
$p \leq \sqrt{2}$, and $p$ is rational, so $p < \sqrt{2}$ strictly — hence
$[p,q)$ contains points below $\sqrt{2}$ and cannot sit inside
$[\sqrt{2},3)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the standard topology and
$\mathcal{C} = \{[a, b) \mid a < b\}$. Every hypothesis of Lemma 13.2 except
openness survives: given $U$ open and $x \in U$ there is $\delta > 0$ with
$(x - \delta, x + \delta) \subset U$, and then $[x, x + \delta) \in \mathcal{C}$
satisfies $x \in [x, x+\delta) \subset U$. But the members are not open in
$\mathbb{R}$, and the topology $\mathcal{C}$ generates is $\mathbb{R}_\ell$,
strictly finer than the one you started with — so $\mathcal{C}$ is not a basis
*for the topology of $X$*, and the conclusion fails on the openness clause alone.
Note what a *bad* witness would look like: $\{[x, x+1) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$
also consists of non-open sets, but it fails the covering condition too — no
member fits inside $U = (0, 1/2)$ — so it breaks two hypotheses at once and
isolates neither.
(d) $[\sqrt{2}, 3)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ and is not open in the
topology generated by $\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Condition (1).* Given $x \in X$: the set $X$ is open, so by hypothesis
there is $C \in \mathcal{C}$ with $x \in C \subset X$.
*Condition (2).* Let $x \in C_1 \cap C_2$ with $C_1, C_2 \in \mathcal{C}$. Both
are open, so $C_1 \cap C_2$ is open (top-01, axiom (3)); by hypothesis there is
$C_3 \in \mathcal{C}$ with $x \in C_3 \subset C_1 \cap C_2$.
*The topologies agree.* Write $\mathcal{T}$ for the topology of $X$ and
$\mathcal{T}'$ for the one generated by $\mathcal{C}$.
⟨your step: $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal{T}'$ is the hypothesis restated; for
$\mathcal{T}' \subset \mathcal{T}$, express $W \in \mathcal{T}'$ as a union of
elements of $\mathcal{C}$ — which lemma lets you? — and finish⟩

(c) Let $U$ be open in the standard topology and let $x \in U$. There is a
basis element $(a,b)$ with $x \in (a,b) \subset U$. ⟨your step: choose the
rationals, then invoke Lemma 13.2 and say what its hypothesis required you to
check about $\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{Q}}$'s members⟩

(b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — comparing topologies through their bases)

Lemma 13.3 (p. 81): if $\mathcal{B}$, $\mathcal{B}'$ are bases for
$\mathcal{T}$, $\mathcal{T}'$ on $X$, then $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than
$\mathcal{T}$ if and only if for each $x \in X$ and each $B \in \mathcal{B}$
containing $x$ there is $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ with $x \in B' \subset B$.

Munkres defines three topologies on $\mathbb{R}$ (pp. 81–82): the **standard**
topology from the intervals $(a,b)$; the **lower limit** topology
$\mathbb{R}_\ell$ from the intervals $[a,b)$; and the **$K$-topology**
$\mathbb{R}_K$ from the intervals $(a,b)$ together with the sets $(a,b) - K$,
where $K = \{1/n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}_+\}$.

(a) Prove Lemma 13.3, both directions.
(b) Prove that $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ is strictly finer than the standard topology,
following Munkres's argument, and say which of the two directions of
Lemma 13.3 each half uses.
(c) Prove that $\mathbb{R}_K$ is strictly finer than the standard topology.
State the basis element and the point that witness strictness.
(d) Munkres leaves the last part of Lemma 13.4 to the reader — "We leave it to
you to show that the topologies of $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ and $\mathbb{R}_K$ are not
comparable" (p. 82), and sets it again as Exercise 6. Do it: produce a set open
in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ and not in $\mathbb{R}_K$, and a set open in
$\mathbb{R}_K$ and not in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$.

*(Munkres §13, p. 81 for Lemma 13.3; pp. 81–82 for the three topologies and for
Lemma 13.4, whose incomparability clause is stated and left unproved.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d), one direction reuses the witness from (b). For the other, $0$ and the
set $(-1,1) - K$ are the pair to think about — is any $[a,b)$ containing $0$
inside it?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) The "finer" half: given $(a,b) \ni x$, the basis element $[x,b)$ of
$\mathbb{R}_\ell$ contains $x$ and lies inside — that is criterion (2) verified,
so (2) $\Rightarrow$ (1) applies. The "strictly" half is a direct argument, not
an application of the lemma: given $[x,d)$, no open interval containing $x$ lies
inside it, because any such interval contains points less than $x$.
(d) For $\mathbb{R}_K$ not finer than $\mathbb{R}_\ell$: the set $[0,1)$ is
$\mathbb{R}_\ell$-open; a $\mathbb{R}_K$-basis element containing $0$ is either
an interval $(a,b) \ni 0$ or $(a,b) - K \ni 0$, and both contain negatives.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The witness is $B = (-1,1) - K$ and the point $0 \in B$: every open
interval containing $0$ contains $1/n$ for large $n$, hence meets $K$, hence is
not contained in $B$.
(d) $[0,1)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ and not in $\mathbb{R}_K$;
$(-1,1) - K$ is open in $\mathbb{R}_K$ and not in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) (2) $\Rightarrow$ (1). Let $U \in \mathcal{T}$ and $x \in U$. Since
$\mathcal{B}$ generates $\mathcal{T}$ there is $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with
$x \in B \subset U$; by (2) there is $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ with
$x \in B' \subset B \subset U$. As $x$ was arbitrary, $U \in \mathcal{T}'$ by
the definition of the generated topology.
(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2). ⟨your step: $B$ is itself $\mathcal{T}$-open, hence
$\mathcal{T}'$-open; now apply the definition of generation on the
$\mathcal{B}'$ side⟩

(c) Every basis element $(a,b)$ of the standard topology is also a basis
element of $\mathbb{R}_K$, so criterion (2) holds trivially and $\mathbb{R}_K$
is finer. For strictness, take $B = (-1,1) - K$, a basis element of
$\mathbb{R}_K$, and the point $0 \in B$.
⟨your step: show no standard basis element $(a,b)$ with $0 \in (a,b)$ satisfies
$(a,b) \subset B$ — what does $b > 0$ force?⟩

(b), (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the order topology, and the cases Munkres leaves)

Munkres's definition (p. 84): let $X$ be a simply ordered set with more than one
element, and let $\mathcal{B}$ consist of (1) all open intervals $(a,b)$;
(2) all $[a_0, b)$ where $a_0$ is the smallest element of $X$, if any; (3) all
$(a, b_0]$ where $b_0$ is the largest element, if any. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a
basis, and the topology it generates is the **order topology**.

(a) Munkres checks the first basis condition and then writes of the second,
"Several cases need to be checked; we leave it to you" (p. 84). Check them.
Intersect a type-(1) with a type-(1), a type-(1) with a type-(2), a type-(2)
with a type-(2), and so on, and confirm each intersection is again of one of the
three types or is empty. Say how many cases there are and why the type-(2) with
type-(3) case is the one that needs the hypothesis "$X$ has more than one
element" the least.
(b) Prove Munkres's Example 3: the order topology on $\mathbb{Z}_+$ is the
discrete topology. Then prove Example 4: on $X = \{1,2\} \times \mathbb{Z}_+$
in the dictionary order, writing $a_n = 1 \times n$ and $b_n = 2 \times n$, the
order topology is **not** discrete, and the single exception is $\{b_1\}$.
Prove both halves — that every other one-point set is open, and that $\{b_1\}$
is not.
(c) Define the four *rays* determined by $a$ (p. 85). Prove that the open rays
$(a, +\infty)$ and $(-\infty, a)$ are open in the order topology, treating
separately the case where $X$ has a largest element and the case where it does
not.
(d) Prove that the open rays form a **subbasis** for the order topology, in
Munkres's sense (p. 82: a subbasis is a collection whose union is $X$, and the
topology it generates is all unions of finite intersections of its members).
Both inclusions are needed; for one of them, express each of the three basis
types as a finite intersection of open rays.

*(Munkres §14, p. 84 for the definition and the left-to-the-reader case check;
p. 85 for Examples 3 and 4 and for the definition of rays; p. 86 for the proof
that open rays are open and form a subbasis. The subbasis definition is §13,
p. 82.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (b), the point of $\{b_1\}$ is that it has no immediate predecessor in $X$:
everything below it is an $a_n$, and there are infinitely many of those with no
largest.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Work with endpoints. An intersection of two intervals is an interval whose
endpoints are the larger of the two left endpoints and the smaller of the two
right endpoints; the only question is whether the resulting endpoint types are
among (1)–(3), and a half-open end can only survive if it was the extreme
element of $X$.
(d) $(a,b) = (-\infty,b) \cap (a,+\infty)$. The types (2) and (3), where they
exist, *are* open rays: $[a_0, b) = (-\infty, b)$ and $(a, b_0] = (a, +\infty)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) For $n > 1$, $\{n\} = (n-1, n+1)$ is a type-(1) basis element; and
$\{1\} = [1,2)$ is type (2). For $X = \{1,2\}\times\mathbb{Z}_+$: any basis
element containing $b_1$ is of the form $(a_m, x)$ or $(a_m, b_k)$ and so
contains points of the $a_i$ sequence.
(c) If $X$ has a largest element $b_0$ then $(a,+\infty) = (a, b_0]$, a type-(3)
basis element; if not, $(a,+\infty) = \bigcup_{x > a} (a,x)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Consider $(a, +\infty) = \{x \mid x > a\}$.
*Case 1: $X$ has a largest element $b_0$.* Then every $x > a$ satisfies
$x \leq b_0$, so $(a,+\infty) = (a, b_0]$, which is a basis element of type (3).
Open.
*Case 2: $X$ has no largest element.* Then for each $x > a$ there is $y > x$,
so $x \in (a,y) \subset (a,+\infty)$; hence
$(a,+\infty) = \bigcup_{y>a}(a,y)$, a union of type-(1) basis elements. Open.
⟨your step: the ray $(-\infty, a)$, by the symmetric argument — say which
element of $X$ plays the role $b_0$ played⟩

(d) The open rays are open by (c), so the topology they generate is contained
in the order topology. ⟨your step: the reverse — express $(a,b)$, then $[a_0,b)$
and $(a,b_0]$, in terms of open rays, and conclude⟩

(a), (b) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the subspace topology, and where inheritance fails)

Munkres's definition (p. 88): for $Y \subset X$, the **subspace topology** is
$\mathcal{T}_Y = \{Y \cap U \mid U \in \mathcal{T}\}$.

(a) Verify that $\mathcal{T}_Y$ is a topology, using Munkres's two displayed
identities (p. 89). Then prove Lemma 16.1: if $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $X$
then $\mathcal{B}_Y = \{B \cap Y \mid B \in \mathcal{B}\}$ is a basis for the
subspace topology. Munkres's proof is three lines and ends by citing an earlier
lemma; name it and say exactly what it supplies.
(b) Prove Lemma 16.2: if $U$ is open in $Y$ and $Y$ is open in $X$, then $U$ is
open in $X$. Then show by example that the hypothesis "$Y$ is open in $X$"
cannot be dropped — give $X$, $Y$ and $U$ with $U$ open in $Y$ and not in $X$.
(c) Prove Theorem 16.3: for $A \subset X$ and $B \subset Y$, the product
topology on $A \times B$ agrees with the subspace topology it inherits from
$X \times Y$. Munkres's proof compares *bases* rather than open sets — say why
that is legitimate, and which result of §13 licenses it.
(d) Now the anomaly. Munkres warns that for an ordered set $X$ and $Y \subset X$,
"the resulting order topology on $Y$ need not be the same as the topology that
$Y$ inherits as a subspace of $X$" (p. 90). Work his Example 2 in full:
$Y = [0,1) \cup \{2\} \subset \mathbb{R}$, where $\{2\}$ is open in the subspace
topology and not in the order topology on $Y$. Then define *convex* (p. 90) and
prove Theorem 16.4: if $Y$ is convex in $X$ then the two topologies agree.
Finally, say in one sentence which hypothesis of Theorem 16.4 the set
$[0,1) \cup \{2\}$ fails, and exhibit the pair of points that witnesses it.

*(Munkres §16, p. 88 for the subspace topology; p. 89 for the verification, for
Lemma 16.1 and Lemma 16.2; pp. 89–90 for Theorem 16.3; p. 90 for the warning,
Examples 1–3 and the definition of convex; p. 91 for Theorem 16.4.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For the counterexample in (b), take $Y$ to be a closed interval in
$\mathbb{R}$. For the last part of (d), convexity fails at a pair straddling
the gap in $Y$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Two topologies with the same basis are equal, because by Lemma 13.1 each is
the collection of all unions of that basis. So it suffices to show
$(U \times V) \cap (A \times B) = (U \cap A) \times (V \cap B)$ and to read
both sides as general basis elements.
(d) For Theorem 16.4, Munkres works with the subbasis of open rays from §14
rather than with the basis, which is what makes the proof short: intersecting
an open ray of $X$ with a convex $Y$ gives an open ray of $Y$, or $Y$, or
$\varnothing$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $X = \mathbb{R}$, $Y = [0,1]$, $U = [0, 1/2)$. Then $U = Y \cap (-1, 1/2)$
is open in $Y$, and $U$ is not open in $\mathbb{R}$ because no interval about
$0$ lies inside it.
(d) $\{2\} = Y \cap (3/2, 5/2)$ is open in the subspace topology. In the order
topology on $Y$, a basis element containing $2$ has the form
$\{x \in Y \mid a < x \leq 2\}$ — note $2$ is the largest element of $Y$, so it
is a type-(3) set — and any such set contains points of $[0,1)$.
Convexity fails at $a = 1/2$, $b = 2$: the interval $(1/2, 2)$ of $\mathbb{R}$
is not contained in $Y$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>

⟨your step: name the lemma this hands off to, and state precisely what its

(d) *Theorem 16.4.* Let $Y$ be convex in $X$ and consider the ray

⟨your step: say why "lower bound or upper bound" is exactly what convexity

(b), (c) ⟨your step⟩
</details>
