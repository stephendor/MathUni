## Problem 1 (easy–medium — cosets)

(a) State Definition 11.50 (p. 254) and prove that the left cosets of a subgroup $K\le G$ partition $G$, with every coset of the same size as $K$.

(b) Define the **index** $[G:K]$ and prove $|G| = [G:K]\,|K|$ for finite $G$.

(c) Compute the left cosets of $K = \langle x\rangle = \{e,x\}$ in $D_6$, and the right cosets, and observe they differ — Example 11.51 (p. 255).

(d) Remark 11.56 (p. 256) notes that $G$ acts on the set of left cosets whether or not $K$ is normal. Describe that action and its orbits.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $g\in gK$, and $gK\cap hK\ne\varnothing$ forces $gK = hK$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Use the relations $x^2=e$, $y^3=e$, $xy=y^{-1}x$ from aa-25.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $g = ge\in gK$, so the cosets cover $G$. If $z\in gK\cap hK$, write $z = gk = hk'$; then $g = hk'k^{-1}$, so $gK\subseteq hK$ and by symmetry they are equal. And $k\mapsto gk$ is a bijection $K\to gK$, with inverse $z\mapsto g^{-1}z$. So all cosets have $|K|$ elements.

(b) The index is the number of left cosets. By (a) they partition $G$ into equal blocks of size $|K|$, so $|G| = [G:K]\,|K|$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) In $D_6 = \{e,y,y^2,x,xy,xy^2\}$ with $K = \{e,x\}$, and using $y^kx = xy^{-k}$: the left cosets are
$$K = \{e,x\},\qquad yK = \{y,\ yx\} = \{y,\ xy^2\},\qquad y^2K = \{y^2,\ xy\}.$$
The right cosets are
$$K = \{e,x\},\qquad Ky = \{y,\ xy\},\qquad Ky^2 = \{y^2,\ xy^2\}.$$
So $yK = \{y,xy^2\}$ while $Ky = \{y,xy\}$ — different sets. $K$ is not normal, which is Example 11.51's point.

(d) $G$ acts on $G/K$ by $g\cdot(aK) = (ga)K$, well defined because $aK = bK$ gives $gaK = gbK$. The action is **transitive**: every coset $aK$ is $a\cdot K$. Its stabiliser at $K$ is $K$ itself, so orbit–stabiliser (aa-24 Problem 5(b)) recovers $[G:K] = |G|/|K|$ — part (b) again, now as an instance of a general count rather than a separate argument.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — normality)

(a) State Definition 11.52 (p. 255) and prove Remark 11.53 (p. 255): it suffices to check $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$ for every $g$.

(b) Remark 11.54 (p. 255) warns that $gK = Kg$ does **not** mean $gk = kg$ for each $k\in K$. Exhibit a normal subgroup and elements witnessing the difference.

(c) Prove Example 11.59 (p. 257): a subgroup of index $2$ is normal.

(d) Prove Example 11.60 (p. 257): the centre $Z(G)$ is normal. Prove Example 11.62 (p. 258): $\mathcal{A}_n$ is normal in $S_n$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Apply the containment to $g^{-1}$ as well.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) With two cosets, the complement of $K$ is both the other left coset and the other right coset.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Suppose $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$ for every $g\in G$. Applying it to $g^{-1}$ gives $g^{-1}Kg\subseteq K$, and conjugating that containment by $g$ gives $K\subseteq gKg^{-1}$. So the containment is an equality for every $g$. The point is that "for every $g$" is what upgrades one containment to two.

(b) Take $G = D_6$ and $K = \langle y\rangle = \{e,y,y^2\}$, normal by (c) since $[G:K]=2$. Then $xK = Kx$ as sets, but $xy = y^2x \ne yx$. So the coset equation is a statement about sets and not about elements — this is the single most commonly misread definition in the chapter.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $[G:K]=2$. The left cosets are $K$ and $G\setminus K$; the right cosets are $K$ and $G\setminus K$. So for $g\in K$, $gK = K = Kg$; and for $g\notin K$, $gK = G\setminus K = Kg$. Hence $gK = Kg$ always.

(d) *Centre:* for $z\in Z(G)$ and any $g$, $gzg^{-1} = zgg^{-1} = z\in Z(G)$, so $gZ(G)g^{-1}\subseteq Z(G)$ and (a) applies. Note $Z(G)$ is more than normal — every one of its elements commutes with everything, which by (b) is strictly stronger than normality of the subgroup.

*Alternating group:* $\mathcal{A}_n = \ker\big((-1)^{(\cdot)}\big)$ by aa-25 Problem 4(d), and kernels are normal by Proposition 11.58 (p. 256). Alternatively $[S_n:\mathcal{A}_n]=2$ and (c) applies. Two proofs, and the first is the one that generalises.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — normal means a union of classes)

(a) Prove Proposition 11.57 (p. 256): a subgroup $K\le G$ is normal if and only if it is a union of conjugacy classes of $G$.

(b) Use the conjugacy classes of $D_8$ computed in aa-24 Problem 6(d) to list every normal subgroup of $D_8$.

(c) Do the same for $S_3$.

(d) Explain how (a) turns "is this subgroup normal?" from a question about $|G|$ conjugations into a question about a list.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $K$ normal says $gKg^{-1}=K$, i.e. $K$ is closed under conjugation, i.e. it contains the whole class of each of its elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) A normal subgroup contains $e$, has order dividing $8$, and is a union of classes of sizes $1,1,2,2,2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $K$ is normal iff $gkg^{-1}\in K$ for all $g\in G$, $k\in K$ — by Remark 11.53 — iff for each $k\in K$ the entire conjugacy class of $k$ lies in $K$. Since $K$ is the union of the classes it meets, that is exactly "$K$ is a union of classes".

(b) $D_8$ has classes $\{e\}$, $\{r^2\}$, $\{r,r^3\}$, $\{s,r^2s\}$, $\{rs,r^3s\}$ of sizes $1,1,2,2,2$. A normal subgroup is a union of these containing $\{e\}$, of order dividing $8$ (Problem 1(b)). The possibilities: $\{e\}$; $\{e,r^2\}$; $\{e,r^2\}\cup\{r,r^3\} = \langle r\rangle$; $\{e,r^2\}\cup\{s,r^2s\}$; $\{e,r^2\}\cup\{rs,r^3s\}$; and $D_8$. **Six normal subgroups**, out of the ten subgroups counted in aa-23 Problem 5(b) — the four missing ones are the order-two subgroups generated by a single reflection, and each fails because its reflection's class has two elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $S_3$ has classes of sizes $1,3,2$. Unions containing $e$ with order dividing $6$: $\{e\}$; $\{e\}\cup\{$three-cycles$\} = \mathcal{A}_3$, of order $3$; and $S_3$. The union $\{e\}\cup\{$transpositions$\}$ has four elements, and $4\nmid6$, so it is excluded — and independently it is not a subgroup at all, by aa-23 Problem 2(d). **Three normal subgroups** out of six.

(d) Directly, normality asks for $|G|\cdot|K|$ conjugations to be checked, or $|G|$ set equalities. Proposition 11.57 replaces that with: compute the conjugacy classes once, then read off which unions are subgroups. The classes are computed once per group and reused for every subgroup question — and in aa-28 the same list, with the class sizes, becomes the class equation and answers questions about $G$ that mention no subgroup at all.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — kernels and normal subgroups are the same thing)

(a) Prove Proposition 11.58 (p. 256): $\ker\varphi$ is normal for every group homomorphism $\varphi$.

(b) Prove the converse half of Remark 11.66 (p. 259): every normal subgroup is a kernel.

(c) Prove that $\varphi$ is injective if and only if $\ker\varphi = \{e\}$.

(d) State the two facts of (a) and (b) as one sentence and compare with aa-10 Problem 1(c).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Needs the quotient group of Problem 5; state it and use it.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $\varphi(g)=\varphi(h)$ iff $\varphi(gh^{-1}) = e$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup by aa-23 Problem 3(c). For normality, let $k\in\ker\varphi$ and $g\in G$; then
$$\varphi(gkg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} = \varphi(g)e\varphi(g)^{-1} = e,$$
so $gkg^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$, and Remark 11.53 finishes it.

(c) If $\varphi$ is injective then only $e$ maps to $e$. Conversely if $\ker\varphi = \{e\}$ and $\varphi(g)=\varphi(h)$, then $\varphi(gh^{-1}) = e$, so $gh^{-1}=e$ and $g=h$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Given $K$ normal, Theorem 11.64 (p. 258) makes $G/K$ a group and the projection $\pi : G\to G/K$, $g\mapsto gK$, a homomorphism. Its kernel is $\{g : gK = K\} = K$. So $K$ is a kernel.

(d) **A subgroup of a group is normal if and only if it is the kernel of some homomorphism.** That is aa-10 Problem 1(c) word for word with "ideal" replaced by "normal subgroup" and "ring" by "group" — and the same reading applies: one half says the definition is not too wide, the other that it is not too narrow, and together they license using either description. The pattern now has three instances in this module, counting submodules and kernels of module maps (aa-16 Problem 6(a) with Remark 8.42), and in each case the intrinsic definition was chosen so that the property can be recognised without producing a homomorphism first.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the quotient group, and what normality is for)

(a) Prove Theorem 11.64 (p. 258): if $K$ is normal, the rule $(aK)(bK) := (ab)K$ is well defined and makes $G/K$ a group — Definition 11.65 (p. 259).

(b) Show the rule is **not** well defined when $K$ is not normal, using $K = \{e,x\}$ in $D_6$ from Problem 1(c).

(c) Compare with aa-16 Problem 5(d), where every submodule admitted a quotient. Explain the difference in one paragraph.

(d) Compute $D_6/\langle y\rangle$ and $S_n/\mathcal{A}_n$ — Example 11.69 (p. 260).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Given $aK = a'K$ and $bK = b'K$, show $abK = a'b'K$; the normality is used to move a $K$ past a group element.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Find two representatives of the same coset whose products with a fixed element land in different cosets.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Suppose $a' = ak_1$ and $b' = bk_2$ with $k_i\in K$. Then
$$a'b' = ak_1bk_2 = ab\,(b^{-1}k_1b)\,k_2,$$
and $b^{-1}k_1b\in K$ **because $K$ is normal**. So $a'b'\in abK$, and the product of cosets is well defined. Associativity, the identity $eK$ and inverses $(aK)^{-1} = a^{-1}K$ are then inherited from $G$.

(b) In $D_6$ with $K = \{e,x\}$, the three left cosets are $K = \{e,x\}$, $yK = \{y,xy^2\}$ and $y^2K = \{y^2,xy\}$, computed in Problem 1(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) Take the pair of cosets $(K,\ yK)$ and evaluate the proposed product with two different representatives of the *first* one. Both $e$ and $x$ lie in $K$, and
$$e\cdot y = y\in yK,\qquad x\cdot y = xy\in y^2K .$$
Since $yK\ne y^2K$, the rule assigns $K\cdot yK$ two different values and therefore defines nothing. The failure is exactly the correction term of (a): with $b = y$ and $k_1 = x$, that term is $y^{-1}xy = xy^2\notin K$, so the step "$b^{-1}k_1b\in K$" is false — and it is the only step of (a) that used normality.

(c) For **modules**, the sub-object is required to be stable under everything the ring does to it, and multiplying cosets is not part of the structure — the only operations are addition, which is symmetric in the two arguments, and scaling, where the scalar comes from outside. So every submodule works. For **groups** there is one operation and both arguments come from the same group, so the correction term $b^{-1}k_1b$ appears and has to land back in $K$. Normality is precisely the condition making that happen — the exact analogue of absorption for ideals in aa-09 Problem 4(d), and for the same reason: a quotient's multiplication has to survive changing representatives on both sides.

(d) $\langle y\rangle$ has index $2$ in $D_6$, so $D_6/\langle y\rangle$ has two elements and is $\cong C_2$. Likewise $[S_n:\mathcal{A}_n]=2$ for $n\ge2$, so $S_n/\mathcal{A}_n\cong C_2$ — and under the isomorphism the two cosets are the even and the odd permutations, so the quotient map *is* the sign homomorphism. Example 11.69 is that observation.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — normality is not transitive)

(a) Prove Example 11.63 (p. 258): $V = \{e,(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$ is a normal subgroup of $\mathcal{A}_4$.

(b) Show $V$ is abelian, and deduce that $N = \{e,(1\,2)(3\,4)\}$ is normal in $V$.

(c) Show $N$ is **not** normal in $\mathcal{A}_4$, by exhibiting a conjugating element.

(d) Conclude that "normal in" is not a transitive relation, and say which step of Problem 3(a) explains why.

(e) Prove Example 11.61 (p. 257): the rotation subgroup $\langle y\rangle\cong C_n$ is normal in $D_{2n}$ for every $n\ge3$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Conjugation preserves cycle type, and $V$ consists of the identity together with every element of cycle type $2{+}2$ in $S_4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Conjugate $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ by a three-cycle.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $V$ is a subgroup: each non-identity element is its own inverse, and the product of any two distinct ones is the third — for instance $(1\,2)(3\,4)\cdot(1\,3)(2\,4) = (1\,4)(2\,3)$. It is normal in $\mathcal{A}_4$ by Proposition 11.57 (p. 256): conjugation preserves cycle type, and $V$ is $\{e\}$ together with *all three* elements of type $2{+}2$, hence a union of classes.

(b) $V$ is abelian: it has four elements, each of order dividing $2$, so by aa-23 Problem 6(a) it is abelian — indeed $V\cong C_2\times C_2$. In an abelian group every subgroup is normal, since $gkg^{-1} = k$. So $N\trianglelefteq V$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Take $g = (1\,3\,4)$, i.e. the permutation sending $1\mapsto3$, $3\mapsto4$, $4\mapsto1$, and fixing $2$. Then
$$g\,(1\,2)(3\,4)\,g^{-1} = (3\,2)(4\,1) = (1\,4)(2\,3),$$
because conjugation by $g$ relabels each entry by $g$. That element is in $V$ but not in $N$. So $gNg^{-1}\ne N$ and $N$ is not normal in $\mathcal{A}_4$.

(d) $N\trianglelefteq V$ and $V\trianglelefteq\mathcal{A}_4$, and $N\not\trianglelefteq\mathcal{A}_4$. So normality is **not transitive**, and "normal subgroup" is not a property of a subgroup alone but of a pair.

Problem 3(a) explains it: normality of $N$ in $V$ says $N$ is a union of $V$-conjugacy classes, and normality in $\mathcal{A}_4$ says it is a union of $\mathcal{A}_4$-classes. The larger group has fewer, larger classes — here the three elements of type $2{+}2$ form one $\mathcal{A}_4$-class and three separate $V$-classes — so being a union of the small classes says nothing about the large ones. Everything about (c) is contained in that sentence.

(e) $\langle y\rangle$ has order $n$ in a group of order $2n$, so it has index $2$ and Example 11.59 (p. 257) applies. Directly: $x y^k x^{-1} = y^{-k}\in\langle y\rangle$ by aa-25 Problem 1(c), and every element of $D_{2n}$ is $y^j$ or $xy^j$, so every conjugate of a rotation is a rotation.
</details>
