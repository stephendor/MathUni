## Problem 1 (easy — four limits from the definition)

Use Definition 4.2.1 to prove each limit statement.

(a) $\lim_{x \to 2}(2x + 4) = 8$
(b) $\lim_{x \to 0} x^3 = 0$
(c) $\lim_{x \to 2} x^3 = 8$
(d) $\lim_{x \to \pi} [[x]] = 3$, where $[[x]]$ is the greatest integer not exceeding $x$

*(Abbott §4.2, Exercise 4.2.1, p. 108)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In each case start from $|f(x) - L|$ and work until $|x - c|$ appears as a factor. Part (d) is different in kind — nothing needs to be estimated.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) is exact: $|2x + 4 - 8| = 2|x - 2|$. (c) needs the factorisation $x^3 - 8 = (x-2)(x^2 + 2x + 4)$ together with a preliminary restriction such as $\delta \le 1$ to bound the second factor.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Assume at the outset $|x - 2| < 1$, so $1 < x < 3$ and $x^2 + 2x + 4 < 9 + 6 + 4 = 19$. Then $|x^3 - 8| < 19|x - 2|$, so $\delta = \min\{1, \epsilon/19\}$ works.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Given $\epsilon > 0$ take $\delta = \epsilon/2$; then $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$ gives $|2x + 4 - 8| = 2|x-2| < \epsilon$.

(d) Take $\delta = \min\{\pi - 3, 4 - \pi\}$. For $0 < |x - \pi| < \delta$ we have $3 < x < 4$, so $[[x]] = 3$ exactly, and $|[[x]] - 3| = 0 < \epsilon$ for every $\epsilon$.

Two habits to establish now, because they recur for the rest of the course. The "assume $\delta \le 1$ first" move in (c) is not a trick: you are allowed to shrink $\delta$ freely, since a smaller response answers the same challenge. And in (d) the response does not depend on $\epsilon$ at all — write that down explicitly rather than letting it pass, because noticing when the usual dependence is absent is what an-11's uniform continuity turns into a definition.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — two limits that do not exist)

Use Corollary 4.2.5 to show that neither limit exists.

(a) $\lim_{x \to 0} |x|/x$
(b) $\lim_{x \to 1} g(x)$, where $g$ is Dirichlet's function: $g(x) = 1$ for rational $x$ and $g(x) = 0$ for irrational $x$

*(Abbott §4.2, Exercise 4.2.3, p. 108)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You need two sequences approaching the point along which the images go to different places. For (b) you need one sequence of rationals and one of irrationals.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Corollary 4.2.5 requires the sequences to lie in the domain, converge to $c$, and avoid $c$. Check all three conditions explicitly; the third is the one most often skipped.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $x_n = 1/n$ gives $|x_n|/x_n = 1$ for every $n$, and $y_n = -1/n$ gives $-1$. Both sequences converge to $0$ and avoid it, and $1 \ne -1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Take $x_n = 1 + 1/n$, a sequence of rationals converging to $1$ with no term equal to $1$; then $g(x_n) = 1$ for all $n$. Take $y_n = 1 + \sqrt{2}/n$, irrational for every $n$ since $\sqrt 2/n$ is irrational and $1$ is rational; then $g(y_n) = 0$. Both converge to $1$, so by Corollary 4.2.5 the limit does not exist.

Justify the irrationality of $y_n$ rather than asserting it — if $1 + \sqrt2/n$ were rational then $\sqrt 2 = n(y_n - 1)$ would be rational, contradicting an-01's Theorem 1.1.1. Small justifications like this are where the marks in an analysis course actually sit, and the same construction recurs whenever a sequence of irrationals converging to a given point is needed.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — continuity at every isolated point)

(a) Show, using Definition 4.3.1, that any function with domain $\mathbb{Z}$ is continuous at every point of its domain.
(b) Show in general that if $c$ is an isolated point of $A$, then every $f : A \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $c$.

*(Abbott §4.3, Exercise 4.3.4, p. 113)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Recall an-07's Definition 3.2.6: an isolated point has a neighbourhood meeting $A$ only at itself.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta$ so that $V_\delta(c) \cap A = \{c\}$. Then the only $x$ the definition speaks about is $c$ itself, and the required inequality is $0 < \epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) For $c \in \mathbb{Z}$, take $\delta = 1/2$. If $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $|x - c| < 1/2$, then $x = c$, so $|f(x) - f(c)| = 0 < \epsilon$ whatever $f$ and $\epsilon$ are.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Since $c$ is isolated, there is $\epsilon_0 > 0$ with $V_{\epsilon_0}(c) \cap A = \{c\}$. Given any $\epsilon > 0$, set $\delta = \epsilon_0$. Any $x \in A$ with $|x - c| < \delta$ lies in $V_{\epsilon_0}(c) \cap A$ and so equals $c$, giving $|f(x) - f(c)| = 0 < \epsilon$. Hence $f$ is continuous at $c$.

Then say what this reveals about the definition. Continuity at an isolated point is free and carries no information — which is why Theorem 4.3.2(ii) has to be stated with the proviso "if $c$ is a limit point of $A$". Definition 4.3.1 and "the limit equals the value" agree exactly at limit points and come apart only here, and this exercise is the whole of the difference.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — a function continuous at every irrational)

Thomae's function is defined by $t(x) = 1$ if $x = 0$; $t(x) = 1/n$ if $x = m/n \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}$ is in lowest terms with $n > 0$; and $t(x) = 0$ if $x \notin \mathbb{Q}$.

(a) Show that $t$ fails to be continuous at every rational point.
(b) Using Theorem 4.3.2(iii), show that $t$ is continuous at every irrational point.

*(Abbott §4.3, Exercise 4.3.6, p. 113)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), fix an irrational $c$ and $\epsilon > 0$, and consider the set $\{x : t(x) \ge \epsilon\}$. How many of its members can lie in a bounded interval?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) is Corollary 4.3.3 with a sequence of irrationals. For (b), show that $\{x : t(x) \ge \epsilon\}$ meets any bounded interval in only finitely many points, then choose $\delta$ smaller than the distance from $c$ to the nearest of them.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $c = m/n$ be rational, so $t(c) = 1/n > 0$. Choose irrationals $x_k \to c$ with $x_k \ne c$, for instance $x_k = c + \sqrt{2}/k$. Then $t(x_k) = 0$ for every $k$, so $t(x_k) \to 0 \ne t(c)$, and Corollary 4.3.3 applies.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Fix irrational $c$ and $\epsilon > 0$. If $t(x) \ge \epsilon$ then $x$ is rational with denominator $n \le 1/\epsilon$ in lowest terms. Within the interval $(c-1, c+1)$ there are only finitely many rationals with denominator at most $1/\epsilon$ — at most $2 \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil$ of them for each admissible denominator, and finitely many admissible denominators. None equals $c$, since $c$ is irrational.

So let $\delta$ be smaller than $1$ and smaller than the distance from $c$ to the nearest of those finitely many points. Then every $x \in V_\delta(c)$ has $t(x) < \epsilon$, and since $t(c) = 0$ this says $|t(x) - t(c)| < \epsilon$. Hence $t$ is continuous at $c$.

Note what this function settles. Continuity is a genuinely pointwise property: $t$ is continuous on an uncountable dense set and discontinuous on a countable dense set, and both sets are dense in every interval. Any intuition that continuity should be a property of regions rather than points does not survive this example.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium-hard — composition, twice)

(a) Prove Theorem 4.3.9, that a composition of continuous functions is continuous, using the $\epsilon$-$\delta$ characterisation.
(b) Prove it again using the sequential characterisation, Theorem 4.3.2(iv).

*(Abbott §4.3, Exercise 4.3.2, p. 113)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In (a) the $\delta$ produced by the outer function's continuity becomes the $\epsilon$ handed to the inner one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Set up (a) by applying continuity of $g$ at $f(c)$ first, even though $f$ is applied first — the proof runs backwards along the composition. In (b), apply Theorem 4.3.2(iv) twice, once to each function.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $\epsilon > 0$. Continuity of $g$ at $f(c)$ gives $\gamma > 0$ such that $|y - f(c)| < \gamma$ and $y \in B$ imply $|g(y) - g(f(c))| < \epsilon$. Continuity of $f$ at $c$, applied with the challenge $\gamma$, gives $\delta > 0$ such that $|x - c| < \delta$ and $x \in A$ imply $|f(x) - f(c)| < \gamma$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Chaining these, $|x - c| < \delta$ with $x \in A$ gives $f(x) \in B$ with $|f(x) - f(c)| < \gamma$, hence $|g(f(x)) - g(f(c))| < \epsilon$. So $g \circ f$ is continuous at $c$.

(b) Let $(x_n) \to c$ with $x_n \in A$. By Theorem 4.3.2(iv) applied to $f$, $f(x_n) \to f(c)$; this is a sequence in $B$ converging to $f(c) \in B$, so the same theorem applied to $g$ gives $g(f(x_n)) \to g(f(c))$. By Theorem 4.3.2(iv) once more, now in the reverse direction, $g \circ f$ is continuous at $c$.

Compare the two proofs in a closing sentence. The second is three lines and the first is eight, and the reason is that all of the index bookkeeping in (a) was done once and for all when Theorem 4.3.2 was proved. That is the return on establishing an equivalence: you pay once and spend the sequential form repeatedly.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the topological characterisation)

Let $g$ be defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and for $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ write $g^{-1}(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : g(x) \in A\}$. Show that $g$ is continuous if and only if $g^{-1}(O)$ is open whenever $O \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is open.

*(Abbott §4.4, Exercise 4.4.11, p. 120)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Both directions are Theorem 4.3.2(iii) rewritten. Recall an-07's Definition 3.2.1: $O$ is open exactly when every point of it has a neighbourhood inside $O$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For the forward direction, take $c \in g^{-1}(O)$, find an $\epsilon$-neighbourhood of $g(c)$ inside $O$, and pull it back. For the converse, given $c$ and $\epsilon$, apply the hypothesis to the open set $V_\epsilon(g(c))$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
($\Rightarrow$) Let $O$ be open and $c \in g^{-1}(O)$, so $g(c) \in O$. Openness of $O$ gives $\epsilon > 0$ with $V_\epsilon(g(c)) \subseteq O$. Continuity at $c$, in the form of Theorem 4.3.2(iii), gives $\delta > 0$ with $g(V_\delta(c)) \subseteq V_\epsilon(g(c)) \subseteq O$, which says $V_\delta(c) \subseteq g^{-1}(O)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $c$ was an arbitrary point of $g^{-1}(O)$, every point of it has a neighbourhood inside it, so $g^{-1}(O)$ is open by Definition 3.2.1.

($\Leftarrow$) Fix $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\epsilon > 0$. The set $V_\epsilon(g(c))$ is open, so by hypothesis $U = g^{-1}(V_\epsilon(g(c)))$ is open; and $c \in U$ because $g(c) \in V_\epsilon(g(c))$. Openness of $U$ gives $\delta > 0$ with $V_\delta(c) \subseteq U$, which unpacks to: $|x - c| < \delta$ implies $|g(x) - g(c)| < \epsilon$. That is Definition 4.3.1 at $c$, and $c$ was arbitrary.

Finish with the observation the unit is aimed at. Neither direction used a single property of $\mathbb{R}$ beyond the definition of an open set — no completeness, no order, no arithmetic. So the statement transfers unchanged to any setting in which "open" makes sense, and in Semester 2 it is not a theorem about continuous functions but the definition of one. Note also that the hypothesis "defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$" is doing real work: for a function on a smaller domain $A$, the correct statement asks that $g^{-1}(O)$ be open *relative to* $A$, and getting that right is the subtlety the general definition has to carry.
</details>
