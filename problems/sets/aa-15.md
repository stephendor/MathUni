## Problem 1 (easy–medium — five modules, one definition)

Verify each of the following against Definition 8.1 (p. 154), saying which clause each check corresponds to.

(a) $k^n$ over a field $k$ — Example 8.3 (p. 155).

(b) $R$ over itself, with the ring multiplication as the action — Example 8.4 (p. 155).

(c) An ideal $I\subseteq R$, over $R$ — Example 8.5 (p. 155).

(d) $R/I$ over $R$ — Example 8.7 (p. 155).

(e) $S$ over $R$, given a ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$, with $r\cdot s := f(r)s$ — Example 8.6 (p. 155).

(f) Then check that (d) is the special case of (e) taking $f$ to be the projection $\pi : R\to R/I$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The four additive clauses always say that $(M,+)$ is an abelian group; the four action clauses say the action distributes both ways, is associative with ring multiplication, and that $1_R$ acts as the identity.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(e) Each clause reduces to a clause about $S$ plus one property of $f$. Which clause of Definition 4.15 (p. 64) does $1_R\cdot s = s$ need?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The four additive clauses are the additive part of Definition 3.1 (p. 41); associativity of the action is associativity of ring multiplication; the two distributive laws are the ring's; and $1_R\cdot r = r$ is the multiplicative identity clause.

(c) An ideal is closed under addition and negation and absorbs multiplication by $R$ — which is precisely closure of the action. The remaining clauses are inherited from $R$, exactly as in aa-07 Problem 3(b): equations restrict, existence statements do not, and absorption supplies the one existence statement needed.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(e) $r\cdot(s+s') = f(r)(s+s') = f(r)s + f(r)s'$ by distributivity in $S$; $(r+r')\cdot s = f(r+r')s = (f(r)+f(r'))s$ using that $f$ respects addition; $(rr')\cdot s = f(rr')s = f(r)f(r')s = r\cdot(r'\cdot s)$ using that $f$ respects multiplication. And $1_R\cdot s = f(1_R)s = 1_Ss = s$ — **which needs the third clause of Definition 4.15**, the one aa-08 Problem 2(c) showed cannot be derived. Without it this construction would not produce a module.

(f) $\pi$ is a ring homomorphism by Proposition 5.35 (p. 89), and $r\cdot(a+I) = \pi(r)(a+I) = ra+I$ is the action of Example 8.7. So (d) is (e) with $S = R/I$, $f = \pi$. This is worth noticing because it means the module structure on a quotient ring is not a new construction — it is the general one applied to a map already in hand.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the facts about zero that are not axioms, again)

Let $M$ be an $R$-module.

(a) Prove Proposition 8.9 (p. 156): $0_R\cdot m = 0_M$ for every $m\in M$.

(b) Prove $r\cdot 0_M = 0_M$ for every $r\in R$.

(c) Prove $(-r)\cdot m = -(r\cdot m) = r\cdot(-m)$.

(d) Compare this problem with aa-05 Problem 3. What is the same, and what is different about which distributive law does the work?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $0_R\cdot m = (0_R+0_R)\cdot m$, then cancel in $(M,+)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Cancellation in $M$ is available because $(M,+)$ is an abelian group — every element has an additive inverse by clause (iv).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $0_R\cdot m = (0_R+0_R)\cdot m = 0_R\cdot m + 0_R\cdot m$, using the distributive law over addition *in $R$*. Adding $-(0_R\cdot m)$ to both sides gives $0_M = 0_R\cdot m$.

(b) $r\cdot 0_M = r\cdot(0_M+0_M) = r\cdot0_M + r\cdot0_M$, using the distributive law over addition *in $M$*; cancel as before.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $r\cdot m + (-r)\cdot m = (r + (-r))\cdot m = 0_R\cdot m = 0_M$ by (a), so $(-r)m$ is an additive inverse of $rm$, and inverses in an abelian group are unique. The other equality uses (b) the same way.

(d) The shape is identical: a statement about zero that the axioms do not list, proved from distributivity plus cancellation, and worth proving once so that it never has to be checked again. What is different is that a module has **two** distributive laws over two different additions, and (a) and (b) use one each. In a ring both statements are the single fact $0\cdot a = a\cdot 0 = 0$ because there is only one addition; here they are genuinely two facts about two structures, and a proof of one is not a proof of the other. That is the first place where module axioms are not merely ring axioms with new names.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — a ℤ-module is an abelian group and nothing more)

(a) State Definition 8.11 (p. 157) and check that $(R,+)$ is an abelian group for any ring $R$ — Example 8.12 (p. 157).

(b) Prove Proposition 8.10 (p. 156): an abelian group $(A,+)$ carries **exactly one** $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure, given by $n\cdot a = a + \cdots + a$.

(c) Conclude that "$\mathbb{Z}$-module" and "abelian group" name the same objects.

(d) Compare with aa-08 Problem 3(a), which said there is exactly one *ring* homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to R$. State the common principle.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Uniqueness first: the clause $1\cdot a = a$ and the distributive law over addition in $\mathbb{Z}$ force the value of $n\cdot a$ for every $n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Existence is then a check that the forced formula satisfies all four action clauses, and it is the same induction as aa-07 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) An abelian group is a set with an associative, commutative addition having an identity and inverses — clauses (i)–(iv) of Definition 8.1 with no ring in sight. $(R,+)$ satisfies exactly those four clauses of Definition 3.1 (p. 41).

(b) *Uniqueness.* Any $\mathbb{Z}$-action has $1\cdot a = a$, and $(n+1)\cdot a = n\cdot a + 1\cdot a$, so by induction $n\cdot a$ is the $n$-fold sum for $n\ge0$; and $(-n)\cdot a = -(n\cdot a)$ by Problem 2(c). So the action is determined on all of $\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) *Existence.* Define $n\cdot a$ by that formula. The two distributive laws and associativity are the statements $(m+n)a = ma+na$, $n(a+b) = na+nb$ and $(mn)a = m(na)$, each an induction; commutativity of $+$ in $A$ is what makes the second one true. And $1\cdot a = a$ by definition.

(c) An abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module by (b), and a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is an abelian group by forgetting the action — Example 8.13 (p. 157). Since the action is *uniquely determined* by the group, nothing is lost in either direction. Had the action merely existed, the two notions would differ: there would be groups carrying several actions, and a $\mathbb{Z}$-module would be a group plus a choice.

(d) In both cases $\mathbb{Z}$ is the *initial* object: there is exactly one structure-preserving map out of it into anything, because $1$ must go to $1$ (rings) or because $1\cdot a = a$ is an axiom (modules), and addition then forces everything else. That is the same sentence twice, and it is why aa-21 can study abelian groups by studying $\mathbb{Z}$-modules without any translation.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — maps of modules)

(a) State Definition 8.14 (p. 158) and prove that an $R$-module homomorphism $f$ satisfies $f(0_M) = 0_N$ and $f(-m) = -f(m)$.

(b) Prove Example 8.18 (p. 159): an $R$-module homomorphism $f : R\to M$ is determined by $f(1_R)$, and for every $m\in M$ there is one with $f(1_R)=m$.

(c) Prove Example 8.16 (p. 159): if $R$ is commutative, then $\mu_r : M\to M$, $\mu_r(m) = rm$, is an $R$-module homomorphism.

(d) Show (c) can fail without commutativity: describe the property of $R$ that $\mu_r$ being $R$-linear requires.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) $f(r) = f(r\cdot 1) = r f(1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) The clause to check is $\mu_r(sm) = s\mu_r(m)$, i.e. $r(sm) = s(rm)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $f(0_M) = f(0_M+0_M) = f(0_M)+f(0_M)$, so $f(0_M)=0_N$ by cancellation; then $f(m)+f(-m) = f(0_M)=0_N$ identifies $f(-m)$ as the inverse of $f(m)$. Exactly Proposition 4.16 (p. 65) with modules in place of rings.

(b) If $f$ is a homomorphism then $f(r) = f(r\cdot 1_R) = r\cdot f(1_R)$, so $f$ is determined by $f(1_R)$. Conversely, given $m\in M$, define $f(r) = rm$; then $f(r+r') = (r+r')m = rm+r'm$ and $f(sr) = (sr)m = s(rm) = sf(r)$, so $f$ is a homomorphism with $f(1_R)=m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Additivity is the distributive law $r(m+m') = rm+rm'$. For $R$-linearity, $\mu_r(sm) = r(sm) = (rs)m$ and $s\mu_r(m) = s(rm) = (sr)m$; these agree because $rs = sr$.

(d) Without commutativity the two sides are $(rs)m$ and $(sr)m$, and $\mu_r$ is $R$-linear exactly when $(rs-sr)m = 0$ for all $s\in R$ and $m\in M$ — for this to hold for every module, $r$ must commute with every element of $R$, i.e. $r$ must lie in the **centre** of $R$. So multiplication by a general element is only additive, and the map $R\to\operatorname{End}(M)$ that (c) suggests is a map of rings only from the centre. This is the second place where modules are not rings with new names: the scalars have to commute past each other for scaling to be a map of modules at all.

Note that (b) contrasts sharply with rings. There is exactly one *ring* homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to R$, and exactly one $R\to R$ fixing $1$; but the $R$-module homomorphisms $R\to M$ are in bijection with the elements of $M$, since $f(1)$ is free. A ring homomorphism must preserve $1$; a module homomorphism has no $1$ to preserve.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — isomorphism, and the ring is part of the data)

(a) Prove that a bijective $R$-module homomorphism has an inverse that is again one, so Definition 8.20's two formulations (p. 160) agree.

(b) Prove $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.

(c) Prove $\mathbb{Z}\not\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules for any $n\ge1$.

(d) Example 8.8 (p. 155) insists that $R$ is part of the data. Illustrate: describe two genuinely different module structures on the same set $\mathbb{R}$, and give a property that distinguishes them.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Is the same argument as aa-08 Problem 4(a), with one clause fewer to check.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Look for a property preserved by isomorphism: is there a nonzero element killed by some positive integer?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $f : M\to N$ be a bijective homomorphism and $n,n'\in N$. Writing $n = f(m)$, $n' = f(m')$: $f^{-1}(n+n') = f^{-1}(f(m+m')) = m+m' = f^{-1}(n)+f^{-1}(n')$, and $f^{-1}(rn) = f^{-1}(f(rm)) = rm = rf^{-1}(n)$. There is no third clause to check, unlike the ring case.

(b) The map $[a]_6\mapsto([a]_2,[a]_3)$ is well defined, additive, and $\mathbb{Z}$-linear automatically since every additive map between $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is $\mathbb{Z}$-linear by Problem 3(b). It is bijective by counting and injectivity, or by aa-10 Problem 5 — this is the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and here it is an isomorphism of modules as well as of rings.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ every element $m$ satisfies $n\cdot m = 0$. In $\mathbb{Z}$, only $0$ satisfies $n\cdot m = 0$. An isomorphism $f$ would give $n\cdot f(1) = f(n\cdot 1) = f(n)$, and $f$ injective with $f(0)=0$ forces $n = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ — false. So no isomorphism exists. The invariant used is "some positive integer kills every element", which is preserved by any isomorphism because it is written in the module operations alone.

(d) Take the set $\mathbb{R}$. As an $\mathbb{R}$-module it is generated by the single element $1$: every real is $r\cdot 1$. As a $\mathbb{Q}$-module it is not finitely generated — a finite set generates only a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-subspace, which is countable, and $\mathbb{R}$ is not. So "cyclic" holds for one structure and fails for the other on the same underlying set with the same addition. What changed is only which ring is allowed to act, and that is the whole of Example 8.8's point: $M$ alone is not a module, the pair $(R, M)$ is.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — counting maps, and how differently modules and rings behave)

(a) Show that the submodules of $R$, regarded as an $R$-module, are exactly the ideals of $R$ — Example 8.5 (p. 155).

(b) Let $m,n\ge1$. Prove that the $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are in bijection with $\{a\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} : m\cdot a = 0\}$, and that there are exactly $\gcd(m,n)$ of them.

(c) Count both, for $m = 12$, $n = 8$: the module homomorphisms and the **ring** homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$.

(d) Explain the discrepancy in one sentence, referring to the clause of Definition 4.15 (p. 64) that Definition 8.14 (p. 158) has no analogue of.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) A homomorphism out of $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is an additive map determined by the image $a$ of $[1]_m$, and the only constraint is that $[1]_m$'s relation $m[1]_m = 0$ must survive.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $\{a : ma = 0\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ consists of the multiples of $n/\gcd(m,n)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A submodule of $R$ is a subset closed under addition, containing $0$, closed under negation, and closed under multiplication by every $r\in R$ — which is Proposition 5.9 (p. 81)'s characterisation of an ideal. Proposition 8.23 (p. 161) is the general submodule criterion and specialises to it.

(b) A $\mathbb{Z}$-module map $f$ is determined by $a = f([1]_m)$, since $f([k]_m) = k\cdot a$. Such an $f$ is well defined precisely when $[k]_m = [k']_m$ implies $ka = k'a$, i.e. when $m\cdot a = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $m\cdot a = 0$ means $n \mid ma$. Writing $d = \gcd(m,n)$, this says $\tfrac nd \mid \tfrac md a$, and $\gcd(\tfrac nd,\tfrac md)=1$, so $\tfrac nd\mid a$ — by aa-02 Problem 3(a). The multiples of $n/d$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ number exactly $d$. So there are $\gcd(m,n)$ homomorphisms.

(c) Module homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$: $\gcd(12,8) = 4$ of them, sent by $[1]_{12}\mapsto[0],[2],[4],[6]$. Ring homomorphisms: **none**, by aa-08 Problem 3(c), since $8\nmid12$.

(d) Definition 4.15 requires $f(1_R)=1_S$ and Definition 8.14 requires nothing of the kind — a module has no distinguished element to preserve — so the ring condition pins $f([1]_{12})$ to $[1]_8$, which fails the relation $12\cdot[1]_8 = [4]_8 \ne 0$, while the module condition merely asks that the image satisfy that relation, and four elements of $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ do.
</details>
