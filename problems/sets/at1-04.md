## Problem 1 (easy — lifting, by hand)

Let $p : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ be $p(s) = (\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s)$, and let
$\omega_n(s) = (\cos 2\pi n s, \sin 2\pi n s)$.

(a) Show that $\widetilde{\omega}_n(s) = ns$ is a lift of $\omega_n$ starting at
$0$, i.e. that $p\widetilde{\omega}_n = \omega_n$ and $\widetilde{\omega}_n(0) = 0$.
(b) Compute $p^{-1}(x_0)$ for the basepoint $x_0 = (1,0)$.
(c) Verify directly that every open arc in $S^1$ is evenly covered by $p$:
given an arc $U$, describe $p^{-1}(U)$ as a disjoint union of open sets each
mapped homeomorphically onto $U$.
(d) Explain why $U = S^1$ itself is *not* evenly covered, and why the
definition of a covering space therefore has to be local.

*(Hatcher §1.1, p. 29)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), an arc is $p(J)$ for an open interval $J$ of length less than $1$; the
preimage is the union of the translates $J + k$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Everything is the periodicity of cosine and sine with period $1$ in this
parametrisation. For (d), ask whether $p^{-1}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ can be written
as a disjoint union of open sets each homeomorphic to $S^1$ via $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $p^{-1}(x_0) = \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
(d) $\mathbb{R}$ is connected, so it is not a disjoint union of two or more
non-empty open sets; and $\mathbb{R}$ itself is not homeomorphic to $S^1$ (one
is compact, the other is not).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $p(\widetilde{\omega}_n(s)) = p(ns) = (\cos 2\pi n s, \sin 2\pi n s) = \omega_n(s)$,
and $\widetilde{\omega}_n(0) = 0$. Geometrically, embedding $\mathbb{R}$ in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ as the helix $s \mapsto (\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s, s)$ makes $p$
the restriction of the projection $(x,y,z) \mapsto (x,y)$, and
$\widetilde{\omega}_n$ winds $|n|$ times up the helix if $n > 0$ and down if
$n < 0$.

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the proof of Theorem 1.7, granted the lifting facts)

Assume the two lifting facts Hatcher isolates:

- (a) for each path $f : I \to X$ starting at $x_0$ and each
  $\tilde{x}_0 \in p^{-1}(x_0)$ there is a unique lift $\tilde{f}$ starting at
  $\tilde{x}_0$;
- (b) for each homotopy $f_t$ of paths starting at $x_0$ and each such
  $\tilde{x}_0$ there is a unique lifted homotopy $\tilde{f}_t$ of paths
  starting at $\tilde{x}_0$.

(a) *Surjectivity of $n \mapsto [\omega_n]$.* Let $f$ be a loop at $x_0$. Lift
it to $\tilde{f}$ starting at $0$ and explain why $\tilde{f}(1)$ is an integer
$n$. Then produce a homotopy $f \simeq \omega_n$ and say which earlier result
lets you push a homotopy in $\mathbb{R}$ down to one in $S^1$.
(b) *Injectivity.* Suppose $\omega_m \simeq \omega_n$. Lift the homotopy using
fact (b) and use the uniqueness clause of (a) to identify the two ends. Then
deduce $m = n$ from the one property of a homotopy *of paths* that has been
doing the work since at1-03.
(c) Conclude that $\pi_1(S^1)$ is infinite cyclic generated by $[\omega]$, and
verify the auxiliary claim $[\omega]^n = [\omega_n]$.
(d) State precisely where the argument would break if fact (b) gave a lifted
homotopy that was merely a homotopy, not a homotopy *of paths*.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Theorem 1.7 and its proof, pp. 29–30)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$p\tilde{f}(1) = f(1) = x_0$, and you computed $p^{-1}(x_0)$ in Problem 1.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The whole proof is: a loop downstairs becomes a path upstairs from $0$ to some
integer; $\mathbb{R}$ is convex so any two paths with the same endpoints are
homotopic there (at1-03, Example 1.1); push down. Uniqueness runs the same
machine backwards, and the punchline is that $\tilde{f}_t(1)$ cannot move
because clause (1) of a homotopy of paths forbids it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\tilde{f} \simeq \widetilde{\omega}_n$ via the linear homotopy
$(1-t)\tilde{f} + t\widetilde{\omega}_n$; composing with $p$ gives
$f \simeq \omega_n$.
(b) $\tilde{f}_t(1)$ is independent of $t$, equals $m$ at $t=0$ and $n$ at
$t=1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By fact (a) there is a lift $\tilde{f} : I \to \mathbb{R}$ starting at $0$.
Since $p\tilde{f}(1) = f(1) = x_0$ and $p^{-1}(x_0) = \mathbb{Z}$, the endpoint
$\tilde{f}(1)$ is some integer $n$. Another path in $\mathbb{R}$ from $0$ to
$n$ is $\widetilde{\omega}_n$, and the two are homotopic rel endpoints by the
linear homotopy $(1-t)\tilde{f} + t\widetilde{\omega}_n$ — legitimate because
$\mathbb{R}$ is convex, which is at1-03's Example 1.1. ⟨your step: compose with
$p$ and say why the result is still a homotopy of paths⟩

(b) ⟨your step⟩ (c) ⟨your step⟩ (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra)

Theorem 1.8: every nonconstant polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$ has
a root in $\mathbb{C}$.

(a) Assume $p(z) = z^n + a_1 z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n$ has no roots. Verify that
$$f_r(s) = \frac{p(re^{2\pi i s})/p(r)}{\lvert p(re^{2\pi i s})/p(r)\rvert}$$
is a well-defined loop in $S^1$ based at $1$, for each $r \geq 0$. Say exactly
where "no roots" is used.
(b) Show that as $r$ varies, $f_r$ is a homotopy of loops based at $1$, and
deduce $[f_r] = 0$ in $\pi_1(S^1)$ for every $r$ by evaluating at $r = 0$.
(c) Fix $r$ bigger than $|a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|$ and bigger than $1$. Prove
Hatcher's chain of inequalities for $|z| = r$:
$$|z^n| > (|a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|)|z^{n-1}| \geq |a_1 z^{n-1}| + \cdots + |a_n|
\geq |a_1 z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n|,$$
justifying each step. (Take care with the second: it is where $r > 1$ is used.)
(d) Deduce that $p_t(z) = z^n + t(a_1 z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n)$ has no roots on
$|z| = r$ for $t \in [0,1]$, obtain a homotopy from $f_r$ to $\omega_n$, and
conclude $n = 0$ using Theorem 1.7.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Theorem 1.8, p. 31)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), $f_0$ is the constant loop at $1$: substitute $r = 0$ into the formula.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The shape of the argument is: one homotopy shows the class is $0$; a second
homotopy shows the class is $n$ times a generator; Theorem 1.7 says the
generator has infinite order, so $n = 0$. Everything else is checking that the
denominators never vanish.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Both $p(re^{2\pi i s})$ and $p(r)$ are non-zero because $p$ has no roots,
so the quotient is defined and non-zero, and dividing by its modulus lands in
$S^1$. At $s = 0$ and $s = 1$ the value is $p(r)/p(r)$ normalised, i.e. $1$.
(c) The second inequality uses $|z|^{n-1} = r^{n-1} \geq 1$ term by term; the
third is the triangle inequality.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) With $|z| = r > |a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|$ and $r > 1$:

*First inequality.* $|z^n| = r \cdot r^{n-1} > (|a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|) r^{n-1}
= (|a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|)|z^{n-1}|$, using $r > |a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|$.

*Second inequality.* $(|a_1| + \cdots + |a_n|)r^{n-1}
= |a_1|r^{n-1} + |a_2|r^{n-1} + \cdots + |a_n|r^{n-1}$, and since $r > 1$ we
have $r^{n-1} \geq r^{n-2} \geq \cdots \geq r^0 = 1$, so each term dominates
the corresponding $|a_k|r^{n-k}$. Hence the sum is at least
$|a_1 z^{n-1}| + \cdots + |a_n|$. ⟨your step: write out the term-by-term
comparison⟩

*Third inequality.* ⟨your step⟩

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — Brouwer, and the retraction at1-01 promised)

Theorem 1.9: every continuous map $h : D^2 \to D^2$ has a fixed point.

(a) Suppose $h(x) \neq x$ for all $x$. Define $r(x)$ to be the point of $S^1$
where the ray in $\mathbb{R}^2$ starting at $h(x)$ and passing through $x$
leaves $D^2$. Explain why $r$ is well defined (this is where $h(x) \neq x$ is
used) and why it is continuous.
(b) Show that $r(x) = x$ for $x \in S^1$, so $r$ is a retraction of $D^2$ onto
$S^1$ in the sense of at1-01.
(c) Prove that no retraction $r : D^2 \to S^1$ exists: take any loop $f_0$ in
$S^1$, use the linear homotopy in $D^2$ to a constant, apply $r$, and derive a
contradiction with Theorem 1.7.
(d) In at1-01 you proved that $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ deformation
retracts onto $S^{n-1}$, and the lesson remarked that no map gives a
deformation retraction of the disk onto its boundary sphere, deferring the
proof to this unit. You have now proved something stronger for $n = 2$. State
what, and explain the gap between "no retraction" and "no deformation
retraction" — which of the two is the stronger conclusion, and why does (c)
give it?

*(Hatcher §1.1, Theorem 1.9 and its proof, pp. 31–32)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (d): a deformation retraction yields a retraction (take $t = 1$), so ruling
out all retractions rules out all deformation retractions too.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) is the cleanest argument in the section. In $D^2$ every loop is
nullhomotopic because $D^2$ is convex (at1-03, Example 1.4). Applying $r$
transports that nullhomotopy into $S^1$, where it cannot exist. Note the
argument uses only that $\pi_1(S^1) \neq 0$, not the full strength of
Theorem 1.7.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $h(x) = x$ the ray is undefined, having no direction. Continuity holds
because small perturbations of $x$ produce small perturbations of $h(x)$, hence
of the ray.
(c) $rf_t$ is a homotopy in $S^1$ from $rf_0 = f_0$ to the constant loop at
$x_0$, so every loop in $S^1$ is nullhomotopic and $\pi_1(S^1) = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Suppose $r : D^2 \to S^1$ is a retraction, so $r$ is continuous and
$r|S^1 = \mathbb{1}$. Let $f_0$ be any loop in $S^1$, based at $x_0$. Regard it
as a loop in $D^2$. Since $D^2$ is convex, the linear homotopy
$f_t(s) = (1-t)f_0(s) + t x_0$ is a homotopy of loops in $D^2$ from $f_0$ to
the constant loop at $x_0$ (at1-03, Examples 1.1 and 1.4).

Now compose with $r$. Because $r$ is continuous, $r f_t$ is a homotopy of loops
in $S^1$; because $r$ is the identity on $S^1$ and $f_0$ has image in $S^1$, we
have $rf_0 = f_0$; and $rf_1$ is the constant loop at $r(x_0) = x_0$.
⟨your step: conclude that every element of $\pi_1(S^1, x_0)$ is trivial, and
name the contradiction⟩

</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — Borsuk–Ulam, and the limits of what π₁ can prove)

Theorem 1.10: for every continuous map $f : S^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ there exists
a pair of antipodal points $x$ and $-x$ in $S^2$ with $f(x) = f(-x)$.

(a) Prove the case $n = 1$ that Hatcher calls easy: for every continuous
$f : S^1 \to \mathbb{R}$ there is an $x$ with $f(x) = f(-x)$. Use the
intermediate value theorem on $g(x) = f(x) - f(-x)$, and say why $g$ changes
sign.
(b) Corollary 1.11 states that whenever $S^2$ is expressed as the union of
three closed sets, at least one of them must contain a pair of antipodal
points. Deduce it from Theorem 1.10. (Hint: for closed sets $A_1, A_2$ define
$f(x) = (d(x, A_1), d(x, A_2))$ using the distance function, and consider the
cases $f(x) = f(-x)$ with each coordinate zero or non-zero.)
(c) Hatcher records that Brouwer's theorem holds for $D^n$ and Borsuk–Ulam for
maps $S^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, obtained as Corollary 2.15 and Corollary 2B.7
respectively, "using homology groups in place of $\pi_1$". Explain why $\pi_1$
alone cannot reach the general case: what is $\pi_1(S^n)$ for $n \geq 2$, and
why does that make the Problem 4 argument collapse in higher dimensions?
(You may take the value of $\pi_1(S^n)$ from at1-05, where van Kampen supplies
it; state it as a forward citation rather than proving it.)
(d) Hatcher observes that these proofs "show just the existence of fixed points
without giving any clue as to how to find one in explicit cases", and says the
same of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra proof. Identify the step in your
Problem 3 and Problem 4 solutions that makes each argument non-constructive,
and state in one sentence what a constructive proof would have to supply
instead.

*(Hatcher §1.1, Theorem 1.10 and Corollary 1.11, pp. 32–33; the remarks on
Corollary 2.15, Corollary 2B.7 and non-constructiveness, p. 32)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) For $n \geq 2$ the sphere $S^n$ is simply-connected, so the contradiction
in Problem 4(c) — that $\pi_1$ of the boundary sphere is non-zero — is simply
false.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) is a one-line application of the intermediate value theorem once you notice
$g(-x) = -g(x)$. For (d), look for the phrase "suppose on the contrary" and for
the appeal to a homotopy class being zero: both establish that something exists
by ruling out its absence, which locates nothing.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $g(-x) = f(-x) - f(x) = -g(x)$, so if $g(x_1) > 0$ at some point then
$g(-x_1) < 0$; $S^1$ is connected and $g$ continuous, so $g$ vanishes somewhere.
(c) $\pi_1(S^n) = 0$ for $n \geq 2$ (at1-05), so "this contradicts the fact
that $\pi_1(S^{n-1})$ is nonzero" has no force once $n - 1 \geq 2$.
</details>
