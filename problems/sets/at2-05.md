## Problem 1 (easy — tensor products by the rules) [interleaves aa-15]

Using only properties (1)–(5) of the tensor product as listed in §3.2, compute:

(a) $\mathbb{Z} \otimes \mathbb{Z}_5$ and $\mathbb{Z}_5 \otimes \mathbb{Z}$;
(b) $\mathbb{Z}_4 \otimes \mathbb{Z}_6$;
(c) $(\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_3) \otimes (\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_6)$;
(d) $\mathbb{Z}_m \otimes \mathbb{Z}_n$ in general, in terms of $m$ and $n$.

Then state which properties you used at each step, and explain why property (2)
is the one that makes finitely generated computations mechanical.

*(Hatcher §3.2, tensor product properties (1)–(5), p. 215)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Property (5) is $\mathbb{Z}_n \otimes A \cong A/nA$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Reduce every question to a sum of cyclic-times-cyclic pieces using (2), then hit
each piece with (4) or (5).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathbb{Z}_4 \otimes \mathbb{Z}_6 \cong \mathbb{Z}_6 / 4\mathbb{Z}_6 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By (4), $\mathbb{Z} \otimes \mathbb{Z}_5 \cong \mathbb{Z}_5$; by (1) the other
order gives the same.
(b) By (5), $\mathbb{Z}_4 \otimes \mathbb{Z}_6 \cong \mathbb{Z}_6/4\mathbb{Z}_6$;
and $4\mathbb{Z}_6 = \{0,4,2\} = 2\mathbb{Z}_6$ has order $3$, so the quotient is
$\mathbb{Z}_2$.
(c) By (2) applied twice, the answer is
$(\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}) \oplus (\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_6) \oplus (\mathbb{Z}_3\otimes\mathbb{Z}) \oplus (\mathbb{Z}_3\otimes\mathbb{Z}_6)$,
which by (4) and (5) is $\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_6 \oplus \mathbb{Z}_3 \oplus \mathbb{Z}_3$.
(d) $\mathbb{Z}_m \otimes \mathbb{Z}_n \cong \mathbb{Z}_n/m\mathbb{Z}_n \cong \mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(m,n)}$,
since $m\mathbb{Z}_n = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}_n$ has index $\gcd(m,n)$.
Property (2) — distributivity over direct sums — is what lets a finitely
generated group be broken into cyclic summands (aa-20) and handled one summand
at a time; without it there would be no reduction to (4) and (5).
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — why the cross product needs a tensor product)

(a) Define the cross product $H^*(X;R) \times H^*(Y;R) \to H^*(X \times Y;R)$
and prove it is bilinear, saying which property of cup product you used.
(b) Explain in two or three sentences why a bilinear map $A \times B \to C$ is
almost never a group homomorphism, giving a concrete failure with
$A = B = C = \mathbb{Z}$.
(c) State property (7) of tensor products and use it to produce the map
$H^*(X;R) \otimes_R H^*(Y;R) \to H^*(X \times Y;R)$.
(d) Why is $A \otimes_R B$ generally different from $A \otimes B$, and for which
rings $R$ does §3.2 say they coincide?

*(Hatcher §3.2, pp. 214–216)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$a \times b = p_1^*(a) \smile p_2^*(b)$ where $p_1, p_2$ are the projections.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b) test additivity in *both* variables at once: a bilinear map sends
$(a+a', b+b')$ to a sum of four terms, a homomorphism to a sum of two.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Multiplication $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is bilinear but
$(1+1)(1+1) = 4 \neq 1 + 1 = 2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $a \times b = p_1^*(a) \smile p_2^*(b)$. Cup product is distributive and
$p_i^*$ is additive, so $(a + a') \times b = a \times b + a' \times b$ and
similarly in the second variable: the cross product is linear in each variable
separately.
(b) A homomorphism out of $A \times B$ must satisfy
$\varphi(a+a', b+b') = \varphi(a,b) + \varphi(a',b')$, whereas bilinearity
expands the left side into four terms. Concretely, multiplication
$\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ is bilinear, yet
$\varphi((1,1)+(1,1)) = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$ while
$\varphi(1,1)+\varphi(1,1) = 2$.
(c) Property (7): a bilinear map $\varphi : A \times B \to C$ induces a
homomorphism $A \otimes B \to C$ with $a \otimes b \mapsto \varphi(a,b)$.
Applying it to the cross product gives the $R$-module homomorphism
$H^*(X;R)\otimes_R H^*(Y;R) \to H^*(X\times Y;R)$, $a \otimes b \mapsto a \times b$.
(d) $A \otimes_R B$ is the quotient of $A \otimes B$ by the further relations
$ra \otimes b = a \otimes rb$, so it is generally smaller. §3.2 notes they agree
when $R$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}_m$, and also when $R$ is $\mathbb{Q}$ or
any subring of $\mathbb{Q}$; the example $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ shows they
can differ, with $R \otimes_R R = R$ of rank $2$ but $R \otimes R$ free of rank $4$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the sign in the graded tensor product)

The multiplication on a tensor product of graded rings is defined by
$(a \otimes b)(c \otimes d) = (-1)^{|b||c|} ac \otimes bd$.

(a) Reproduce Hatcher's verification that the cross product $\mu$ is then a ring
homomorphism, giving each equality of the chain and naming the result used.
(b) Identify the single step at which Theorem 3.11 is used, and say exactly
which two classes are being commuted past each other.
(c) Show that without the sign $(-1)^{|b||c|}$ the map $\mu$ would fail to be
multiplicative, by exhibiting the discrepancy in general terms.
(d) Explain why $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^2)$
are isomorphic as rings but must be distinguished as *graded* rings, and what
goes wrong if you confuse them inside a tensor product.

*(Hatcher §3.2, the computation on p. 216 and Example 3.16, p. 217)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The chain of equalities moves $p_1^*(c)$ past $p_2^*(b)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Write out $\mu((a\otimes b)(c \otimes d))$ and $\mu(a\otimes b)\mu(c\otimes d)$
separately and compare; the sign appears on one side and must be produced on the
other by a commutation.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$p_1^*(c) \smile p_2^*(b) = (-1)^{|b||c|} p_2^*(b) \smile p_1^*(c)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $\mu((a\otimes b)(c\otimes d)) = (-1)^{|b||c|}\mu(ac \otimes bd) = (-1)^{|b||c|}(a \smile c)\times(b \smile d) = (-1)^{|b||c|} p_1^*(a\smile c) \smile p_2^*(b \smile d)$
by definition of the cross product; this equals
$(-1)^{|b||c|} p_1^*(a)\smile p_1^*(c) \smile p_2^*(b) \smile p_2^*(d)$
because $p_1^*$ and $p_2^*$ are ring homomorphisms (Proposition 3.10); commuting
$p_1^*(c)$ past $p_2^*(b)$ absorbs the sign, giving
$p_1^*(a)\smile p_2^*(b)\smile p_1^*(c)\smile p_2^*(d) = (a\times b)(c \times d) = \mu(a\otimes b)\mu(c\otimes d)$.
(b) At the commutation of $p_1^*(c)$ with $p_2^*(b)$: Theorem 3.11 supplies
$p_1^*(c)\smile p_2^*(b) = (-1)^{|b||c|}p_2^*(b) \smile p_1^*(c)$, since
$|p_1^*(c)| = |c|$ and $|p_2^*(b)| = |b|$.
(c) Without the sign in the definition, the left-hand side would lack the factor
$(-1)^{|b||c|}$ while the right-hand side still acquires it from the
commutation, so the two would differ by exactly $(-1)^{|b||c|}$ — equal only
when $|b||c|$ is even, or when $2 = 0$ in $R$.
(d) As ungraded rings both are $R \oplus R\alpha$ with $\alpha^2 = 0$. As graded
rings the generator is odd-dimensional in the exterior algebra and
even-dimensional in the truncated polynomial ring, and the tensor product
multiplication's sign $(-1)^{|b||c|}$ reads the dimension. Confusing them
changes the signs, hence changes which products commute and which
anticommute — and so gives the wrong ring for a product of spheres unless
$R = \mathbb{Z}_2$, where signs are irrelevant.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — computing cohomology rings of products)

Use Theorem 3.15 and Example 3.16 to compute the following as graded rings, and
list the ranks of $H^n$ in each case.

(a) $H^*(S^1 \times S^3; \mathbb{Z})$.
(b) $H^*(S^2 \times S^2; \mathbb{Z})$.
(c) $H^*(T^3;\mathbb{Z})$, where $T^3$ is the product of three circles.
(d) In (b), decide whether the square of every element of $H^2$ is zero, and
compare with what Theorem 3.11 alone would have told you.

For each, check that the freeness hypothesis of Theorem 3.15 actually holds.

*(Hatcher §3.2, Theorem 3.15 and Example 3.16, pp. 216–217)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$H^*(S^k;\mathbb{Z})$ is $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]$ with $|\alpha| = k$ for
$k$ odd, and $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^2)$ with $|\alpha| = k$ for $k$ even.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Tensor the factors' rings in the graded sense; the additive ranks are then the
products of the factors' ranks, read off the diagonal $i + j = n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\alpha_3]$ with $|\alpha_1| = 1$,
$|\alpha_3| = 3$; ranks $1, 1, 0, 1, 1$ in degrees $0$ to $4$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Both spheres are odd-dimensional, so by Example 3.16
$H^*(S^1\times S^3;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\alpha_3]$
with $|\alpha_1| = 1$, $|\alpha_3| = 3$. Additively: $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees
$0, 1, 3, 4$ (generated by $1, \alpha_1, \alpha_3, \alpha_1\alpha_3$) and $0$ in
degree $2$.
(b) $H^*(S^2;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^2)$ with $|\alpha| = 2$,
so the product ring is $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^2) \otimes \mathbb{Z}[\beta]/(\beta^2)$
with $|\alpha| = |\beta| = 2$: ranks $1, 0, 2, 0, 1$ in degrees $0$ to $4$, with
$H^2$ spanned by $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and $H^4$ by $\alpha\beta$.
(c) $H^*(T^3;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3]$
with all $|\alpha_i| = 1$; ranks $1, 3, 3, 1$.
(d) In (b) the generators have even dimension, so they commute strictly and
$\alpha^2 = 0$, $\beta^2 = 0$ come from the truncation, not from any sign. But
$(\alpha+\beta)^2 = \alpha^2 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2 = 2\alpha\beta \neq 0$, so
*not* every square in $H^2$ vanishes. Theorem 3.11 would have said nothing here:
it constrains squares only in odd dimensions, and $2$ is even.
Freeness holds in every case, each factor's cohomology being free of finite rank
in each degree.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — finishing the mission's example)

(a) Compute $H^*(S^2 \times S^1;\mathbb{Z})$ as a graded ring using
Theorem 3.15, and exhibit a non-zero product of a $1$-dimensional class with a
$2$-dimensional class.
(b) Compute $H^*(S^2 \vee S^1 \vee S^3;\mathbb{Z})$ as a graded ring using
Example 3.14, and show every product of positive-dimensional classes vanishes.
(c) Using Corollary 3.3 in reverse, deduce the integral homology groups of both
spaces from the cohomology you computed, and verify they agree. Say carefully
why the deduction is legitimate here and where it could fail.
(d) Conclude that the two spaces are not homotopy equivalent, and state the
general moral in one sentence: what does the cup product see that the sequence
of homology groups cannot?

*(Hatcher §3.2, Theorem 3.15 p. 216 and Example 3.14 pp. 213–214; Corollary 3.3, p. 196)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$S^2$ contributes $\mathbb{Z}[\beta]/(\beta^2)$ with $|\beta| = 2$ and $S^1$
contributes $\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]$ with $|\alpha| = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), all the cohomology is free, so by Corollary 3.3 there can be no torsion
in homology and the ranks transfer degree by degree — but argue it, do not
assume it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^*(S^2\times S^1;\mathbb{Z})$ has $\mathbb{Z}$ in each of degrees $0,1,2,3$,
spanned by $1, \alpha, \beta, \alpha\beta$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By Theorem 3.15,
$H^*(S^2\times S^1;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\beta]/(\beta^2) \otimes \Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha]$
with $|\beta| = 2$, $|\alpha| = 1$; the freeness hypothesis holds since
$H^k(S^1;\mathbb{Z})$ is free of finite rank for every $k$. Additively this is
$\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0,1,2,3$ with basis $1, \alpha, \beta, \alpha\beta$.
The product $\alpha \smile \beta = \alpha\beta$ is a generator of $H^3$, hence
non-zero.
(b) By Example 3.14,
$\tilde{H}^*(S^2 \vee S^1 \vee S^3;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \tilde{H}^*(S^2) \times \tilde{H}^*(S^1) \times \tilde{H}^*(S^3)$
as rings, with coordinatewise multiplication. A product of two
positive-dimensional classes is computed coordinatewise; within a single sphere
factor, the product of two positive-dimensional reduced classes lands in a
degree where that factor's reduced cohomology vanishes (each sphere has just one
non-zero reduced degree), and across factors the product is zero because each
element has a zero coordinate. So all such products vanish. Additively the ring
is $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0,1,2,3$ — the same as (a).
(c) Every $H^n$ computed is free of finite rank, and $H^n = 0$ for $n > 3$.
Corollary 3.3 gives $H^n \cong (H_n/T_n) \oplus T_{n-1}$. Inductively from the
top: $T_n = 0$ for large $n$; if $H^{n+1}$ is free then its summand $T_n$ must
vanish, so $T_n = 0$; then $H^n \cong H_n/T_n = H_n$ is free of the same rank.
Hence both spaces have $H_n \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for $n = 0,1,2,3$ and $0$
otherwise — identical homology. The deduction is legitimate because the homology
groups are finitely generated, which is what Corollary 3.3 requires; it would
fail without that hypothesis, and it would also fail to determine $H_n$ if some
$H^{n+1}$ had torsion, since then $T_n$ could be non-zero.
(d) The two spaces have isomorphic homology in every degree but non-isomorphic
cohomology rings, one having a non-zero product of positive-dimensional classes
and the other not; since a homotopy equivalence induces a graded ring
isomorphism (at2-04), they are not homotopy equivalent. The moral: homology
records how many holes there are in each dimension, while the cup product
records how those holes are linked — which of them intersect, in the geometric
reading of Example 3.7.
</details>
