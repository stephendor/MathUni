## Problem 1 (easy — is it a graded module?) [interleaves aa-15]

For each of the following, decide whether it is a $\mathbb{Z}^2$-graded
$R$-module in the sense of Definition 11.2, with $R = \mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$
(Definition 11.1). Justify each answer against the definition, clause by clause.

(a) $M_{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{k}$ for all $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{Z}^2$, with every
$t_i \bullet$ the identity map.
(b) $M_{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{k}$ for $\mathbf{u} \geq (0,0)$ and $0$ otherwise, with
$t_i \bullet$ the identity where both source and target are non-zero.
(c) $M_{(0,0)} = \mathbf{k}$, $M_{(1,0)} = M_{(0,1)} = \mathbf{k}$,
$M_{(1,1)} = \mathbf{k}$, all other grades $0$, with $t_1 \bullet$ the identity and
$t_2 \bullet$ the zero map.

*(Dey §11.1.1, Definition 11.2, p. 325)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 11.2 requires two things: a direct-sum decomposition indexed by $\mathbb{Z}^d$, and $t_i \cdot M_{\mathbf{u}} \subseteq M_{\mathbf{u}+e_i}$ for every $i$ and every $\mathbf{u}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The grade-shifting condition is automatic once you specify maps $t_i\bullet : M_{\mathbf{u}} \to M_{\mathbf{u}+e_i}$. The condition that is easy to miss is the commutativity $(t_j\bullet)\circ(t_i\bullet) = (t_i\bullet)\circ(t_j\bullet)$ that makes the ring action well defined.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
All three specify maps of the right shape. Check commutativity of the square at each grade — that is where a candidate can fail.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Definition 11.2 asks for $M = \bigoplus_{\mathbf{u}} M_{\mathbf{u}}$ with $t_i \cdot M_{\mathbf{u}} \subseteq M_{\mathbf{u}+e_i}$; equivalently a collection of linear maps $t_i \bullet$ satisfying $(t_j\bullet)\circ(t_i\bullet) = (t_i\bullet)\circ(t_j\bullet)$. (a) and (b) satisfy this — both composites round any square are the identity, respectively zero-or-identity consistently. For (c) compare the two routes from $M_{(0,0)}$ to $M_{(1,1)}$: via $t_1$ then $t_2$ gives $0$, via $t_2$ then $t_1$ gives $0$ as well, so it commutes and (c) is also a graded module. State in each case which clause you checked; the point of the exercise is that "assigns a vector space to each grid point" is not by itself enough.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — from a bifiltration to a module)

Let $X_{\mathbf{u}}$ be the two-parameter simplicial filtration on vertices
$\{a,b\}$ given by: $X_{(0,0)} = \{a\}$, $X_{(1,0)} = \{a,b\}$,
$X_{(0,1)} = \{a\}$, $X_{(1,1)} = \{a, b, ab\}$, with $X_{\mathbf{u}}$ constant
beyond these grades in each direction.

(a) Verify that this is a $2$-parameter simplicial filtration in the sense of
Definition 11.4.
(b) Write out the $0$-dimensional persistence module $M$ (Definition 11.5) it induces
over $\mathbf{k}$: give $\dim M_{\mathbf{u}}$ at each of the four grades and describe
each map $t_i \bullet$.
(c) Confirm that every square in your diagram commutes.

*(Dey §11.1.1, Definition 11.4 and Definition 11.5, pp. 326–327)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$H_0$ counts connected components; the maps are induced by inclusion of complexes.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Read off the components at each grade first, then ask what each inclusion does to them. The edge $ab$ appearing at $(1,1)$ is the only merge event.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\dim M_{(0,0)} = 1$, $\dim M_{(1,0)} = 2$, $\dim M_{(0,1)} = 1$, $\dim M_{(1,1)} = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 11.4 requires $X_{\mathbf{u}} \subseteq X_{\mathbf{u}+e_i}$ for each $i$ and each grade. Check the four inclusions: $\{a\} \subseteq \{a,b\}$, $\{a\} \subseteq \{a\}$, $\{a,b\} \subseteq \{a,b,ab\}$, $\{a\} \subseteq \{a,b,ab\}$. All hold.
(b) $M_{(0,0)} = \mathbf{k}\langle [a]\rangle$; $M_{(1,0)} = \mathbf{k}\langle [a],[b]\rangle$; $M_{(0,1)} = \mathbf{k}\langle [a]\rangle$; $M_{(1,1)} = \mathbf{k}\langle [a]\rangle$ since $ab$ merges the two components. The map $t_1\bullet : M_{(0,0)} \to M_{(1,0)}$ is the inclusion $[a] \mapsto [a]$; $t_2\bullet : M_{(1,0)} \to M_{(1,1)}$ sends both $[a]$ and $[b]$ to $[a]$.
(c) Both routes $M_{(0,0)} \to M_{(1,1)}$ send $[a] \mapsto [a]$, so the square commutes — as Definition 11.5 requires, the maps being induced by inclusions guarantees this.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — free and shifted modules)

(a) Using Definition 11.7, describe the shifted module $R_{\to (2,1)}$ explicitly:
give $\dim (R_{\to(2,1)})_{\mathbf{v}}$ for every $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{Z}^2$.
(b) Using Definition 11.8, decide whether the module of Problem 2(b) is free, and
prove your answer.
(c) Give a homogeneous basis for $R_{\to(2,1)} \oplus R_{\to(0,0)}$ and state the
grade of each basis element.

*(Dey §11.1.1, Definition 11.7 and Definition 11.8, p. 328)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$(M_{\to \mathbf{u}})_{\mathbf{v}} = M_{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}$, and the special module $R$ of Definition 11.3 has $R_{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{k}$ exactly when $\mathbf{u} \geq \mathbf{0}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), a free module is a direct sum of shifted copies of $R$; every such module has all its $t_i\bullet$ maps injective wherever the source is non-zero. Look for a map in Problem 2 that is not injective.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(R_{\to(2,1)})_{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{k}$ iff $\mathbf{v} \geq (2,1)$, and $0$ otherwise. In Problem 2, $t_2\bullet : M_{(1,0)} \to M_{(1,1)}$ has a one-dimensional kernel.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By Definition 11.7, $(R_{\to(2,1)})_{\mathbf{v}} = R_{\mathbf{v}-(2,1)}$, which is $\mathbf{k}$ precisely when $\mathbf{v} - (2,1) \geq (0,0)$, i.e. $v_1 \geq 2$ and $v_2 \geq 1$; it is $0$ elsewhere. So the module is a shifted "quadrant" with apex at $(2,1)$.
(b) Not free. In a free module $\bigoplus_j R_{\to \mathbf{u}_j}$ every structure map $t_i \bullet$ is injective on each summand where the source is non-zero, hence injective overall. But in Problem 2, $t_2\bullet : M_{(1,0)} \to M_{(1,1)}$ takes a $2$-dimensional space to a $1$-dimensional one and so has non-trivial kernel ($[a]-[b] \mapsto 0$). Therefore $M$ is not isomorphic to any direct sum of shifted copies of $R$.
(c) $\{e_1, e_2\}$ with $\operatorname{gr}(e_1) = (2,1)$ and $\operatorname{gr}(e_2) = (0,0)$, where $e_j$ is the multiplicative identity of the $j$-th summand (Definition 11.9).
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — morphisms preserve grades)

Let $M$ be the module of Problem 2(b) and let $N$ be the module with
$N_{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{k}$ at every grade $\mathbf{u} \geq (0,0)$ and $0$ elsewhere,
all non-trivial $t_i \bullet$ being the identity.

(a) Write down a non-zero morphism $f : M \to N$ in the sense of Definition 11.6 and
verify the commuting square at each grade where both modules are non-zero.
(b) Show that any morphism $g : N \to M$ must have $g_{(1,0)}$ non-surjective.
(c) Explain why a grade-*shifting* map — one sending $M_{\mathbf{u}}$ into
$N_{\mathbf{u}+e_1}$ — is not a morphism under Definition 11.6, and name the
construction from §11.1 that lets you talk about such maps properly.

*(Dey §11.1.1, Definition 11.6, pp. 327–328)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 11.6 demands $f(M_{\mathbf{u}}) \subseteq N_{\mathbf{u}}$ — the *same* grade, not a shifted one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a) send each component class to the generator of $N$. For (b) compare dimensions. For (c) recall Definition 11.7.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\dim N_{(1,0)} = 1 < 2 = \dim M_{(1,0)}$, so no linear map into $M_{(1,0)}$ from a one-dimensional space can be onto.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Define $f_{\mathbf{u}}$ to send every generator of $M_{\mathbf{u}}$ to the generator of $N_{\mathbf{u}}$ (so $f_{(1,0)}([a]) = f_{(1,0)}([b]) = 1$). Each square commutes because both routes land on the generator of $N$ at the target grade. This is grade-preserving, hence a morphism by Definition 11.6.
(b) $g_{(1,0)} : N_{(1,0)} \to M_{(1,0)}$ is a linear map from a $1$-dimensional space to a $2$-dimensional one, so its image has dimension at most $1$ and it cannot be surjective.
(c) Definition 11.6 requires $f(M_{\mathbf{u}}) \subseteq N_{\mathbf{u}}$; a map raising the grade violates this on the nose. The right way to speak of such maps is to shift one module first: a grade-raising map $M \to N$ is a genuine morphism $M \to N_{\to e_1}$ in the sense of Definition 11.7. (This is the same manoeuvre that makes an $\varepsilon$-interleaving a pair of honest morphisms into shifted modules — cf. cat-04.)
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — why the barcode does not survive) [interleaves aa-12, tda1-04]

The one-parameter structure theorem rests on $\mathbf{k}[t]$ being a principal ideal
domain. This problem asks you to locate exactly where that argument breaks.

(a) Prove that the ideal $(t_1, t_2) \subseteq \mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$ is not principal.
Conclude that $\mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$ is not a PID.
(b) Explain in one paragraph which step of the one-parameter argument — module over a
PID $\Rightarrow$ decomposition into cyclic summands $\Rightarrow$ intervals
$\Rightarrow$ barcode — is unavailable for $d \geq 2$, being precise about what
survives and what does not.
(c) The chapter opening asserts that indecomposables still *exist* for finitely
generated modules over $\mathbb{Z}^d$, by the Krull–Schmidt theorem, while also
saying they "cannot be completely characterized as in the one-parameter case".
These are not in conflict. Explain the distinction, and say which of the two claims
would be contradicted by someone reporting a "two-parameter barcode".

*(Dey ch. 11 opening, pp. 321–322; §11.1.1, Definition 11.1, p. 325)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), suppose $(t_1,t_2) = (f)$ and consider the degrees of $f$ against the fact that $f$ must divide both $t_1$ and $t_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) A common divisor of $t_1$ and $t_2$ in $\mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$ must be a unit; but a unit generates the whole ring, and $(t_1,t_2)$ is proper since it omits the constants. (b) and (c) turn on the difference between *existence* of a decomposition and *classification* of its pieces.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $(t_1,t_2) = (f)$ then $f \mid t_1$ and $f \mid t_2$, forcing $f$ to be a non-zero constant, so $(f) = \mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2] \ni 1$. But $1 \notin (t_1,t_2)$, since every element of $(t_1,t_2)$ has zero constant term.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Suppose $(t_1,t_2) = (f)$. Then $f$ divides $t_1$ and $t_2$. Any divisor of $t_1$ has $t_2$-degree $0$ and $t_1$-degree at most $1$; any divisor of $t_2$ likewise has $t_1$-degree $0$. So $f$ has degree $0$ in both variables, i.e. $f \in \mathbf{k}^{\times}$ is a unit, giving $(f) = \mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$. But every element of $(t_1,t_2)$ has zero constant term, so $1 \notin (t_1,t_2)$ and the ideal is proper — a contradiction. Hence $(t_1,t_2)$ is not principal and $\mathbf{k}[t_1,t_2]$ is not a PID.
(b) The one-parameter chain runs: a persistence module is a finitely generated graded $\mathbf{k}[t]$-module; $\mathbf{k}[t]$ is a PID; the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID splits it into cyclic summands; graded cyclic summands are exactly intervals; the multiset of intervals is the barcode. For $d \geq 2$ the second link fails by part (a), and with it everything downstream: there is no classification of the summands as intervals, hence no barcode.
(c) *Existence* of a decomposition into indecomposables is guaranteed by Krull–Schmidt for finitely generated modules over $\mathbb{Z}^d$; the indecomposables are also unique up to isomorphism and reordering. What fails is *classification*: unlike the one-parameter case, the indecomposables are not drawn from a simple list such as the intervals, and no complete discrete invariant analogous to the barcode is available. Someone reporting a "two-parameter barcode" is contradicting the second claim — they are asserting a classification, not merely a decomposition.
</details>
