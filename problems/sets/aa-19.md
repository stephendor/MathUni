## Problem 1 (easy–medium — two definitions, stated with exact sequences)

(a) State Definition 9.22 (p. 183) and Definition 9.23 (p. 183), and show that finitely presented implies finitely generated.

(b) Write down a finite presentation of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module — that is, an exact sequence $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m}\to\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\to0$ — and give the matrix.

(c) Do the same for $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, using aa-17 Problem 4(c).

(d) Show a module can have many presentations by giving a second one for $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ with $m=2$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) A presentation includes a surjection from a free module, which is what "finitely generated" asks for by aa-17 Problem 4(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Use the splitting $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Finitely generated means there is an exact sequence $R^{\oplus m}\to M\to0$, i.e. a surjection from a finite free module; finitely presented means there is an exact sequence $R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}\to M\to0$, so that the *relations* among those $m$ generators are themselves generated by finitely many. Dropping the left-hand term of the second sequence gives the first, so finitely presented implies finitely generated.

(b) $\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\times6}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\to0$, with matrix $(6)$. Exactness in the middle says $\ker(\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) = 6\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{im}(\times6)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by $(1,[0])$ and $(0,[1])$, and the relations are generated by $(0,2)$. So
$$\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\ A\ }\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}\to\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to0,\qquad A = \binom02 .$$

(d) Using $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$:
$$\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}\xrightarrow{\ \operatorname{diag}(2,3)\ }\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}\to\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\to0 .$$
So the same module is presented by a $1\times1$ matrix and by a $2\times2$ one. A presentation is a *choice*, not an invariant, and the content of aa-20's classification is that among all these choices there is a canonical one.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — Noetherian rings)

(a) State Definition 9.28 (p. 187), and prove that $R$ is Noetherian if and only if every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated.

(b) Deduce that every PID is Noetherian.

(c) Deduce that every field is Noetherian, and that $\mathbb{Z}$ is.

(d) Show that a quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) ($\Rightarrow$) Given an ideal $I$, build an ascending chain from finitely many elements at a time. ($\Leftarrow$) The union of a chain is an ideal — aa-12 Problem 3(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Use the correspondence between ideals of $R/I$ and ideals of $R$ containing $I$ — aa-11 Problem 4(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) ($\Leftarrow$) Let $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$ be a chain; $J = \bigcup I_n$ is an ideal by aa-12 Problem 3(a), so $J = (a_1,\dots,a_k)$ for finitely many $a_i$; each $a_i$ lies in some $I_{n_i}$, and taking $N = \max n_i$ puts all of them in $I_N$, whence $J\subseteq I_N$ and the chain stabilises at $N$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose some ideal $I$ is not finitely generated. Pick $a_1\in I$; since $(a_1)\ne I$, pick $a_2\in I\setminus(a_1)$; and so on. This produces a strictly ascending chain $(a_1)\subsetneq(a_1,a_2)\subsetneq\cdots$ inside $I$, which never stabilises.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) In a PID every ideal is generated by one element, hence finitely generated, so (a) applies. This is Proposition 6.32 (p. 116) restated: that proposition proved the chain condition directly, and (a) shows the two formulations are the same.

(c) A field has exactly two ideals (aa-09 Problem 3(b)), both finitely generated — by $0$ and by $1$. And $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID, hence Noetherian by (b). So Example 9.33 (p. 189)'s parenthetical — fields are Noetherian — is this.

(d) Let $R$ be Noetherian and $I$ an ideal. By aa-11 Problem 4(a) the ideals of $R/I$ are the $J/I$ with $I\subseteq J\subseteq R$, and the correspondence preserves inclusion. So an ascending chain in $R/I$ pulls back to an ascending chain in $R$, which stabilises; pushing forward, the original chain stabilises too.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — submodules of free modules over a Noetherian ring)

(a) Prove Lemma 9.31 (p. 188): if $R$ is Noetherian then every submodule of $R^{\oplus m}$ is finitely generated. Induct on $m$.

(b) Where does the base case $m=1$ use the Noetherian hypothesis?

(c) Deduce that every submodule of a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring is finitely generated.

(d) Show (c) fails without the hypothesis, given a ring with a non-finitely-generated ideal.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Project $R^{\oplus m}\to R$ onto the last coordinate; the image and the kernel of the restriction to $N$ are both handled by the inductive hypothesis.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
This is aa-18 Problem 6(b) with "finitely generated" in place of "free" and a Noetherian ring in place of $\mathbb{Z}$ — the same two-step argument.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) *Base case $m=1$:* a submodule of $R$ is an ideal, finitely generated because $R$ is Noetherian, by Problem 2(a).

*Step:* let $N\subseteq R^{\oplus m}$ and let $\pi : R^{\oplus m}\to R$ be the last coordinate. Then $\pi(N)$ is an ideal, so finitely generated, say by $c_1,\dots,c_k$; choose $v_i\in N$ with $\pi(v_i)=c_i$. And $N_0 = N\cap\ker\pi$ is a submodule of $R^{\oplus(m-1)}$, finitely generated by induction, say by $u_1,\dots,u_\ell$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a, cont.) For $n\in N$, $\pi(n) = \sum r_ic_i$, so $n - \sum r_iv_i$ lies in $N\cap\ker\pi = N_0$ and is a combination of the $u_j$. Hence $N$ is generated by $v_1,\dots,v_k,u_1,\dots,u_\ell$ — finitely many.

(b) At the base case and nowhere else: the induction step used only that $\pi(N)$ is a finitely generated ideal, which is the base case's content. Everything above the base is bookkeeping, which is why the whole lemma is equivalent to the statement about ideals.

(c) A finitely generated $M$ is a quotient $R^{\oplus m}/K$ (aa-17 Problem 4(b)). A submodule $N\subseteq M$ pulls back to a submodule $\tilde N\subseteq R^{\oplus m}$ containing $K$, finitely generated by (a); the images of its generators generate $N$.

(d) If $R$ has an ideal $I$ that is not finitely generated, then $R$ itself is a finitely generated $R$-module — cyclic, generated by $1$ — with a submodule $I$ that is not. Problem 6 supplies such a ring.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — finitely generated becomes finitely presented)

(a) Prove Theorem 9.29 (p. 187): over a Noetherian ring, every finitely generated module is finitely presented.

(b) Deduce Example 9.32 (p. 189): every finitely generated abelian group is finitely presented.

(c) Deduce Example 9.33 (p. 189): every finitely generated vector space is finitely presented.

(d) Explain why (a) makes the distinction between the two definitions invisible over $\mathbb{Z}$, and say where in this module the distinction has to become visible.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Take a surjection $R^{\oplus m}\to M$; its kernel is a submodule of $R^{\oplus m}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply Lemma 9.31, then turn a finite generating set of the kernel into a map from $R^{\oplus n}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $M$ be generated by $m$ elements, so there is a surjection $\varphi : R^{\oplus m}\to M$ (aa-17 Problem 4(a)). Its kernel $K$ is a submodule of $R^{\oplus m}$, hence finitely generated by Lemma 9.31 (p. 188), say by $k_1,\dots,k_n$. By aa-18 Problem 2(a) there is a homomorphism $\psi : R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}$ with $\psi(e_j)=k_j$, and $\operatorname{im}\psi = K = \ker\varphi$. So
$$R^{\oplus n}\xrightarrow{\ \psi\ }R^{\oplus m}\xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ }M\to0$$
is exact, which is Definition 9.23.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian by Problem 2(c), and abelian groups are $\mathbb{Z}$-modules by aa-15 Problem 3(c). Apply (a).

(c) A field is Noetherian by Problem 2(c). Apply (a). In fact more is true — Theorem 9.35 (p. 190) says such a module is *free*, so the presentation can be taken with the zero matrix.

(d) Over $\mathbb{Z}$, and over any Noetherian ring, "finitely generated" and "finitely presented" pick out the same modules, so no example can distinguish them there — and since every ring named in this module before now is Noetherian ($\mathbb{Z}$, fields, $k[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and every PID), the distinction has been invisible throughout. It becomes visible only over a non-Noetherian ring, which Problem 6 constructs. That is the reason Aluffi gives both definitions rather than one: they are different definitions that happen to agree everywhere the reader has been so far.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — vector spaces are free, and modules are not)

(a) Prove Theorem 9.35 (p. 190): every finitely generated vector space over a field $k$ is free, i.e. isomorphic to $k^{\oplus m}$ for some $m$.

(b) Prove the key step, Lemma 9.36 (p. 190), at least in outline: any matrix over a field can be brought by elementary row and column operations to a form with $1$s on part of the diagonal and $0$s elsewhere.

(c) Deduce that every finitely generated $k$-vector space has a basis, and that any two bases have the same size.

(d) Give a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module that is not free, and locate the step of (a) that fails for it.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Move a nonzero entry to position $(1,1)$, scale it to $1$, then clear its row and column, and recurse.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Present $V$ as $k^{\oplus m}/\operatorname{im} f_A$ and simplify $A$ by (b).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) If $A=0$ there is nothing to do. Otherwise pick a nonzero entry $a_{ij}$; swap row $i$ with row $1$ and column $j$ with column $1$, then multiply row $1$ by $a_{ij}^{-1}$ — **legitimate because $k$ is a field and every nonzero element is a unit**. Now $a_{11}=1$, and subtracting multiples of row 1 and column 1 clears the rest of the first row and column. Recurse on the remaining $(m-1)\times(n-1)$ block.

(a) $V$ is finitely generated, so $V\cong k^{\oplus m}/\operatorname{im} f_A$ for some matrix $A$ (aa-18 Problem 5(a) with aa-17 Problem 4(b)). By (b) and Proposition 9.25 (p. 184), we may replace $A$ by an equivalent matrix with $r$ ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a, cont.) For such a matrix the image is spanned by $e_1,\dots,e_r$, so
$$V\cong k^{\oplus m}/\langle e_1,\dots,e_r\rangle\cong k^{\oplus(m-r)},$$
which is free.

(c) Freeness gives a basis by Proposition 9.6 (p. 175). For the size, a basis of $m$ elements makes $V\cong k^{\oplus m}$, and $k^{\oplus m}\cong k^{\oplus n}$ forces $m=n$ — over a field because dimensions can be compared by cardinality when $k$ is finite, and in general because an isomorphism would give invertible matrices $P,Q$ with $PQ = I_m$, $QP = I_n$, impossible for $m\ne n$ over a field.

(d) $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by one element and is not free (aa-18 Problem 1(c)). The step that fails is in (b): the matrix presenting it is $(2)$, and clearing it requires multiplying by $2^{-1}$, which does not exist in $\mathbb{Z}$. Everything else in the argument survives — and what survives is exactly aa-20's Theorem 9.40, where the diagonal entries can be made to divide one another but not made equal to $1$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a ring where the two definitions come apart)

Let $k$ be a field and let $R = k[x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots]$ be the ring of polynomials in countably many variables — each element involves only finitely many of them.

(a) Show $R$ is not Noetherian, by exhibiting an ascending chain of ideals that never stabilises.

(b) Show the ideal $I = (x_1,x_2,\dots)$ is not finitely generated.

(c) Deduce that $R$ is a finitely generated $R$-module with a submodule that is not finitely generated, so Problem 3(c) genuinely needs its hypothesis.

(d) The module $M = R/I$ is cyclic, hence finitely generated: the quotient map $\pi : R\to M$ is surjective with $\ker\pi = I$, which by (b) is not finitely generated. Explain carefully why this does **not** yet prove $M$ is not finitely presented, and name what would be needed.

(Note the shape of that sequence: it is $I\to R\to M\to 0$, not $R\to R\to M\to 0$. There is no exact sequence of the second kind here at all — the image of any $R$-linear map $R\to R$ is principal, and exactness would force that image to be $I$, which is not even finitely generated.)

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) A finite generating set involves only finitely many variables.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) "Finitely presented" asks that *some* presentation have finitely generated kernel, not that a particular one does.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $(x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2,x_3)\subsetneq\cdots$. Each containment is strict: $x_{n+1}\notin(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, because every element of that ideal is a combination $\sum f_ix_i$ and setting $x_1=\cdots=x_n=0$ sends it to $0$ while $x_{n+1}$ becomes $x_{n+1}\ne0$. So the chain never stabilises and $R$ is not Noetherian.

(b) Suppose $I = (f_1,\dots,f_r)$. Each $f_j$ involves finitely many variables, so together they involve at most some $x_1,\dots,x_N$. Setting those to $0$ kills every element of $(f_1,\dots,f_r)$, but sends $x_{N+1}\in I$ to $x_{N+1}\ne0$. So $I\ne(f_1,\dots,f_r)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $R$ is generated as an $R$-module by $1$, so it is finitely generated — cyclic, even. Its submodules are its ideals (aa-16 Problem 1(c)), and $I$ is one that is not finitely generated by (b). So the conclusion of Problem 3(c) fails here, and its Noetherian hypothesis was not decoration.

(d) Definition 9.23 (p. 183) asks for the *existence* of an exact sequence $R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}\to M\to0$. Exhibiting one presentation whose kernel is not finitely generated leaves open that some *other* choice of generators — perhaps more of them, perhaps a different surjection — has a finitely generated kernel. So (d)'s computation is evidence and not a proof.

What closes the gap is **Schanuel's lemma**: if $0\to K\to R^{\oplus m}\to M\to0$ and $0\to K'\to R^{\oplus m'}\to M\to0$ are two presentations of the same $M$ by free modules, then $K\oplus R^{\oplus m'}\cong K'\oplus R^{\oplus m}$. From it, one kernel is finitely generated exactly when the other is, so the property does not depend on the presentation chosen and $M = R/I$ is genuinely not finitely presented. That lemma is true and is not proved here; it is recorded as the missing step rather than assumed, in the same spirit as aa-12 Problem 5(d) and aa-14 Problem 6(d).
</details>
