## Problem 1 (easy — cell structures and the Euler characteristic)

For a cell complex with finitely many cells, Hatcher defines the Euler
characteristic to be the number of even-dimensional cells minus the number of
odd-dimensional cells.

(a) Describe the cell structure on $S^n$ with exactly two cells, and say what
its attaching map is. Compute the Euler characteristic for $n = 1, 2, 3$.
(b) The torus $S^1 \times S^1$ is built from a square by identifying opposite
sides. List its cells by dimension and compute the Euler characteristic.
(c) Verify Hatcher's count for the house with two rooms: he reports 29
$0$-cells, 51 $1$-cells and 23 $2$-cells, with alternating sum
$29 - 51 + 23$. What value does that give, and why is that value forced?
(d) Hatcher justifies (c) by appeal to Theorem 2.44 — the Euler characteristic
of a cell complex depends only on its homotopy type. Explain in two sentences
why (c) would be a *coincidence* without Theorem 2.44, and what Theorem 2.44
turns it into.

*(Hatcher, ch. 0, pp. 5–6; the two-cell structure on $S^n$ is Example 0.3,
the house-with-two-rooms count is Example 0.2)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a): $S^n = D^n / \partial D^n$. For (c): the house with two rooms is
contractible, and a point has how many cells?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Count cells by dimension in each case and apply the alternating-sum definition
literally. For (d), separate two claims: "this particular cell structure has
alternating sum 1" is arithmetic, while "every cell structure on this space has
alternating sum 1" is a theorem.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\chi(S^n) = 1 + (-1)^n$, so $\chi(S^1) = 0$, $\chi(S^2) = 2$,
$\chi(S^3) = 0$. (b) One $0$-cell, two $1$-cells, one $2$-cell; $\chi = 0$.
(c) $29 - 51 + 23 = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By Example 0.3, $S^n$ has a cell structure with one $0$-cell $e^0$ and one
$n$-cell $e^n$, the $n$-cell attached by the constant map
$S^{n-1} \to e^0$; equivalently $S^n = D^n/\partial D^n$. For $n = 2$ the cells
are one $0$-cell and one $2$-cell, both even-dimensional, so $\chi = 2$. For
$n = 1$ the $1$-cell is odd, so $\chi = 1 - 1 = 0$. ⟨your step: $n = 3$⟩

(b) ⟨your step: the $4g$-gon picture with $g = 1$ gives how many of each cell?⟩

</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — real projective space, inductively)

Hatcher defines $\mathbb{RP}^n$ as the space of all lines through the origin in
$\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, topologised as the quotient of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1} - \{0\}$ by
$v \sim \lambda v$ for $\lambda \neq 0$.

(a) Show that $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is also the quotient $S^n/(v \sim -v)$, the sphere
with antipodal points identified.
(b) Show it is also the quotient of a hemisphere $D^n$ with antipodal points of
$\partial D^n$ identified.
(c) Deduce that $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is obtained from $\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$ by
attaching a single $n$-cell, and identify the attaching map.
(d) Conclude by induction that $\mathbb{RP}^n$ has a cell structure
$e^0 \cup e^1 \cup \cdots \cup e^n$ with one cell in each dimension $i \leq n$,
and write down the characteristic map for the cell $e^i$. Then compute
$\chi(\mathbb{RP}^n)$ as a function of $n$.

*(Hatcher, ch. 0, Example 0.4, p. 6; characteristic maps, p. 7)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Every nonzero vector is a positive multiple of a unit vector, and a unit vector
is determined up to sign. Then every unit vector is $\pm$ a vector in the
closed upper hemisphere.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Each part restricts the previous one to a smaller set of representatives, and
each time you must check the restriction is still surjective and that the
identifications match up. For (c), note $\partial D^n$ with antipodal points
identified *is* $\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$, so the quotient projection
$S^{n-1} \to \mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$ is the attaching map.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The attaching map is the quotient projection
$\varphi : S^{n-1} \to \mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$ identifying antipodal points.
(d) A characteristic map for $e^i$ is the quotient map
$D^i \to \mathbb{RP}^i \subseteq \mathbb{RP}^n$. With one cell in each dimension
$0$ through $n$, the Euler characteristic is
$\chi(\mathbb{RP}^n) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (-1)^i$, an alternating sum of $n+1$
ones — so its value depends only on the parity of $n$. Work out which parity
gives which value.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Restrict to vectors of length $1$. Every line through the origin meets
$S^n$ in exactly two antipodal points, and two unit vectors span the same line
precisely when they differ by $\lambda = \pm 1$. So the composite
$S^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1} - \{0\} \to \mathbb{RP}^n$ is surjective
and identifies exactly $v$ with $-v$. ⟨your step: why is the induced bijection
$S^n/(v \sim -v) \to \mathbb{RP}^n$ a homeomorphism and not merely a continuous
bijection?⟩

(b) ⟨your step⟩

(c) By (b), $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is $D^n$ with antipodal points of $\partial D^n$
identified. Since $\partial D^n = S^{n-1}$ with antipodal points identified is
exactly $\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$, this exhibits $\mathbb{RP}^n$ as the quotient of
$\mathbb{RP}^{n-1} \sqcup D^n$ under $x \sim \varphi(x)$ for
$x \in \partial D^n$ — which is precisely clause (2) of the cell-complex
construction with a single $n$-cell. ⟨your step: name $\varphi$⟩

## Problem 3 (medium — subcomplexes, and a cell whose closure is not one)

A subcomplex of a cell complex $X$ is a closed subspace $A \subseteq X$ that is
a union of cells of $X$.

(a) Prove Hatcher's remark that a subcomplex is a cell complex in its own
right. Specifically: show that because $A$ is closed, the characteristic map of
each cell in $A$ has image contained in $A$, and hence so does the image of
each attaching map.
(b) Show that each skeleton $X^n$ is a subcomplex of $X$.
(c) Give $S^n$ its two-cell structure of Example 0.3. Show that for
$0 < k < n$ the subsphere $S^k \subseteq S^n$ is *not* a subcomplex. Then
describe Hatcher's alternative cell structure — each $S^k$ obtained from the
equatorial $S^{k-1}$ by attaching two $k$-cells — and show that in it every
$S^k$ *is* a subcomplex. How many cells does this structure have in each
dimension?
(d) Hatcher notes that the closure of a cell need not be a subcomplex, though
in most naturally arising structures it is. Reproduce his example: start with
$S^1$ in its minimal cell structure and attach a $2$-cell by a map
$S^1 \to S^1$ whose image is a nontrivial subarc of $S^1$. Explain precisely
why the closure of the $2$-cell fails to be a subcomplex.

*(Hatcher, ch. 0, pp. 7–8)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (c), the two-cell structure has only one cell in each of dimensions $0$ and
$n$; a union of cells taken from that list is very restricted. For (d), "union
of cells" is not "union of parts of cells".
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
In (a) the key phrase is that $\Phi_\alpha(D^n_\alpha)$ is the closure of
$e^n_\alpha$, so containment in the closed set $A$ follows once $A$ contains the
cell. In (d), identify exactly which points of the $1$-cell lie in the closure
of the $2$-cell, and compare with the definition's requirement that a
subcomplex be a *union of cells*.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) In the two-cell structure the only unions of cells are $\emptyset$,
$\{e^0\}$, $\{e^n\}$ and the whole of $S^n$ — and $S^k$ is none of these for
$0 < k < n$. In the equatorial structure there are exactly two cells in each
dimension $0$ through $n$, so $\chi = 1 + (-1)^n$ again.
(d) The closure of the $2$-cell contains the subarc but not all of the $1$-cell.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Let $e^n_\alpha$ be a cell of $A$ with characteristic map
$\Phi_\alpha : D^n_\alpha \to X$. The image $\Phi_\alpha(D^n_\alpha)$ is compact
and contains $e^n_\alpha$; in fact it is the closure of $e^n_\alpha$ in $X$,
since $\Phi_\alpha$ restricts to a homeomorphism from the interior onto
$e^n_\alpha$ and $D^n_\alpha$ is the closure of its interior. As
$e^n_\alpha \subseteq A$ and $A$ is closed, the closure of $e^n_\alpha$ lies in
$A$, so $\Phi_\alpha(D^n_\alpha) \subseteq A$. Restricting to
$\partial D^n_\alpha$ gives $\varphi_\alpha(\partial D^n_\alpha) \subseteq A$.
⟨your step: now assemble the clauses of the cell-complex definition for $A$⟩

</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — the operations, and what they do to cells)

(a) Let $X$ be a cell complex. Prove that the quotient $X^n / X^{n-1}$ is a
wedge sum $\bigvee_\alpha S^n_\alpha$ of $n$-spheres, one for each $n$-cell of
$X$. Say which clause of the quotient construction you are using.
(b) The suspension $SX$ is the quotient of $X \times I$ collapsing
$X \times \{0\}$ to one point and $X \times \{1\}$ to another. Show
$S(S^n) = S^{n+1}$, and describe the cell structure $SX$ inherits when $X$ is a
cell complex: how many $0$-cells, and what is the dimension of the cell
corresponding to an $n$-cell of $X$?
(c) The smash product is $X \wedge Y = X \times Y / X \vee Y$. Prove
$S^m \wedge S^n = S^{m+n}$ by exhibiting a cell structure with just two cells,
of dimensions $0$ and $m+n$. Then state what the case $m = n = 1$ says about
collapsing the longitude and meridian circles of a torus.
(d) The reduced suspension $\Sigma X$ collapses $\{x_0\} \times I$ inside $SX$.
Show $\Sigma X = X \wedge S^1$, using Hatcher's observation that both are the
quotient of $X \times I$ with $X \times \partial I \cup \{x_0\} \times I$
collapsed to a point. Then compare the cell counts of $SX$ and $\Sigma X$ and
say precisely how many cells the reduction saves.

*(Hatcher, ch. 0, pp. 8–10 for the operations; Example 0.10, p. 12, for the
reduced suspension)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), the cells of $X^n/X^{n-1}$ are the cells of $X^n - X^{n-1}$ plus one
new $0$-cell — and an $n$-cell attached to a single $0$-cell can only be
attached by the constant map.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Each part is the same move: identify the cells of the quotient using Hatcher's
rule that the cells of $X/A$ are the cells of $X - A$ plus one new $0$-cell, then
recognise the resulting structure from Example 0.3. For (d), write both spaces
as quotients of $X \times I$ and compare the subspaces being collapsed.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $SX$ has two $0$-cells and one $(n+1)$-cell $e^n \times (0,1)$ per $n$-cell
$e^n$ of $X$. (d) $\Sigma X$ has a single $0$-cell and an $(n+1)$-cell for each
$n$-cell of $X$ other than $x_0$ — so the reduction saves one $0$-cell and one
$1$-cell.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) By the quotient rule, the cells of $X^n/X^{n-1}$ are the cells of
$X^n - X^{n-1}$ — that is, exactly the $n$-cells of $X$ — together with one new
$0$-cell, the image of $X^{n-1}$. For an $n$-cell $e^n_\alpha$ of $X$ attached
by $\varphi_\alpha : S^{n-1} \to X^{n-1}$, the attaching map of the
corresponding cell in the quotient is the composite
$S^{n-1} \to X^{n-1} \to X^{n-1}/X^{n-1}$, which lands in a single point and is
therefore constant. So $X^n/X^{n-1}$ is a $0$-cell with a family of $n$-cells
each attached by a constant map; by Example 0.3 each such $n$-cell together
with the $0$-cell forms an $S^n$, and they meet only in that $0$-cell.
⟨your step: conclude this is the wedge sum⟩

(b) ⟨your step⟩ (c) ⟨your step⟩ (d) ⟨your step⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — collapsing and attaching, two routes to one answer)

Hatcher gives two criteria for homotopy equivalence in this chapter: collapse a
contractible subcomplex, or vary an attaching map by a homotopy. Example 0.8 is
worked by the first; Example 0.11 rederives it by the second. Do both, then
compare.

Let $X$ be the space obtained from $S^2$ by attaching the two ends of an arc
$A$ to the north and south poles, and let $B$ be an arc in $S^2$ joining those
two points.

(a) Give $X$ a CW structure with the two endpoints of $A$ and $B$ as $0$-cells,
the interiors of $A$ and $B$ as $1$-cells, and the rest of $S^2$ as a $2$-cell.
Check it is a legitimate cell structure.
(b) *Route one (collapsing).* Both $A$ and $B$ are contractible subcomplexes.
Identify $X/A$ and $X/B$ concretely, and deduce that $S^2/S^0$ and
$S^1 \vee S^2$ are homotopy equivalent. State exactly which result licenses each
collapse — and note that Proposition 0.17 requires the homotopy extension
property, so say what supplies it here.
(c) *Route two (attaching).* The sphere with two points identified is obtained
by attaching $S^2$ to $S^1$ by a map wrapping a closed arc $A \subseteq S^2$
around $S^1$. Since $A$ is contractible, that attaching map is homotopic to a
constant map. Apply Proposition 0.18 to conclude, and say where the hypothesis
that $(S^2, A)$ is a CW pair is used.
(d) Now generalise, following Example 0.14: if $(X,A)$ is a CW pair and $A$ is
contractible *in* $X$ — meaning the inclusion $A \hookrightarrow X$ is
homotopic to a constant map — then $X/A \simeq X \vee SA$. Use it to prove
$S^n/S^i \simeq S^n \vee S^{i+1}$ for $i < n$, and check that $i = 0$, $n = 2$
recovers part (b). Finally, explain in one sentence why the two routes are not
the same argument, referring to Corollary 0.21 for what "homotopy equivalent"
is being established in each.

*(Hatcher, ch. 0, Example 0.8 p. 11 and Example 0.9 p. 12 for route one;
"Attaching Spaces" and Example 0.11 pp. 12–13 for route two; Example 0.13
p. 13 and Example 0.14 p. 14; Proposition 0.17 p. 15 and Proposition 0.18 p. 16;
Corollary 0.21 p. 16)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b): collapsing $A$ identifies the two poles of $S^2$; collapsing $B$
leaves the sphere intact and turns $A$ into a loop.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Route one collapses a subspace and needs Proposition 0.17 (with Proposition
0.16 supplying the homotopy extension property for a CW pair). Route two keeps
the pieces and moves the glue, needing Proposition 0.18. The pattern to notice
is that both criteria trade a contractible piece for a simplification, but one
contracts it inside the total space and the other contracts it inside the
attaching data.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $X/A$ is $S^2/S^0$, the sphere with two points identified; $X/B$ is
$S^1 \vee S^2$. Both are homotopy equivalent to $X$, hence to each other.
The homotopy extension property comes from Proposition 0.16, since $(X,A)$ and
$(X,B)$ are CW pairs.
(d) $S^i$ is contractible in $S^n$ when $i < n$, so
$S^n/S^i \simeq S^n \vee S^{i+1}$; at $i=0$, $n=2$ this is
$S^2/S^0 \simeq S^2 \vee S^1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) The subcomplex $A$ is a single arc: one $1$-cell with its two $0$-cells, so
it is contractible. $(X,A)$ is a CW pair, so by Proposition 0.16 it has the
homotopy extension property, and Proposition 0.17 then says
$q : X \to X/A$ is a homotopy equivalence. Collapsing $A$ pulls the two poles
of $S^2$ together, so $X/A = S^2/S^0$.

Likewise $B$ is contractible and $(X,B)$ is a CW pair, so $X \simeq X/B$.
Collapsing $B$ leaves $S^2$ unchanged as a set but closes the arc $A$ into a
loop meeting the sphere at one point, so $X/B = S^1 \vee S^2$.

Since $\simeq$ is an equivalence relation (at1-01, Problem 3),
$S^2/S^0 \simeq X \simeq S^1 \vee S^2$. ⟨your step: write the chain out and say
which direction of each equivalence you used⟩

</details>
