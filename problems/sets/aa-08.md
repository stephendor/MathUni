## Problem 1 (easy — six candidate maps)

For each function, decide whether it is a ring homomorphism in the sense of Definition 4.15 (p. 64). When it is not, name every clause that fails.

(a) $f : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $f(a) = 2a$.

(b) $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $\varphi(a) = [a]_n$, for $n \ge 0$.

(c) $\det : M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$.

(d) conjugation $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$, $a + bi \mapsto a - bi$.

(e) $\psi : \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $\psi([a]_{12}) = [a]_4$.

(f) $e_2 : \mathbb{Z}[x]\to\mathbb{Z}$, $e_2(\alpha(x)) = \alpha(2)$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Definition 4.15 asks for three things: $f(a+b) = f(a)+f(b)$, $f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$, and $f(1_R) = 1_S$. Check all three every time — several of these fail exactly one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (e), there is a fourth question before the three: is $\psi$ even a function? That is aa-03's Problem 5.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Fails two clauses. $f(1) = 2 \ne 1$, and $f(ab) = 2ab$ while $f(a)f(b) = 4ab$, which differ whenever $ab \ne 0$. Addition is the one clause it satisfies. This is Aluffi's Example 4.24 (p. 67).

(b) A homomorphism, and Example 4.19 (p. 65) is exactly it: all three clauses are the definitions of $+$ and $\cdot$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ read backwards.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$ and $\det I = 1$, so two clauses hold — and addition fails badly: $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \det\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = 0$ while the determinant of the sum is $1$. Example 4.26 (p. 67). Multiplicativity alone is not a homomorphism.

(d) A homomorphism, and a bijection: $\overline{z+w} = \bar z + \bar w$, $\overline{zw} = \bar z\bar w$, $\bar 1 = 1$. Example 4.23 (p. 66), and Problem 6 returns to it.

(e) First, $\psi$ is well defined because $4 \mid 12$ — without that it is not a function at all, by aa-03 Problem 5. Given that, all three clauses follow from the operations on both sides being defined by representatives. Example 4.21 (p. 66).

(f) A homomorphism: evaluation at $2$ respects sums and products of polynomials, and the constant polynomial $1$ evaluates to $1$. Example 4.22 (p. 66). Nothing depends on the number $2$; any element of any commutative ring containing $\mathbb{Z}$ would do, which is the observation aa-13 turns into a theorem.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — what follows, and what has to be demanded)

(a) Prove Proposition 4.16 (p. 65): a ring homomorphism sends $0_R$ to $0_S$.

(b) Prove that $f(-a) = -f(a)$, and that $f(na) = nf(a)$ for every $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

(c) Definition 4.15 (p. 64) **demands** $f(1_R) = 1_S$ rather than deriving it. Show that this is unavoidable: exhibit a map between nonzero rings that respects $+$ and $\cdot$ and sends $1$ somewhere else.

(d) Show that if $f$ respects $+$ and $\cdot$ and is **surjective**, then $f(1_R) = 1_S$ comes free. Where does your argument in (c) evade this?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $f(0) = f(0+0) = f(0)+f(0)$; now cancel, which is Proposition 3.17 (p. 50). (c) aa-07's Problem 5 already built the map.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Given $s \in S$, write $s = f(r)$ and compute $f(1_R)s$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $f(0_R) = f(0_R + 0_R) = f(0_R) + f(0_R)$, and cancelling $f(0_R)$ from both sides leaves $0_S = f(0_R)$. Note that this uses the additive clause only — a map respecting multiplication alone need not send $0$ to $0$.

(b) $f(a) + f(-a) = f(a + (-a)) = f(0) = 0$, so $f(-a)$ is an additive inverse of $f(a)$, and by uniqueness (Proposition 3.15, p. 48) it is *the* one. The statement about $na$ is then induction for $n \ge 0$ and the sign rule for $n < 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Take $\iota : R \to R\times S$, $a \mapsto (a, 0_S)$, with $R$ and $S$ both nonzero — aa-07 Problem 5(c). It respects both operations coordinatewise, and $\iota(1_R) = (1_R, 0_S) \ne (1_R, 1_S) = 1_{R\times S}$. So the third clause is genuinely independent of the first two, and Definition 4.15 is not being redundant.

(d) Suppose $f$ respects both operations and is onto. Let $s \in S$ and pick $r$ with $f(r) = s$. Then
$$f(1_R)\,s = f(1_R)f(r) = f(1_R\cdot r) = f(r) = s,$$
and symmetrically $s\,f(1_R) = s$. So $f(1_R)$ is a two-sided multiplicative identity for $S$, and identities are unique (Proposition 3.14, p. 48), giving $f(1_R) = 1_S$.

The map in (c) evades this by not being surjective: its image is $R\times\{0_S\}$, which misses $(1_R,1_S)$ entirely. Inside its own image, $\iota(1_R)$ *is* the identity — which is exactly the observation of aa-07 Problem 3(c), that the subset has an identity of its own that is not the ambient one.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — counting the homomorphisms out of ℤ and out of ℤ/nℤ)

(a) Prove that for every ring $R$ there is **exactly one** ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to R$.

(b) Prove Example 4.25 (p. 67): there is no ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$.

(c) For $n, m \ge 1$, determine exactly how many ring homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ there are, in terms of $n$ and $m$.

(d) Check (c) against (b), and against the case $m = 1$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) A homomorphism must send $1$ to $1$ and respect addition, so its value at every integer is forced. What remains is to check the forced map really is one — which is aa-07 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) The same forcing gives $[a]_n \mapsto [a]_m$ as the only candidate. Whether it is a function at all is aa-03 Problem 5.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Uniqueness: $f(1) = 1_R$ is demanded, and $f(n) = n\cdot 1_R$ follows by Problem 2(b). Existence: the map $n \mapsto n\cdot 1_R$ respects $+$ and $\cdot$ by aa-07 Problem 6(a), and sends $1$ to $1_R$. So there is one and there is not more than one. (Aluffi's Example 4.20, p. 65, is this map.)

(b) A homomorphism $g:\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ must have $g([1]_2) = 1$, hence $g([0]_2) = g([1]_2 + [1]_2) = 2$. But $g([0]_2) = 0$ by Proposition 4.16 (p. 65), and $2 \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) **One if $m \mid n$, none otherwise.** The value at $[1]_n$ is forced to be $[1]_m$, and additivity then forces $[a]_n \mapsto [a]_m$; so there is at most one candidate, and the only question is whether it is well defined. By aa-03 Problem 5 that holds exactly when $m \mid n$. When it does, the three clauses are immediate from the operations being defined on representatives.

(d) The obstruction in (b) is the same one: $\mathbb{Z}$ is not $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ for any $m \ge 1$, but the computation is identical — $[0]_2$ must go to $0$ and additivity sends it to $2$. Put the other way, a homomorphism out of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ needs its target to have characteristic dividing $n$ (aa-07 Problem 6), and $\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $0$, which divides nothing.

At $m = 1$ the target is the zero ring and $1 \mid n$ always, so there is exactly one homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\{0\}$ — the constant map, which is Aluffi's Example 4.17 (p. 65). Here $1_S = 0_S$ and the third clause of Definition 4.15 is satisfied by an equation that looks alarming and is not.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — an isomorphism is a homomorphism with a homomorphic inverse)

(a) Prove Proposition 4.30 (p. 71): if $f : R \to S$ is a bijective ring homomorphism, then $f^{-1}$ is a ring homomorphism too.

(b) Deduce Corollary 4.31 (p. 71): $f$ is an isomorphism if and only if there is a ring homomorphism $g : S\to R$ with $g\circ f = \mathrm{id}_R$ and $f\circ g = \mathrm{id}_S$.

(c) Prove Corollary 4.33 (p. 71): $\cong$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Which of Proposition 4.27's statements (p. 68) does the transitive case need?

(d) A bijective *group* homomorphism and a bijective *continuous map* are not analogous here in one respect. State what (a) is really asserting, and why the same sentence is false for continuous maps.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Given $s, s' \in S$, write $s = f(r)$, $s' = f(r')$ and apply $f^{-1}$ to $f(r) + f(r') = f(r+r')$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Transitivity needs that the composition of two ring homomorphisms is a ring homomorphism, which is Proposition 4.27; reflexivity needs that the identity map is one.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $s, s' \in S$ and put $r = f^{-1}(s)$, $r' = f^{-1}(s')$. Then $f(r + r') = f(r)+f(r') = s + s'$, so $f^{-1}(s+s') = r + r' = f^{-1}(s) + f^{-1}(s')$. The multiplicative clause is identical. And $f(1_R) = 1_S$ gives $f^{-1}(1_S) = 1_R$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) ($\Rightarrow$) Take $g = f^{-1}$, a homomorphism by (a), and the two compositions are the identities because $f$ is a bijection. ($\Leftarrow$) If such a $g$ exists then $f$ has a two-sided inverse as a *function*, so it is a bijection, hence an isomorphism by Definition 4.29 (p. 70).

(c) Reflexive: $\mathrm{id}_R$ is a ring homomorphism and a bijection. Symmetric: (a). Transitive: if $f : R\to S$ and $h : S\to T$ are isomorphisms then $h\circ f$ is a bijection, and it is a homomorphism because a composition of ring homomorphisms is one — Proposition 4.27 (p. 68).

(d) What (a) asserts is that for rings, "bijective homomorphism" and "homomorphism with a homomorphic inverse" are the same condition — so no separate word is needed for the second. That is a fact about rings, not a formality: for continuous maps it is **false**, since a continuous bijection need not have a continuous inverse ($t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)$ on $[0,2\pi)$ is the standard witness). Remark 4.34 (p. 72) notes that Aluffi could have taken the two-sided-inverse version as the definition; the reason it does not matter here is precisely (a), and the reason it matters elsewhere is that (a) has no analogue there.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — telling rings apart)

(a) Prove Proposition 4.36 (p. 72): under an isomorphism, units go to units and zero-divisors go to zero-divisors.

(b) Prove Corollary 4.37 (p. 72): being an integral domain, and being a field, transfer across an isomorphism.

(c) Show $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\not\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$ without checking all $24$ bijections. Compare Example 4.38 (p. 73).

(d) Show $\mathbb{Z}\not\cong\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}\not\cong\mathbb{C}$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) If $ab = 1$ apply $f$; if $ab = 0$ with $b \ne 0$ apply $f$ and use injectivity to keep $f(b) \ne 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Count units, or count solutions of $x + x = 0$. (d) One of these is about units and the other about squares.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $ab = ba = 1_R$ then $f(a)f(b) = f(ab) = f(1_R) = 1_S$ and likewise on the other side, so $f(a)$ is a unit. If $ab = 0$ with $b \ne 0_R$ then $f(a)f(b) = f(0_R) = 0_S$, and $f(b) \ne 0_S$ because $f$ is injective and $f(0_R) = 0_S$ — that injectivity step is the whole content, since without it $f(b)$ might be $0$ and nothing would have been shown. The case $ba = 0$ is the same computation on the other side, and Definition 3.22 (p. 53) counts both.

(b) Domain: $S$ inherits "no zero-divisors" from $R$ by (a) applied to $f^{-1}$, and $1_S \ne 0_S$ because $f$ is injective and $1_R \ne 0_R$. Field: every nonzero $s \in S$ is $f(r)$ for a nonzero $r$, which is a unit, so $s$ is a unit by (a).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has two units, $[1]$ and $[3]$. $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$ has one, $(1,1)$ — the other three elements are $0$ or zero-divisors. A bijection carrying units to units cannot match a $2$-element set with a $1$-element one, so by (a) there is no isomorphism. (The $x + x = 0$ count of aa-07 Problem 1 does the same job and needs no proposition at all.)

(d) $\mathbb{Z}\not\cong\mathbb{Q}$: $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field and $\mathbb{Z}$ is not, so Corollary 4.37 forbids it. Concretely, $2$ is a non-unit in $\mathbb{Z}$ and every nonzero element of $\mathbb{Q}$ is a unit.

$\mathbb{R}\not\cong\mathbb{C}$: both are fields, so (b) is no help and a finer invariant is needed. Suppose $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ were an isomorphism. Then $f(i)^2 = f(i^2) = f(-1) = -f(1) = -1$, so $-1$ is a square in $\mathbb{R}$, which it is not. The invariant used is "$-1$ is a square", and it is an invariant because $f$ respects squaring and fixes $-1$ — Example 4.35 (p. 72) makes the same point for commutativity, and the pattern is that any property written purely in $+$, $\cdot$, $0$ and $1$ transfers.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — how few automorphisms a field can have)

An **automorphism** of a ring $R$ is an isomorphism $R \to R$.

(a) Verify Example 4.40 (p. 73): complex conjugation is an automorphism of $\mathbb{C}$.

(b) Prove that the identity is the **only** ring automorphism of $\mathbb{Z}$, and the only one of $\mathbb{Q}$.

(c) Prove that the identity is the only ring **homomorphism** $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ — not merely the only automorphism. Suggested route: show any such $f$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$; show $f$ sends non-negatives to non-negatives; deduce $f$ is order-preserving; then squeeze with rationals.

(d) In the light of (c), what is it about $\mathbb{C}$ that lets conjugation exist?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Problem 3(a) already forces the value on $\mathbb{Z}$. For $\mathbb{Q}$, apply $f$ to $q\cdot(1/q) = 1$. (c) Every non-negative real is a square.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) If $f(x) \ne x$, there is a rational strictly between $f(x)$ and $x$; apply order-preservation to it and read off a contradiction.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Conjugation respects both operations and fixes $1$ (Problem 1(d)), and it is its own inverse, so Corollary 4.31 (p. 71) makes it an isomorphism without any separate check of bijectivity.

(b) On $\mathbb{Z}$: Problem 3(a) says there is exactly one homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, and the identity is one, so it is the only one — automorphism or not. On $\mathbb{Q}$: $f$ fixes $\mathbb{Z}$ by the same forcing, and from $q\cdot q^{-1} = 1$ we get $f(q^{-1}) = f(q)^{-1}$, so $f$ fixes every ratio of integers.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be a ring homomorphism. It fixes $\mathbb{Q}$ pointwise, by (b)'s argument verbatim.

*Non-negatives.* If $x \ge 0$ then $x = t^2$ for some real $t$, so $f(x) = f(t)^2 \ge 0$.

*Order.* If $x < y$ then $y - x > 0$, so $f(y) - f(x) = f(y-x) \ge 0$, i.e. $f(x) \le f(y)$.

*Squeeze.* Suppose $f(x) \ne x$ for some $x$. If $f(x) < x$, choose a rational $q$ with $f(x) < q < x$. From $q < x$ and order-preservation, $f(q) \le f(x)$; but $f(q) = q > f(x)$, a contradiction. If $f(x) > x$, choose a rational $q$ with $x < q < f(x)$: then $f(x) \le f(q) = q < f(x)$, again impossible. So $f = \mathrm{id}$.

Note that $f$ was never assumed bijective. Injectivity comes out as a consequence, and this is stronger than (b) in kind, not just in setting.

(d) The proof of (c) turned entirely on $\mathbb{R}$'s order being **definable from the ring operations**: "$x \ge 0$" means "$x$ is a square", and a homomorphism cannot help respecting that. $\mathbb{C}$ has no such definable order — every complex number is a square, so the squares carry no information — and conjugation is free to move $i$ to $-i$ because nothing written in $+$ and $\cdot$ distinguishes the two square roots of $-1$. A field can be rigid or not, and what decides it is how much of the field's structure the arithmetic already encodes.
</details>
