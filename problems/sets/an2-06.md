**One thing is new in this unit, and it is a test, not a theorem about limits.**
A series of functions is by definition (**Definition 6.4.1**) a sequence of
partial sums, so every transfer result from an2-05 applies to series with no new
proof: Abbott's **Theorem 6.4.2** and **Theorem 6.4.3** are each one line long.
What series need that sequences did not is a *usable criterion* for uniform
convergence, and that is the Weierstrass M-test.

**Which book supplies the M-test, and why it matters here.** Both do, and they do
different things with it. Abbott's **Corollary 6.4.5** (p. 168) is a corollary of
his **Theorem 6.4.4**, which he states with no proof and no exercise attached;
6.4.4 is in turn an2-05's **Theorem 6.2.5** in series clothing, whose own proof he
set as Exercise 6.2.6. Lindström's **Lindström's Proposition 4.3.4** (p. 89) states the same
result for functions on an arbitrary set and **proves it in full** from the
completeness of ℝ. So this unit takes the *proof* from Lindström and the
*placement and applications* from Abbott, and says which is which at each use.
(The plan's earlier reason for preferring Abbott — that Lindström only sets the
M-test as an exercise in §4.2 — was false. He sets it there **and** proves the
same result at p. 89.)

**The hook promises two things and only one of them survives Taylor.** "Add
infinitely many functions safely" is §6.4 and §6.5. "Approximate ANY continuous
function by polynomials" is *not* what a Taylor series does — Problem 4 exhibits
Cauchy's infinitely differentiable function whose Taylor series converges
uniformly on all of ℝ to the wrong thing. The promise is redeemed instead by
**Lindström's Theorem 4.10.1** (Lindström, p. 116), which asks for less and gets more.

## Problem 1 (easy — a series is a sequence, and one thing is genuinely new)

(a) State **Definition 6.4.1** and confirm that it defines nothing about series
directly: it defines convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$ to be convergence of
the sequence $s_k = f_1 + \cdots + f_k$, in whichever of the two senses is named.
Then reproduce Abbott's proofs of **Theorem 6.4.2** and **Theorem 6.4.3**, which
are one line each, and say for each exactly which an2-05 result is being applied
and to what.

(b) State **Theorem 6.4.4**, the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence of
series. Show it is an2-05's **Theorem 6.2.5** applied to the sequence $(s_k)$,
by writing $s_n(x) - s_m(x)$ out for $n > m$. Then record what Abbott does with
6.4.4: read p. 168 and say whether he proves it, sets it, or neither.

(c) State the two M-tests side by side — **Corollary 6.4.5** (Abbott, p. 168) and
**Lindström's Proposition 4.3.4** (Lindström, p. 89) — and list the three differences. Two
are in the hypotheses; the third is not in the statement at all.

(d) Prove the M-test, following Lindström. The proof has two halves that are easy
to conflate: first that $\{s_n(x)\}$ converges for each fixed $x$, and second that
the convergence is uniform. Say which property of ℝ the first half uses, and why
the second half needs a *separate* argument rather than following from the first.

(e) Prove Abbott's Exercise 6.4.1: if $\sum_{n=1}^\infty g_n$ converges uniformly
on $A$, then $(g_n)$ converges uniformly to zero on $A$. Then show the M-test's
converse is false: exhibit a series that converges uniformly on $[0,1]$ for which
$\sum_n \sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f_n(x)|$ diverges. (You may quote **Theorem 6.5.4**
from Problem 3 to certify the uniform convergence.)

*(Abbott p. 167 for Definition 6.4.1 and Theorem 6.4.2 with its one-line proof;
p. 168 for Theorem 6.4.3 with proof, Theorem 6.4.4, Corollary 6.4.5 whose proof
is Exercise 6.4.2, and Exercise 6.4.1. Lindström p. 89 for Lindström's Proposition 4.3.4
stated and proved.)*

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) The first half fixes $x$ and works entirely inside ℝ: the partial sums
$S_n = \sum_{k \le n} M_k$ are Cauchy in ℝ, and $|s_m(x) - s_n(x)| \le |S_m - S_n|$
transfers that to $\{s_n(x)\}$. Completeness of ℝ then gives a limit $s(x)$ — one
limit per $x$, which is exactly pointwise convergence and no more.

and the tail $\sum_{k > n} M_k$ supplies one because it was never a function of
$x$ to begin with.

(ii) *The dominating numbers*: Abbott requires $M_n > 0$, Lindström requires the

(b) Abbott neither proves 6.4.4 nor attaches an exercise to it. He writes only
that "the Cauchy Criterion takes the following form" and states it. Its content

as Exercise 6.2.6 (an2-05). So Abbott's route to the M-test passes through two
results neither of which is proved on the page.
</details>

$s_k \to f$ uniformly. Apply **Theorem 6.2.6**: a uniform limit of continuous

## Problem 2 (the M-test in use, and the two places it does not reach)

(a) Lindström's Example 2 (p. 89). Show $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos nx}{n^2}$
converges uniformly on every closed bounded interval, and integrate it term by
term to obtain $\int_0^x f = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin nx}{n^3}$. Name the
result that licenses the term-by-term integration and check its hypotheses.

(b) Abbott's Exercise 6.4.3. Show $g(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \cos(2^n x)/2^n$ is
continuous on all of $\mathbf{R}$, and $h(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} x^n/n^2$ is
continuous on $[-1,1]$. For each, give the $M_n$ explicitly and say which
convergent numerical series you are appealing to.

(c) Abbott's Exercise 6.4.7. Let $h(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^2+n^2}$.
Show $h$ is continuous on $\mathbf{R}$; then show $h$ is differentiable with
continuous derivative. The second half needs the M-test applied to a *different*
series from the one you started with — say which, and where the hypothesis "there
is a point at which the original series converges" is discharged.

(d) Abbott's Exercise 6.4.6. The series $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} x^n/n$
converges at every $x \in [0,1)$ and diverges at $x = 1$. Show it does **not**
converge uniformly on $[0,1)$ by computing $\sup_{x \in [0,1)}$ of the tail
$\sum_{k > m} x^k/k$. Then show $f$ is nevertheless continuous at every point of
$[0,1)$, using the M-test on a well-chosen subinterval. State the general
principle this illustrates in one sentence.

(e) Two series on which the M-test has nothing to say. The first is your example
from Problem 1(e). The second is Lindström's Example 4 from an2-05,
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log(1 + \frac{x}{n^2})$ on $[0,\infty)$. For each, say
whether the M-test applies, whether the series converges uniformly, and what
settles the question when the M-test cannot.

*(Lindström p. 89 for Example 2; p. 88 for Corollary 4.3.3; p. 90 for
Corollary 4.3.6; pp. 90–92 for Example 4. Abbott p. 168 for Exercise 6.4.3;
p. 169 for Exercises 6.4.6 and 6.4.7. All five Abbott items are exercises, set
and not worked.)*

(c) Differentiate under the sum formally first: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{x^2+n^2}
= \frac{-2x}{(x^2+n^2)^2}$. On $|x| \le K$ this is at most $2K/n^4$ in absolute

(d) The tail's supremum over $[0,1)$ is a limit as $x \to 1^-$, and the terms are
all positive.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) $\sup_{x \in [0,1)} \sum_{k>m} x^k/k = \lim_{x \to 1^-} \sum_{k>m} x^k/k
= \sum_{k>m} 1/k = \infty$ for every $m$, so no $m$ makes the tail uniformly
small — uniform convergence fails on $[0,1)$ outright. But continuity is a
*local* property: to prove $f$ continuous at $x_0 \in [0,1)$, pick any $c$ with
$x_0 < c < 1$ and run the M-test on $[0,c]$ with $M_n = c^n/n$.
The principle: uniform convergence is sufficient for continuity but is a global
hypothesis, while continuity is local, so it is enough to have uniform
convergence on *some* neighbourhood of each point.

(c) Apply the M-test twice — once to $\sum 1/(x^2+n^2)$ on all of $\mathbf{R}$
with $M_n = 1/n^2$, and once to the differentiated series on $[-K,K]$ with
$M_n = 2K/n^4$. Then **Theorem 6.4.3** (or **Corollary 4.3.6**) on $[-K,K]$, and
let $K \to \infty$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $|\cos(nx)/n^2| \le 1/n^2$ for every real $x$, and $\sum 1/n^2$ converges, so
by **Lindström's Proposition 4.3.4** the series converges uniformly on all of $\mathbf{R}$,
in particular on any $[a,b]$. Term-by-term integration is **Corollary 4.3.3**,
whose hypotheses are: the $v_n$ continuous (yes, cosines) and the series
uniformly convergent on $[a,b]$ (just shown). Then
$\int_0^x \frac{\cos nt}{n^2}dt = \frac{\sin nx}{n^3}$, and summing gives the
claim. Note Lindström's own statement of Example 2 draws the conclusion only for
"any closed and bounded interval $[a,b]$"; the $M_n = 1/n^2$ he uses in fact
holds on all of $\mathbf{R}$, and the interval is needed for the *integration*
step, not the convergence step.

(e) *First series* ($\sum (-1)^n x^n/n$ on $[0,1]$): the M-test does not apply,
since $\sum \sup |f_n| = \sum 1/n$ diverges; but the series converges uniformly
by **Theorem 6.5.4**. *Second series*: on any bounded $[0,K]$ take $M_n = K/n^2$
and the M-test gives uniform convergence; on all of $[0,\infty)$ it does not
apply, since $\sup_{x \ge 0}\log(1+x/n^2) = \infty$ for every $n$ — and in fact
the convergence is not uniform there either.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) For $g$: $|\cos(2^n x)/2^n| \le 2^{-n} =: M_n$ for all real $x$, and
$\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} = 1$ converges. So $g$ converges uniformly on
$\mathbf{R}$ by the M-test, each term is continuous, and **Theorem 6.4.2** gives
$g$ continuous.

## Problem 3 (power series — what the M-test buys, and the one place it cannot go)

(a) Prove **Theorem 6.5.1**: if $\sum a_n x^n$ converges at $x_0$, it converges
absolutely for every $|x| < |x_0|$. Then explain why this forces the set of

the theorem says nothing about the endpoints.

(b) Prove **Theorem 6.5.2** (Abbott's Exercise 6.5.5): absolute convergence at
$x_0$ gives uniform convergence on $[-c,c]$ with $c = |x_0|$. This is a direct
M-test application — produce the $M_n$.

(c) Now the endpoint. Explain why (b) is useless when the series converges only
*conditionally* at $x = R$, and read Abbott's own explanation of why the triangle

(d) State **Theorem 6.5.6** and **Theorem 6.5.7**. Abbott stresses that to

converges at an endpoint whose differentiated series does not.

(e) Modality audit of §6.5. For each of **Theorem 6.5.1**, **Theorem 6.5.2**,
**Lemma 6.5.3**, **Theorem 6.5.4**, **Theorem 6.5.5**, **Theorem 6.5.6** and

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Boundedness of $(a_n x_0^n)$ is all you need, and it follows from
convergence, not from absolute convergence.
(d) The example is $\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-x)^n/n$ at $x = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Absolute convergence at $x_0$ means $\sum |a_n x_0^n| = \sum |a_n| c^n$
converges. For $|x| \le c$ we have $|a_n x^n| \le |a_n| c^n =: M_n$, and the
M-test applies on $[-c,c]$ with no further work. That is the whole proof — which
is why Abbott can leave it as an exercise.

(c) Conditional convergence at $R$ means $\sum |a_n R^n|$ *diverges*, so the
$M_n = |a_n| R^n$ that (b) would use do not sum. Abbott's remark is that if $A$
bounded the partial sums of $\sum|a_n|$, Lemma 6.5.3 "would be a simple exercise

Summation by parts replaces it.
</details>

</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Suppose $\sum a_n x_0^n$ converges. Then its terms tend to $0$, so the
sequence $(a_n x_0^n)$ is bounded: fix $M > 0$ with $|a_n x_0^n| \le M$ for all
$n$. For $|x| < |x_0|$,
$$|a_n x^n| = |a_n x_0^n|\left|\frac{x}{x_0}\right|^n \le M\left|\frac{x}{x_0}\right|^n,$$
and $\sum M|x/x_0|^n$ is a convergent geometric series since $|x/x_0| < 1$.
Comparison gives absolute convergence.

## Problem 4 (hard — Taylor's series converges, to the wrong function)

(a) Derive Taylor's formula for the coefficients (Abbott's Exercise 6.6.3):
*if* $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n$ on some $(-R,R)$, then
$a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n!$. Supply a reference for each differentiation you perform
under the summation sign. Then state, in one sentence, exactly what has and has
not been established — Abbott is emphatic on this point at p. 178.

(b) State **Theorem 6.6.1** (Lagrange's Remainder Theorem) and verify
**Example 6.6.2**: for $S_5(x) = x - x^3/3! + x^5/5!$ approximating $\sin x$ on
$[-2,2]$, the bound is $|E_5(x)| \le 2^6/6!$. Compute that number to three
decimals. Then prove (Exercise 6.6.5) that $S_N \to \sin$ uniformly on $[-R,R]$
for every $R$, saying which hypothesis of the epilogue's criterion $\sin$
satisfies.

(c) Cauchy's counterexample. Let
$$g(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & x \ne 0\\ 0 & x = 0.\end{cases}$$
Show that for $x \ne 0$ the $n$-th derivative has the form
$g^{(n)}(x) = e^{-1/x^2}P_n(x)/x^{N_n}$ with $P_n$ a polynomial, and that
$g^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for every $n$. Conclude that the Taylor series of $g$ at $0$ is
identically zero, that it therefore converges uniformly on all of $\mathbf{R}$,
and that it equals $g$ at exactly one point.

(d) Now turn Lagrange's theorem on the counterexample. **Theorem 6.6.1** is true
of $g$ — it is a theorem — so for each $N$ and each $x \ne 0$ there is a $c$ with
$|c| < |x|$ and $g(x) = g^{(N+1)}(c)x^{N+1}/(N+1)!$. Abbott's epilogue (p. 181)
gives a sufficient condition for $E_N \to 0$: a single bound $M$ with
$|f^{(N+1)}(c)| \le M$ for all $c \in (-R,R)$ and all $N$. Deduce, **without
computing a single derivative of $g$**, that no such $M$ exists for $g$ on any
$(-R,R)$.

(e) Record the modality, in both books. Abbott presents the counterexample as
running text and sets every step as Exercises 6.6.9–6.6.12; Lindström sets the
same object as Lindström's Exercise 3 to §4.10 (p. 122). Say what a lesson may and may not
claim about this example on that evidence, and name the historical attribution
Abbott gives at p. 181, with its date.

*(Abbott p. 177 for Exercises 6.6.3 and 6.6.4; pp. 178–180 for Theorem 6.6.1,
whose proof begins on p. 179 and is completed by Exercises 6.6.7 and 6.6.8;
p. 179 for Example 6.6.2 and Exercise 6.6.5; p. 180 for the counterexample and
Exercises 6.6.9–6.6.12; p. 181 for the epilogue and the attribution.
Lindström p. 122 for Lindström's Exercise 3 to §4.10.)*

(c) For $g^{(n)}(0)$, use induction with the limit
$g^{(n+1)}(0) = \lim_{x \to 0} g^{(n)}(x)/x$ and the fact that $e^{-1/x^2}$ beats

</details>

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) Taylor: *there is one sequence $(a_n)$ such that for all $x$, the partial
sums $S_N(x) = \sum_{n\le N} a_n x^n$ converge to $f(x)$* — and each $S_N$ is
obtained from $S_{N-1}$ by appending a term, so the family is nested and
determined by $f$'s derivatives at a single point. Weierstrass: *there is a
sequence of polynomials $(p_n)$, subject to no relation whatever between $p_n$
and $p_{n+1}$, converging uniformly to $f$.* And Taylor requires $f$ infinitely
differentiable, where Weierstrass requires only continuity — which is
Lindström's stated motive: "Most continuous functions are not differentiable at
all."

(e) Lindström's Lemma 4.11.3 applies Weierstrass' Theorem on $C([-N,N],\mathbf{R})$ to the
absolute-value function $t \mapsto |t|$, to approximate it by a polynomial and so
put $|f|$ into the closed algebra $\mathcal{A}$. So the general theorem's proof
*consumes* the special one.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) The single property of $[0,1]$ is compactness. It gives uniform continuity of

The choice $N \ge M/(\delta^2\epsilon)$ yields
$M/(2\delta^2 n) \le M/(2\delta^2 N) = \epsilon/2$ for $n \ge N$ — an equality at

(d) $1/x$ on $(0,1)$ fails **boundedness of the domain's closure inside the

domain. Both failures are failures of the hypothesis "$[a,b]$" — that is, of
compactness of the domain, which is an2-03's subject and, by the
Heine–Borel description there, is exactly closed *and* bounded for subsets of
$\mathbf{R}$.

<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(d) *No polynomials converge uniformly to $e^x$ on $\mathbf{R}$.* Suppose
$p_n \to e^x$ uniformly on $\mathbf{R}$. Then $(p_n)$ is uniformly Cauchy, so
there is $N$ with $\sup_{\mathbf{R}}|p_n - p_N| \le 1$ for all $n \ge N$. But
$p_n - p_N$ is a polynomial bounded on all of $\mathbf{R}$, hence constant, say
$p_n = p_N + c_n$. The constants $c_n$ converge (evaluate at $x = 0$), say to
$c$, and therefore $e^x = p_N(x) + c$ for every $x$ — so $e^x$ is a polynomial.
It is not: a polynomial of degree $d$ has vanishing $(d+1)$-st derivative, while
$(e^x)^{(k)} = e^x$ is never identically zero.
