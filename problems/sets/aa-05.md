## Problem 1 (easy — reading Definition 3.1 as a checklist)

(a) Verify that $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies every clause of Definition 3.1 (p. 41), saying for each one what has to be checked and why it holds. You may quote Example 3.4 (p. 42) for the general statement, but do the checking here.

(b) Aluffi's Example 3.5 (p. 42) offers $5\mathbb{Z}$ as a **non**-example. Identify the single clause of Definition 3.1 that fails, and confirm that every other clause holds.

(c) Give a second structure failing exactly one clause of Definition 3.1, and name the clause.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Go through the clauses in order: closure, associativity and commutativity of $+$, the additive identity, additive inverses, associativity of $\cdot$, the multiplicative identity, distributivity.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $5\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under both operations and inherits every identity that is an equation. Which clause is *not* an equation in the elements you already have?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $5\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under $+$ and $\cdot$ (a product of multiples of $5$ is a multiple of $25$, hence of $5$), contains $0$, has additive inverses, and inherits associativity, commutativity of $+$ and distributivity from $\mathbb{Z}$. What it lacks is a multiplicative identity: $1 \notin 5\mathbb{Z}$, and no element of $5\mathbb{Z}$ can act as one, since $5m\cdot 5 = 5$ would force $5m = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Take $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ with the usual operations. Every clause holds — including the multiplicative identity, which $5\mathbb{Z}$ lacked — except the existence of additive inverses: $1$ has no $z$ with $1 + z = 0$.

The two non-examples fail *different* clauses, which is the point of asking for a second one. A definition with seven clauses is worth nothing until you have seen each clause fail on its own; otherwise you are carrying around a list of conditions you cannot tell apart. Aluffi calls $5\mathbb{Z}$ "a ring without identity" and keeps it in view, so the failure is not a disqualification so much as a different structure.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — what uniqueness proofs look like)

(a) Prove Proposition 3.14 (p. 48): the additive identity of a ring is unique, and so is the multiplicative identity.

(b) Prove Proposition 3.15 (p. 48): each $a \in R$ has exactly one additive inverse.

(c) Now prove: if $a \in R$ has a multiplicative inverse, it has only one. Compare your three proofs and say what they have in common.

(d) In (c), did you need multiplication to be commutative? Check your proof line by line.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Every one of these is the same two-line trick: take two objects with the property, and evaluate one expression in two ways.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), evaluate $0' + 0''$ using first that $0'$ is an identity and then that $0''$ is. For (c), evaluate $b'ab''$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $0' = 0' + 0'' = 0''$, the first equality because $0''$ is an additive identity and the second because $0'$ is. The multiplicative case is $1' = 1'\cdot 1'' = 1''$, identically.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) If $a + z' = 0$ and $a + z'' = 0$ then $z' = z' + 0 = z' + (a + z'') = (z' + a) + z'' = 0 + z'' = z''$, using associativity once.

(c) If $b'a = ab' = 1$ and $b''a = ab'' = 1$ then $b' = b'\cdot 1 = b'(ab'') = (b'a)b'' = 1\cdot b'' = b''$.

(d) No: the proof used $b'a = 1$ on the left and $ab'' = 1$ on the right, and never needed to swap a product. This matters because Definition 3.1 (p. 41) does not require commutativity. Example 3.9 (p. 44), the $2\times 2$ real matrices, is a ring in which it fails, and a proof that quietly assumes it is a proof about a smaller class of rings than it claims. What all three arguments have in common is that they never construct anything: each takes two candidates and writes one expression that both must evaluate.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the facts about $0$ that are not axioms)

(a) Prove Proposition 3.17 (p. 50): in any ring, $a + b = a + c$ implies $b = c$.

(b) Prove Corollary 3.18 (p. 51): $0\cdot a = a\cdot 0 = 0$ for every $a$ in every ring. Which axioms does your proof use?

(c) Prove $(-a)b = -(ab)$ and $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

(d) Definition 3.1 says nothing about how $0$ interacts with multiplication. Explain why adding "$0 \cdot a = 0$" as an eighth clause would be harmless but pointless, and what it would cost.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Start from $0 + 0 = 0$ and multiply by $a$, then use distributivity and (a).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) For the first, show $ab + (-a)b = 0$ by factoring; uniqueness of additive inverses (Proposition 3.15, p. 48) then identifies $(-a)b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Add the additive inverse of $a$ to both sides on the left: $-a + (a+b) = -a + (a+c)$, then regroup by associativity to get $0 + b = 0 + c$, i.e. $b = c$.

(b) $a\cdot 0 = a\cdot(0+0) = a\cdot 0 + a\cdot 0$ by distributivity. Also $a \cdot 0 = a\cdot 0 + 0$. Cancel $a \cdot 0$ using (a): $0 = a\cdot 0$. The other side is identical with the other distributive law. Only distributivity, the additive identity, and cancellation are used — no clause about multiplication by $0$, because there is none.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))b = 0\cdot b = 0$ by (b), so $(-a)b$ is *an* additive inverse of $ab$, and by Proposition 3.15 it is *the* one: $(-a)b = -(ab)$. Then $(-a)(-b) = -(a(-b)) = -(-(ab)) = ab$, the last step because $ab$ is an additive inverse of $-(ab)$ and inverses are unique.

(d) It would be harmless because it is true in every ring, so no ring is lost. It would be pointless because it is already a consequence, so no ring is gained. What it would cost is the ability to notice that it *is* a consequence: an axiom system carries information in what it omits, and a reader checking whether some structure is a ring would have one more thing to verify and one less thing to understand. The same is true of the whole of §3.2 — every statement there is a fact about all rings that the definition did not have to mention.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the ring with one element)

(a) Show that the one-element set $R = \{0\}$, with the only possible operations, satisfies Definition 3.1 (p. 41). What is $1_R$?

(b) Prove that in a ring $R$, $1_R = 0_R$ if and only if $R = \{0_R\}$.

(c) Aluffi's Definition 3.1 does not exclude this ring. Say what would have to be added to exclude it, and give one reason a book might prefer not to.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) One direction is (a). For the other, take any $a \in R$ and compute $a = 1\cdot a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use Corollary 3.18 (p. 51) to evaluate $0\cdot a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Every clause is an equation between elements of a one-element set, so every clause holds. Necessarily $1_R = 0_R$, the only element there is.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) If $1 = 0$ then for any $a \in R$, $a = 1\cdot a = 0\cdot a = 0$ by Corollary 3.18, so $R = \{0\}$.

(c) To exclude it one would add the clause $1_R \ne 0_R$. A book might decline because the zero ring is the terminal object of the theory and turns up naturally — it is $\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z}$, which Example 3.4 (p. 42) already supplies — and because excluding it forces a case split into every later construction: a quotient of a ring, or a product over an empty index set, would have to be checked for nontriviality before it could be called a ring at all. The convention costs one degenerate object and saves a hypothesis in a dozen theorems.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — new rings from old)

(a) Let $R$ and $S$ be rings. Show that $R \times S$, with operations defined componentwise, is a ring, and identify $0_{R\times S}$ and $1_{R\times S}$.

(b) Show that if $R$ and $S$ are both nonzero rings then $R \times S$ has zero-divisors — elements $x, y \ne 0$ with $xy = 0$ — no matter how well behaved $R$ and $S$ are.

(c) Let $A$ be any set and $R$ a ring. Show that the set $R^A$ of all functions $A \to R$, with pointwise operations, is a ring. What are its identity elements, and when does it have zero-divisors?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) and (c) are both "check each clause, componentwise or pointwise" — the content is in noticing that each clause only ever involves finitely many elements at a time.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) The obvious candidates are $(1_R, 0_S)$ and $(0_R, 1_S)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $0_{R\times S} = (0_R, 0_S)$ and $1_{R\times S} = (1_R, 1_S)$. Every clause holds because it holds in each coordinate and the operations are defined coordinatewise.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Put $x = (1_R, 0_S)$ and $y = (0_R, 1_S)$. Then $xy = (0_R, 0_S) = 0$. Both are nonzero: $x \ne 0$ requires $1_R \ne 0_R$, which is Problem 4(b) together with $R \ne \{0\}$, and likewise for $y$. So the product of two fields — as clean a pair of rings as exists — already has zero-divisors, and "no zero-divisors" is not a property that survives products.

(c) Pointwise operations make each clause a statement checked at one point of $A$ at a time, so $R^A$ is a ring with $0$ the constant function $0_R$ and $1$ the constant function $1_R$. It has zero-divisors as soon as $|A| \ge 2$ and $R \ne \{0\}$: take $f$ supported on one point and $g$ on another, and $fg$ vanishes everywhere. In fact $R^A \cong R \times R^{A \setminus \{a\}}$ for any $a \in A$, so (c) contains (b).
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — a ring where $a^2 = a$ has no choice but to be commutative)

Call a ring $R$ **Boolean** if $a^2 = a$ for every $a \in R$. (The ring $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is one; so is $R^A$ for $R = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and any set $A$, by Problem 5(c).)

Let $R$ be Boolean.

(a) Prove that $a + a = 0$ for every $a \in R$.

(b) Prove that $ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in R$.

(c) Where in your argument did you use that $R$ is Boolean *for every* element, rather than for the particular elements $a$ and $b$ you started with?

(d) Give a Boolean ring with more than two elements, and one nonzero ring in which $a^2 = a$ holds for some $a \ne 0, 1$ but which is not Boolean.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Expand $(a+b)^2$ and use $(a+b)^2 = a+b$. Then try $b = a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Expanding gives $a^2 + ab + ba + b^2 = a + b$; cancel with Proposition 3.17 (p. 50) to get a relation between $ab$ and $ba$. Then get (a) out of the special case $b = a$, and feed (a) back in.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(a+b)^2 = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2$, using both distributive laws and nothing else. Since $R$ is Boolean this equals $a + b$, and $a^2 + b^2 = a + b$, so cancelling (Proposition 3.17) leaves
$$ab + ba = 0.$$
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Put $b = a$ in that relation: $a^2 + a^2 = 0$, i.e. $a + a = 0$. So every element is its own additive inverse, and $-x = x$ throughout $R$.

(b) From $ab + ba = 0$ we get $ab = -(ba)$, and by (a) $-(ba) = ba$. Hence $ab = ba$.

(c) At the very first step. The hypothesis was applied to $a + b$ — an element neither of the two you were given — and again to $a$ and to $b$ separately. A version of the hypothesis restricted to two fixed elements would yield nothing: the whole argument runs on the freedom to square a sum.

(d) $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$ is Boolean with four elements: $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)$, each equal to its own square. For the second, take $R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, where $[3]^2 = [9] = [3]$ and $[4]^2 = [16] = [4]$, yet $[2]^2 = [4] \ne [2]$, so $R$ is not Boolean. Note $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ *is* commutative — (b) is a sufficient condition for commutativity, not a characterisation of it.
</details>
