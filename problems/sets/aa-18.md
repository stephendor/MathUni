## Problem 1 (easy–medium — independent, generating, both)

(a) State Definition 9.3 (p. 175) and Definition 9.5 (p. 175), and prove Proposition 9.6 (p. 175): $F$ is free of rank $m$ if and only if it has a basis of $m$ elements.

(b) In $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: show $\{2,3\}$ generates but is not independent, and $\{2\}$ is independent but does not generate.

(c) Prove Example 9.7 (p. 176): $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module.

(d) More generally, show that a nonzero finite abelian group is never free.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) $3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 0$ is a nontrivial relation.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) and (d): a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $m\ge1$ is infinite, and of rank $0$ is trivial.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $F\cong R^{\oplus m}$, transport the standard tuple $(e_1,\dots,e_m)$ across the isomorphism: it generates because the $e_i$ do, and it is independent because $\sum r_ie_i = 0$ forces every $r_i=0$ coordinatewise. Conversely a basis $(x_1,\dots,x_m)$ gives a homomorphism $R^{\oplus m}\to F$, $(r_i)\mapsto\sum r_ix_i$, surjective by generation and injective by independence.

(b) $\{2,3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$ since $3-2=1$; it is dependent since $3\cdot2 - 2\cdot3 = 0$ with coefficients $3,-2$ not both zero. $\{2\}$ is independent — $r\cdot2 = 0$ forces $r=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ — and generates only $2\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Note what (b) rules out: over a field, any generating set contains a basis and any independent set extends to one. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\{2,3\}$ generates and contains no basis — neither $\{2\}$ nor $\{3\}$ generates, and the pair is dependent — and $\{2\}$ is independent and extends to no basis. Both halves of the linear-algebra picture fail at once.

(c) Suppose $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m}$. If $m=0$ the right side is trivial and the left has two elements. If $m\ge1$ the right side is infinite and the left is finite. So no $m$ works. Equivalently and more usefully: in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ every element $x$ satisfies $2x=0$, so no single element is independent, so there is no basis at all — Example 9.10 (p. 176) is this argument.

(d) Same reason. If $A$ is finite and nonzero, take $0\ne a\in A$; the elements $a, 2a, 3a,\dots$ cannot all be distinct, so $ma = na$ for some $m<n$, giving $(n-m)a = 0$ with $n-m\ne0$. So no nonzero element is independent, no basis exists, and $A$ is not free. **Torsion is the obstruction**, and identifying exactly how much of a module it obstructs is what aa-20 does.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the universal property of a free module)

(a) Prove Proposition 9.8 (p. 176): for any $R$-module $M$ and any $a_1,\dots,a_m\in M$ there is exactly one homomorphism $f : R^{\oplus m}\to M$ with $f(e_i)=a_i$.

(b) Restate (a) as a bijection between two sets, and say which sets.

(c) Deduce aa-17 Problem 4(a): $M$ is generated by $m$ elements if and only if some $f : R^{\oplus m}\to M$ is surjective.

(d) Compare with aa-15 Problem 4(b). Which case of (a) is it?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Uniqueness: every element of $R^{\oplus m}$ is $\sum r_ie_i$. Existence: define $f$ by that formula and check.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) Homomorphisms out of the free module, against tuples of elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) *Uniqueness.* $(r_1,\dots,r_m) = \sum_i r_ie_i$, so $f(r_1,\dots,r_m) = \sum_i r_if(e_i) = \sum_i r_ia_i$ is forced. *Existence.* That formula is additive — sums add coefficientwise — and $R$-linear, since $f(r\cdot(r_i)) = \sum(rr_i)a_i = r\sum r_ia_i$.

(b) The map $f\mapsto(f(e_1),\dots,f(e_m))$ is a bijection
$$\operatorname{Hom}_R(R^{\oplus m},M)\ \longleftrightarrow\ M^m.$$
"Free on $m$ generators" means exactly this: choosing where the generators go is choosing the map, with no conditions to satisfy.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Given generators $a_1,\dots,a_m$ of $M$, (a) supplies $f$ with $f(e_i)=a_i$, and its image contains every combination $\sum r_ia_i$, hence is all of $M$. Conversely if $f$ is surjective then the $f(e_i)$ generate, since every $m\in M$ is $f(\sum r_ie_i) = \sum r_if(e_i)$.

(d) The case $m=1$: $\operatorname{Hom}_R(R,M)\leftrightarrow M$, which is aa-15 Problem 4(b) — a homomorphism $R\to M$ is determined by $f(1)$, and $f(1)$ is unconstrained. The contrast with rings is the same one as there: a *ring* homomorphism $R\to S$ must send $1$ to $1$, so there is nothing free about it, and $\mathbb{Z}$ — not $R$ — is the ring that has this property, with $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{ring}}(\mathbb{Z},S)$ a single point rather than a copy of $S$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — homomorphisms of free modules are matrices)

(a) Prove Corollary 9.11 (p. 177): the homomorphisms $R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}$ correspond to $m\times n$ matrices over $R$, with the $j$th column of the matrix equal to $f(e_j)$.

(b) Prove Proposition 9.14 (p. 179): composition of homomorphisms corresponds to multiplication of matrices.

(c) Write down the matrix of $f : \mathbb{Z}^{\oplus3}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ with $f(e_1)=(1,2)$, $f(e_2)=(0,-1)$, $f(e_3)=(3,3)$, and compute $\operatorname{im} f$. Compare Example 9.13 (p. 178).

(d) Explain why (b) makes matrix multiplication associative without any computation.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is Problem 2(a) with $M = R^{\oplus m}$, plus the observation that an element of $R^{\oplus m}$ is a column.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Composition of functions is associative for free.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By Problem 2(a), $f$ is determined by the $n$ elements $f(e_1),\dots,f(e_n)$ of $R^{\oplus m}$, and each is an $m$-tuple. Arranging them as columns gives an $m\times n$ matrix, and the correspondence is a bijection because the tuple was unconstrained.

(b) Let $f : R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}$ and $g : R^{\oplus m}\to R^{\oplus\ell}$ have matrices $A$ and $B$. Then $(g\circ f)(e_j) = g(Ae_j) = B(Ae_j) = (BA)e_j$, so the matrix of $g\circ f$ is $BA$ — the columns agree, and by (a) that determines the matrix.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The matrix is
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&3\\2&-1&3\end{pmatrix}.$$
Its image is generated by the three columns. Since $(1,2)$ and $(0,-1)$ already generate $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ — $(1,0) = (1,2)+2(0,-1)$ and $(0,1) = -(0,-1)$ — the image is all of $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$, and the third column is redundant. So $f$ is surjective, and by Theorem 8.44 (p. 168), $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus3}/\ker f$.

(d) Composition of functions is associative: $(h\circ g)\circ f = h\circ(g\circ f)$ as maps. By (b) the matrix of each side is $(CB)A$ and $C(BA)$ respectively, and by (a) the matrix is determined by the map. So $(CB)A = C(BA)$, with no index manipulation anywhere. The usual proof by summation is a verification that the correspondence in (a) is what it claims to be; once that is known, associativity is inherited.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — exact sequences)

(a) State Definition 9.17 (p. 182) and prove: $0\to M\xrightarrow{f}N$ is exact if and only if $f$ is injective, and $M\xrightarrow{f}N\to0$ is exact if and only if $f$ is surjective.

(b) Prove that $0\to M\xrightarrow{f}N\xrightarrow{g}P\to0$ is exact if and only if $f$ is injective, $g$ is surjective, and $g$ induces an isomorphism $N/\operatorname{im} f\cong P$. Compare Example 9.20 (p. 182).

(c) Define $\operatorname{coker} f$ as in Definition 9.21 (p. 183) and restate (b) using it.

(d) Show $0\to\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to0$ is exact, and that it does **not** split — there is no isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Exactness at $M$ means the image of $0\to M$ equals $\ker f$, i.e. $\ker f = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Compare torsion, as in aa-15 Problem 5(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The image of the map $0\to M$ is $\{0\}$, so exactness at $M$ says $\ker f = \{0\}$, i.e. $f$ injective. The kernel of $N\to 0$ is all of $N$, so exactness at $N$ says $\operatorname{im} f = N$, i.e. $f$ surjective.

(b) Exactness at $M$ and at $P$ give injectivity and surjectivity by (a). Exactness at $N$ says $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$, and then Theorem 8.44 (p. 168) applied to the surjection $g$ gives $P\cong N/\ker g = N/\operatorname{im} f$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\operatorname{coker} f = N/\operatorname{im} f$, so (b) reads: a short exact sequence exhibits $M$ as a submodule of $N$ (via $f$) and $P$ as its cokernel. Every short exact sequence is, up to isomorphism, a submodule and its quotient.

(d) Multiplication by $2$ is injective on $\mathbb{Z}$; its image is $2\mathbb{Z}$; and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient. So the sequence is exact.

It does not split. If $\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ then the right side has a nonzero element killed by $2$ and the left side does not, and that property is preserved by isomorphism. So a short exact sequence need not present its middle term as a direct sum of the outer two — which is why aa-20's structure theorem is a theorem rather than an observation, and why the free case in Problem 6 has to be argued.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — a matrix presents a module)

Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix over a commutative ring $R$, and let $f_A : R^{\oplus n}\to R^{\oplus m}$ be the corresponding homomorphism.

(a) Show $\operatorname{coker} f_A = R^{\oplus m}/\langle\text{columns of }A\rangle$.

(b) Compute the cokernel over $\mathbb{Z}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$, for $A = \begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}$, and for $A = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

(c) State Definition 9.15 (p. 181) and prove the easy half of Proposition 9.25 (p. 184): if $A' = QAP$ with $P,Q$ invertible over $R$, then $\operatorname{coker} f_{A'}\cong\operatorname{coker} f_A$.

(d) Use (c) to redo (b)'s second and third cases in one step, and say which invertible matrices you used.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The image of $f_A$ is generated by $f_A(e_j)$, which is the $j$th column.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $Q$ is an isomorphism of the target and $P$ of the source; an isomorphism of the target carries the image of one map onto the image of the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\operatorname{im} f_A$ is generated by the $f_A(e_j)$, and by Corollary 9.11 (p. 177) those are the columns of $A$. So the cokernel is $R^{\oplus m}$ modulo the submodule they generate.

(b) $\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$: columns $(2,0)$ and $(0,3)$ generate $2\mathbb{Z}\oplus3\mathbb{Z}$, so the cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ — aa-17 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) $A = \binom22$: the cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}/\langle(2,2)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — aa-17 Problem 6(c). $A = \binom11$: the cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}/\langle(1,1)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}$ — aa-17 Problem 6(b).

(c) Definition 9.15 calls $A$ and $A'$ equivalent when $A' = QAP$ with $P, Q$ invertible over $R$. Then $f_{A'} = f_Q\circ f_A\circ f_P$ by Proposition 9.14 (p. 179), with $f_P$ and $f_Q$ isomorphisms. Since $f_P$ is onto, $\operatorname{im}(f_A\circ f_P) = \operatorname{im} f_A$; and $f_Q$ carries that submodule isomorphically onto $\operatorname{im} f_{A'}$, so it induces an isomorphism of the quotients $R^{\oplus m}/\operatorname{im} f_A\to R^{\oplus m}/\operatorname{im} f_{A'}$.

(d) $\binom22 = \binom11\cdot(2)$, and $(2)$ is *not* invertible over $\mathbb{Z}$ — which is exactly why the two cokernels differ. What *is* legitimate is
$$Q = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q\binom11 = \binom10,\qquad Q\binom22 = \binom20,$$
with $\det Q = 1$. So the two cokernels are $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}/\langle(1,0)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}/\langle(2,0)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$, recovering both answers with one row operation. Remark 9.16 (p. 181) warns that row and column operations do not capture equivalence over every ring; over $\mathbb{Z}$ they do, and aa-20 turns that into the structure theorem.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — submodules of free modules)

(a) Prove that every submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$ is free of rank $0$ or $1$.

(b) Prove that every submodule of $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$ is free of rank at most $2$.

(c) Show the corresponding statement fails over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: exhibit a submodule of the free rank-1 module $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ that is not free.

(d) What property of $\mathbb{Z}$ does (a) and (b) rest on, and what does (c) show about it?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Project onto the second coordinate. The kernel of the projection restricted to $N$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}\oplus0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) In a commutative ring any two elements $a,b$ satisfy $b\cdot a - a\cdot b = 0$, so no generating set of size $\ge 2$ can be independent.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal, hence $d\mathbb{Z}$ by aa-09 Problem 6(a). If $d=0$ it is the zero module, free of rank $0$. If $d\ne0$ then $\{d\}$ is a basis: it generates, and $rd=0$ forces $r=0$.

(b) Let $N\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus2}$ and let $\pi$ be the second projection. Then $\pi(N)$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$, so $\pi(N) = c\mathbb{Z}$, and $N_0 = N\cap(\mathbb{Z}\oplus0)$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}\oplus 0\cong\mathbb{Z}$, so $N_0 = \mathbb{Z}\cdot u$ for some $u$ (or is zero).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) If $c = 0$ then $N = N_0$ and we are done by (a). Otherwise pick $v\in N$ with $\pi(v) = c$. For any $n\in N$, $\pi(n) = kc$ for some $k$, so $n - kv\in\ker\pi\cap N = N_0$. Hence $N = N_0 + \mathbb{Z}v$. And the sum is direct: if $au + bv = 0$ then applying $\pi$ gives $bc = 0$, so $b=0$, and then $au=0$ gives $a=0$ (or $u=0$, in which case $N = \mathbb{Z}v$ is free of rank 1). So $N$ is free of rank $2$, or $1$, or $0$.

(c) Take $I = (2,x)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[x]$, a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ by aa-16 Problem 1(c). It is not cyclic, since $(2,x)$ is not a principal ideal — aa-11 Problem 6(c) — so it is not free of rank $\le1$. And it is not free of rank $\ge2$ either: any two elements $a, b$ of a commutative ring satisfy the nontrivial relation $b\cdot a + (-a)\cdot b = 0$, with coefficients $b$ and $-a$ not both zero, so no two elements of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ are independent. So $I$ has no basis of any size and is not free.

(d) Both (a) and (b) rest on $\mathbb{Z}$ being a **PID** — every submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $d\mathbb{Z}$, generated by one element, and (b) bootstraps from that one coordinate at a time. The general theorem is that over a PID a submodule of a free module of rank $m$ is free of rank at most $m$, and (c) shows the hypothesis is not decoration: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD, and even a Noetherian ring, and still has a rank-1 free module with a non-free submodule. Being a PID is what aa-20's structure theorem will require, and this is the first place the requirement bites.
</details>
