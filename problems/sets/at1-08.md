## Problem 1 (easy — degree from the definition)

(a) Hatcher defines $\deg f$ for $f : S^n \to S^n$, $n > 0$, by the equation
$f_*(\alpha) = (\deg f)\alpha$ on $H_n(S^n)$. Say exactly which two facts make
this well defined — one about the group $H_n(S^n)$, one about $f_*$ — and why
the definition would fail for $n = 0$.
(b) Prove properties (a) and (d): $\deg \mathbb{1} = 1$, and
$\deg(fg) = \deg f \cdot \deg g$. Each is one line from functoriality. Then
deduce that a homotopy equivalence has degree $\pm 1$.
(c) Prove property (b): if $f$ is not surjective then $\deg f = 0$. Hatcher's
argument factors $f$ through $S^n - \{x_0\}$; say why that subspace has
$H_n = 0$.
(d) Property (c) says $f \simeq g \Rightarrow \deg f = \deg g$. State the
converse, say why it is *not* immediate, and name where Hatcher proves it.
(e) The antipodal map has degree $(-1)^{n+1}$ (property (f)). Using only that
and property (a), decide for which $n$ the antipodal map of $S^n$ is homotopic
to the identity — and say what you have *not* thereby proved when the degrees
agree.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) "Infinite cyclic" is doing the work in one of the two facts; the other is
that $f_*$ is a homomorphism. For $n = 0$, compute $H_0(S^0)$.
(c) $S^n$ minus a point is contractible — that is Hatcher's reason, and it is
the whole of the argument.
(e) Property (c) runs in one direction only. That is the point of (d).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $H_n(S^n)$ is infinite cyclic, so every endomorphism of it is multiplication
by a unique integer; and $f_*$ is an endomorphism because $f$ maps $S^n$ to
itself. Uniqueness of the integer is what makes $\deg f$ well defined. For
$n = 0$, $S^0$ is two points and $H_0(S^0) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$,
which is not infinite cyclic, so "multiplication by $d$" is not forced.
(b) $\mathbb{1}_* = \mathbb{1}$ gives $\deg \mathbb{1} = 1$; $(fg)_* = f_*g_*$
gives $\deg fg = \deg f \deg g$. If $f$ is a homotopy equivalence with inverse
$g$ then $fg \simeq \mathbb{1}$, so $\deg f \deg g = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, forcing
$\deg f = \pm 1$. ⟨your step: say why $\deg f \deg g = 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}$
forces $\pm 1$ and not merely "nonzero".⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Every part of this problem is functoriality plus one fact about $H_n(S^n)$.
That is worth noticing: degree is not a new construction, it is a *reading* of
$f_*$ made possible by the accident that the target group is $\mathbb{Z}$. In
at1-10 the same reading will be unavailable and induced maps will have to be
handled as maps.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — the hairy ball theorem and free actions)

(a) State Theorem 2.28. Then reconstruct Hatcher's forward direction: given a
nowhere-zero tangent vector field $v$ on $S^n$, normalise it and write down the
homotopy $f_t(x) = (\cos t)x + (\sin t)v(x)$. Check three things: that
$f_t(x) \in S^n$ for all $t$, that $f_0 = \mathbb{1}$, and that $f_\pi$ is the
antipodal map. Then finish with degree.
(b) Where in (a) did you use that $v(x)$ is *orthogonal* to $x$? Give the
computation, not a gesture.
(c) Give Hatcher's converse: for $n = 2k - 1$ odd, write down an explicit
nowhere-zero tangent field and verify orthogonality and unit length.
(d) State Proposition 2.29 and reproduce the proof. Identify the exact point
where "$n$ even" is used, and say what the proposition asserts — and does not
assert — when $n$ is odd.
(e) Example 1.43 asked which groups act freely on $S^n$. Say precisely how much
of that question Proposition 2.29 answers.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) $|f_t(x)|^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t$ *only if* the cross term vanishes.
(d) The degree function $d : G \to \{\pm 1\}$ is a homomorphism by property (d)
of degree, and property (g) evaluates it on every non-trivial element.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $|f_t(x)|^2 = \cos^2 t\,|x|^2 + 2\cos t \sin t \langle x, v(x)\rangle
+ \sin^2 t\,|v(x)|^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$, using $|x| = |v(x)| = 1$ and
$\langle x, v(x)\rangle = 0$. $f_0(x) = x$ and $f_\pi(x) = -x$. So
$\mathbb{1} \simeq -\mathbb{1}$, whence $1 = (-1)^{n+1}$ and $n$ is odd.
(d) $d$ is a homomorphism $G \to \{\pm 1\}$; freeness plus property (g) sends
every non-trivial $g$ to $(-1)^{n+1}$. For $n$ even that is $-1$, so
$\ker d$ is trivial and $G$ embeds in $\mathbb{Z}_2$. ⟨your step: for $n$ odd
the same computation sends everything to $+1$, so $d$ is useless — say what
that leaves open, and check your answer against the lens spaces of
Example 2.43.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Both results are the same trick twice: build a homotopy or a homomorphism whose
existence forces an equation between degrees, then read off the parity. Notice
that neither proof ever computes a homology group. Degree is being used purely
as a $\mathbb{Z}$-valued obstruction.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — local degree)

(a) Set up the local degree. For $f : S^n \to S^n$ with $f^{-1}(y)$ finite, say
what $\deg f|x_i$ is, and identify which of the four isomorphisms in Hatcher's
diagram come from excision and which from long exact sequences of pairs. Both
tools are §2.1's; name them.
(b) Prove Proposition 2.30, $\deg f = \sum_i \deg f|x_i$, following Hatcher.
The one step worth slowing down on is $j(1) = \sum_i k_i(1)$: explain why
commutativity of the *lower* triangle gives $p_i j(1) = 1$ for every $i$, and
why that pins $j(1)$ down.
(c) Example 2.32: show $\deg(z \mapsto z^k) = k$ on $S^1$ for $k > 0$ by local
degrees, and then handle $k < 0$ and $k = 0$ as Hatcher does.
(d) Example 2.31 constructs a map $S^n \to S^n$ of any prescribed degree by
collapsing the complement of $k$ disjoint balls. Describe the map, say why each
local degree is $\pm 1$, and say how the signs are chosen.
(e) State Proposition 2.33 and say why it gives a *second* construction of a
degree-$k$ map $S^n \to S^n$ for every $n \geq 1$. Then answer Hatcher's
closing remark: why does the suspension argument show that the *local* degree
of a map $S^n \to S^n$ can be any integer once $n \geq 2$, when the global
degree already could be any integer for $n \geq 1$?

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) $p_i k_j = 0$ for $j \neq i$ because that composition factors through
$H_n(U_j, U_j)$, which is zero.
(e) Look at what $Sf$ does to the two poles of $S^{n+1}$: exactly one preimage
each.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The two upper isomorphisms are excision (Theorem 2.20, §2.1), cutting down
from $S^n$ to a neighbourhood; the two lower ones come from the long exact
sequence of a pair (Theorem 2.16, §2.1) together with the contractibility of
$S^n$ minus a point. Under all four, both top groups become
$H_n(S^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and the top $f_*$ is then multiplication by an
integer.
(c) For $k > 0$ and any $y \in S^1$, $f^{-1}(y)$ is $k$ points, near each of
which $f$ is a local homeomorphism stretching an arc by a factor $k$. The
stretching is removable by a deformation that does not change local degree, so
each local degree equals that of a rotation, which is $+1$ because a rotation
is homotopic to the identity. Hence $\deg f = k$. ⟨your step: $k < 0$ by
composing with $z \mapsto z^{-1}$ — say why that map is a reflection, and give
its degree.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Local degree is the only genuinely new *technique* in this unit, and the
cellular boundary formula is nothing but this technique applied to attaching
maps. Get (b) solid: everything in Problems 4 and 5 is a consequence.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the cellular chain complex)

(a) State Lemma 2.34 in all three parts. For (a) of the lemma, explain why
$X^n/X^{n-1}$ is a wedge of $n$-spheres and which §2.1 results turn that into
the statement about $H_k(X^n, X^{n-1})$.
(b) Prove Lemma 2.34(b) and the finite-dimensional case of 2.34(c) from the
long exact sequence of $(X^n, X^{n-1})$, as Hatcher does: show the middle map
$H_k(X^{n-1}) \to H_k(X^n)$ is surjective when $k \neq n$ and injective when
$k \neq n-1$, then run along the chain of skeleta.
(c) Write down the cellular chain complex: the groups $H_n(X^n, X^{n-1})$ and
the maps $d_n = j_{n-1}\partial_n$. Verify $d_n d_{n+1} = 0$ directly, and say
which composite in the diagram being zero is the reason.
(d) State the theorem that cellular homology agrees with singular homology, and
give Hatcher's proof from the diagram. It is four sentences; the content is
that $j_n$ is injective and carries $\operatorname{Im}\partial_{n+1}$ onto
$\operatorname{Im} d_{n+1}$ and $H_n(X^n)$ onto $\operatorname{Ker}\partial_n$.
(e) Deduce applications (i), (ii) and (iii), and use (iii) to compute
$H_i(\mathbb{CP}^n)$ and $H_i(S^n \times S^n)$ for $n > 1$. Then say what (ii)
gives you for a space with exactly one $n$-cell, and check it against
$\mathbb{R}P^2$ from at1-07 — where the answer is $\mathbb{Z}_2$, not
$\mathbb{Z}$.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(c) $d_n d_{n+1} = j_{n-1}\partial_n j_n \partial_{n+1}$, and $\partial_n j_n$
is two consecutive maps in a long exact sequence.
(e) (ii) bounds the number of *generators*, not the group.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) $d_n d_{n+1} = j_{n-1}(\partial_n j_n)\partial_{n+1}$, and $\partial_n j_n$
is the composition $H_n(X^n) \to H_n(X^n, X^{n-1}) \to H_{n-1}(X^{n-1})$ of two
consecutive maps in the long exact sequence of the pair $(X^n, X^{n-1})$, hence
zero. So $d_n d_{n+1} = 0$ and the cellular chain groups do form a complex.
(e) (ii) says $H_n(X)$ is generated by at most $k$ elements when $X$ has $k$
$n$-cells, because $\operatorname{Ker} d_n$ sits inside a free abelian group of
rank $k$ and $H_n$ is a quotient of it. With one $n$-cell, $H_n(X)$ is cyclic —
which is consistent with $H_1(\mathbb{R}P^2) = \mathbb{Z}_2$; cyclic does not
mean infinite cyclic. ⟨your step: give a CW structure on $\mathbb{R}P^2$ with
one 1-cell and confirm the count.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Part (d) is the only place in this unit where you should feel the §2.1
machinery pressing. The proof of Lemma 2.34 consumes good pairs, excision and
two long exact sequences, none of which this unit develops. Cite them by number
and move on; at1-09 is where the exact-sequence toolkit is built properly.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — computing, and what the computation misses)

(a) Compute $H_*(M_g)$ from the cellular chain complex
$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}^{2g} \to \mathbb{Z} \to 0$, giving the reason
$d_1 = 0$ and the reason $d_2 = 0$. The second reason is a statement about the
maps $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}$; say what it is.
(b) Compute $H_*(N_g)$. Here $d_2(1) = (2, \dots, 2)$; derive that from the
attaching word $a_1^2 \cdots a_g^2$ and the Cellular Boundary Formula, then
change basis to read off $H_1(N_g) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{g-1} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$.
Check $g = 1$ against at1-07's computation of $H_*(\mathbb{R}P^2)$.
(c) Now $\mathbb{R}P^n$, Example 2.42 — the centrepiece of the unit. The
attaching map of $e^k$ is the double cover $\varphi : S^{k-1} \to
\mathbb{R}P^{k-1}$. Compute $\deg(q\varphi)$ by local degrees, using that the
two sheets differ by the antipodal map of $S^{k-1}$, and conclude that $d_k$ is
$0$ or multiplication by $2$ according to the parity of $k$. Then read off
$H_k(\mathbb{R}P^n)$.
(d) State Theorem 2.44 and prove that $\chi(X) = \sum_n (-1)^n \operatorname{rank} H_n(X)$,
taking as given the rank identity for a short exact sequence of finitely
generated abelian groups. Then compute $\chi(\mathbb{R}P^4)$ both ways.
(e) The reflection, and the point of the unit. Write out the Betti numbers of
$\mathbb{R}P^4$ — the ranks of its homology groups, in the sense defined on
Hatcher p. 130 — and compare them with the Betti numbers of a one-point space.
They agree in every degree, and so do the Euler characteristics. Say what the
homology of $\mathbb{R}P^4$ knows that its Betti numbers do not, name the
invariant that carries it, and explain why a computation that reports only free
ranks would have thrown away everything interesting about this space.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) Each $a_i$ occurs with total exponent 2 in the word, so the corresponding
$\Delta_{\alpha\beta}$ is homotopic to $z \mapsto z^2$.
(c) $\deg(q\varphi) = \deg \mathbb{1} + \deg(-\mathbb{1}) = 1 + (-1)^k$, and
Proposition 2.30 is what lets you add the two.
(e) Compute $H_*(\mathbb{R}P^4)$ from (c) first, then take ranks. The answer to
"what is thrown away" is a word Hatcher defines in the same sentence as
"Betti number".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $d_1 = 0$ because there is a single 0-cell and $H_0$ of a connected complex
must be $\mathbb{Z}$. $d_2 = 0$ because each $a_i$ and each $b_i$ occurs in
$[a_1, b_1]\cdots[a_g, b_g]$ together with its inverse, so total exponent zero,
so each $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}$ is homotopic to a constant map and has degree 0.
Both boundary maps vanish, so the homology equals the chain groups:
$\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}^{2g}, \mathbb{Z}$.
(c) $q\varphi$ restricts to a homeomorphism on each component of
$S^{k-1} - S^{k-2}$, and the two restrictions differ by precomposition with the
antipodal map, of degree $(-1)^k$. So the two local degrees are $1$ and
$(-1)^k$, and Proposition 2.30 gives $\deg q\varphi = 1 + (-1)^k$: that is $2$
for $k$ even and $0$ for $k$ odd. ⟨your step: assemble the chain complex for
$n$ even and for $n$ odd separately, and read off the homology in each case.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(e) From (c), $H_*(\mathbb{R}P^4) = (\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}_2, 0, \mathbb{Z}_2, 0)$
in degrees $0$ through $4$. Ranks: $\mathbb{Z}_2$ has no $\mathbb{Z}$ summand,
so its rank is $0$. The Betti numbers are therefore $1, 0, 0, 0, 0$ — exactly
those of a point. And $\chi(\mathbb{R}P^4) = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 = 1$ from the
cell counts, which agrees with $\sum (-1)^k \operatorname{rank} H_k = 1$, as
Theorem 2.44 requires; a point also has $\chi = 1$. ⟨your step: name what
distinguishes the two homologies, say which of Hatcher's two p. 130 terms
carries it, and then state carefully what this does *not* show — in particular,
whether you have proved that Betti numbers never distinguish $\mathbb{R}P^4$
from a point, or only that these ones do not.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) through (d) are drill and they are worth doing in order; (e) is the
reflection and is the reason the unit exists. Cellular homology makes the
computation cheap enough that it is tempting to compress the answer to a list
of numbers. This unit's best example is precisely one where that compression
loses everything: $\mathbb{R}P^4$ has interesting homology and boring Betti
numbers. Keep that in view when a later unit hands you a barcode.
</details>
