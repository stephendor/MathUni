## Problem 1 (easy–medium — the submodule criterion, and four subsets)

(a) Prove Proposition 8.23 (p. 161): a subset $M'\subseteq M$ is a submodule if and only if it is nonempty and closed under addition and under the action of $R$.

(b) Where does closure under negation come from?

(c) Apply the criterion: show the **left** ideals of $R$ are exactly the submodules of $R$ over itself — Example 8.24 (p. 161). For commutative $R$ these are the ideals of aa-09; the distinction is aa-15 Problem 6(a)'s.

(d) Let $R$ be a commutative integral domain and $M$ an $R$-module. Prove the **torsion** subset $T(M) = \{m\in M : rm = 0$ for some $r\ne0\}$ is a submodule, and identify the exact step that uses the domain hypothesis.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Act by $-1_R$, and use aa-15 Problem 2(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) If $r_1m_1 = 0$ and $r_2m_2 = 0$, what kills $m_1+m_2$? You need that element to be nonzero.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A submodule satisfies both conditions and contains $0$. Conversely, given them, pick $m\in M'$; then $(-1_R)m = -m\in M'$ by aa-15 Problem 2(c), so $0 = m + (-m)\in M'$, and the remaining clauses are equations inherited from $M$.

(b) From closure under the action, exactly as in Proposition 5.9 (p. 81) for ideals, and for the same reason: negation is an instance of scaling.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Both conditions in (a), read for $M = R$ with the ring multiplication as the action, say "closed under addition" and "absorbs multiplication by every $r\in R$" — which is Proposition 5.9's characterisation of an ideal. So the two notions coincide exactly, and Example 8.31 (p. 162) is the observation that for commutative $R$ the lattice of ideals *is* the lattice of submodules of $R$.

(d) $0\in T(M)$ since $1\cdot 0 = 0$ and $1\ne0$. If $r_1m_1 = 0$ and $r_2m_2 = 0$ with $r_1,r_2\ne0$, then
$$(r_1r_2)(m_1+m_2) = r_2(r_1m_1) + r_1(r_2m_2) = 0,$$
and the domain hypothesis is used at exactly one point: to know $r_1r_2\ne0$, so that the element produced is a legitimate witness. For scaling, $r_1(sm) = s(r_1m) = 0$, using commutativity.

Without the hypothesis the argument produces nothing usable — over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the witnesses $r_1=[2]$, $r_2=[3]$ multiply to $[0]$ — and it is worth being clear that this shows the *proof* fails, not the conclusion. Deciding whether $T(M)$ can genuinely fail to be a submodule over a ring with zero-divisors is a further question, and one this set does not settle.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — sums, intersections, and the union that is not)

(a) Prove Example 8.27 (p. 162): the intersection of any family of submodules of $M$ is a submodule.

(b) Prove Example 8.26 (p. 162): $N_1+N_2 = \{n_1+n_2 : n_i \in N_i\}$ is the smallest submodule containing $N_1$ and $N_2$.

(c) Show $N_1\cup N_2$ is a submodule if and only if one contains the other.

(d) Prove Example 8.25 (p. 162): the set of linear combinations $r_1m_1+\cdots+r_nm_n$ is the smallest submodule containing $m_1,\dots,m_n$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) If neither contains the other, pick $a\in N_1\setminus N_2$ and $b\in N_2\setminus N_1$ and ask where $a+b$ lives.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) and (d) are both "check the two conditions of Proposition 8.23".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Each $N_i$ contains $0$, so the intersection is nonempty; and if $m,m'$ lie in every $N_i$ then so do $m+m'$ and $rm$. This works for *arbitrary* families, unlike the union.

(b) $N_1+N_2$ is closed under addition — $(n_1+n_2)+(n_1'+n_2') = (n_1+n_1')+(n_2+n_2')$ — and under scaling, and contains $N_1$ (take $n_2=0$) and $N_2$. Any submodule containing both is closed under addition and so contains every $n_1+n_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Suppose $N_1\cup N_2$ is a submodule and neither contains the other; choose $a\in N_1\setminus N_2$ and $b\in N_2\setminus N_1$. Then $a+b$ lies in the union, so in $N_1$ or in $N_2$. If $a+b\in N_1$ then $b = (a+b)-a\in N_1$, a contradiction; if $a+b\in N_2$ then $a\in N_2$, likewise. Conversely a union of nested submodules is the larger one.

This is aa-12 Problem 3(c) in a new setting — $2\mathbb{Z}\cup3\mathbb{Z}$ was the ideal version — and the reason is identical: closure under addition has to compare elements from different members, and only nesting lets it.

(d) The set of such combinations is closed under addition (add coefficientwise) and under scaling, since $r(\sum r_im_i) = \sum(rr_i)m_i$; and it contains each $m_j$ by taking $r_j=1$ and the rest $0$ — which uses the axiom $1_R\cdot m = m$, the one clause of Definition 8.1 (p. 154) that is easy to forget is doing anything. Any submodule containing all the $m_i$ contains every scaling and every sum, hence the whole set. Definition 8.28 (p. 162) names it $\langle m_1,\dots,m_n\rangle$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — generated, finitely generated, cyclic)

Let $R$ be a commutative ring.

(a) State Definition 8.29 (p. 162) and give a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module that is not cyclic.

(b) Prove that an $R$-module $M$ is cyclic if and only if $M\cong R/I$ for some ideal $I$ — Example 8.30 (p. 162).

(c) Identify $I$ in terms of the generator, and say when two cyclic modules are isomorphic.

(d) Give an $R$-module that is not finitely generated, and prove it.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) If $M = \langle m\rangle$, consider $f : R\to M$, $r\mapsto rm$ — which aa-15 Problem 4(b) already built.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) The kernel of that $f$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) A module is cyclic when one element generates it. $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ and is not cyclic: a single $(a,b)$ generates only the multiples $(na,nb)$, and hitting $(1,0)$ forces $b = 0$, after which $(0,1)$ is out of reach.

(b) ($\Leftarrow$) $R/I$ is generated by $1+I$, since $a+I = a\cdot(1+I)$. ($\Rightarrow$) If $M=\langle m\rangle$, the map $f : R\to M$, $f(r) = rm$, is an $R$-module homomorphism by aa-15 Problem 4(b), and it is surjective because $m$ generates.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) The First Isomorphism Theorem for modules — Theorem 8.44, p. 168, or the argument of aa-10 Problem 2(a) transplanted — gives $M\cong R/\ker f$, and $\ker f$ is a submodule of $R$, hence an ideal by Problem 1(c).

(c) $I = \ker f = \{r\in R : rm = 0\}$, the **annihilator** of the generator. So a cyclic module is $R$ modulo the relations its generator satisfies, and two cyclic modules are isomorphic exactly when generators can be chosen with the same annihilator. For $M = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ with generator $[1]$, the annihilator is $n\mathbb{Z}$ and the statement reads $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — true and uninformative, which is what a correct general theorem should look like on its motivating example.

(d) $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Suppose $\mathbb{Q} = \langle q_1,\dots,q_n\rangle$ and let $d>0$ be a common denominator of the $q_i$. Every integer combination $\sum a_iq_i$ can be written with denominator $d$, so its reduced denominator divides $d$. But $1/(d+1)$ has reduced denominator $d+1$, which does not divide $d$. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}$ — though it is generated by one element over itself, which is Example 8.8's point (p. 155) that the ring is part of the data.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — direct sums)

(a) State Definition 8.33 (p. 163) and verify $M\oplus N$ is an $R$-module.

(b) Prove the **universal property**: for any $R$-module $P$, giving an $R$-module homomorphism $M\oplus N\to P$ is the same as giving a pair of homomorphisms $M\to P$ and $N\to P$. Compare Example 8.35 (p. 164).

(c) Prove $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, and $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\not\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

(d) Example 8.34 (p. 163) recalls that $k^n$ is a direct sum of $n$ copies of $k$. Is every finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module a direct sum of copies of $\mathbb{Z}$? Answer with a witness.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) The two maps are $m\mapsto f(m,0)$ and $n\mapsto f(0,n)$; going back, $(m,n)\mapsto g(m)+h(n)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) For the non-isomorphism, find an element of one that no element of the other matches — as in aa-07 Problem 1.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) All eight clauses are checked coordinatewise, since both operations are defined coordinatewise; $0_{M\oplus N} = (0_M,0_N)$.

(b) Given $f : M\oplus N\to P$, put $g(m) = f(m,0)$ and $h(n) = f(0,n)$; both are homomorphisms, being $f$ composed with an inclusion. Given $g$ and $h$, put $f(m,n) = g(m)+h(n)$; this is additive and $R$-linear because $g$ and $h$ are. The two constructions are mutually inverse because $(m,n) = (m,0)+(0,n)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The map $[a]_6\mapsto([a]_2,[a]_3)$ is well defined and additive, and every additive map between $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is $\mathbb{Z}$-linear by aa-15 Problem 3(b). It is bijective by the Chinese Remainder Theorem (aa-10 Problem 5), or by checking six values.

$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\not\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus2}$: in the right-hand module every element $m$ satisfies $2m=0$, while $[1]_4$ has $2[1]_4 = [2]_4\ne0$. The property "$2m = 0$ for every $m$" is written in the module operations alone, so it transfers across any isomorphism.

This is aa-07 Problem 1 again, and the pattern is the same both times: $6 = 2\cdot3$ splits and $4 = 2\cdot2$ does not, because the two factors of $6$ are coprime and the two factors of $4$ are not.

(d) No. $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is finitely generated — by one element — and is not a direct sum of copies of $\mathbb{Z}$, since any such sum is either $0$ or infinite. Over a field every finitely generated module is a direct sum of copies of the field, which is the theorem that vector spaces have bases; over $\mathbb{Z}$ that fails, and repairing it is exactly what aa-20's structure theorem does — the answer there is a sum of copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ *and* cyclic torsion pieces.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — every submodule gives a quotient)

Let $K$ be a submodule of an $R$-module $M$, and let $m\sim_K m'$ mean $m'-m\in K$.

(a) Prove $\sim_K$ is an equivalence relation, and prove Lemma 8.39 (p. 166): the operations of Definition 8.37 (p. 166) are well defined on $M/K$.

(b) Prove Theorem 8.40 (p. 167): $M/K$ is an $R$-module.

(c) Prove Proposition 8.41 (p. 167): the projection $\pi : M\to M/K$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $K$; deduce Remark 8.42 (p. 167), that every submodule is a kernel.

(d) Compare with aa-09 Problem 4. For rings, only **ideals** admit quotients, and a subring generally does not. For modules, **every** submodule does. Explain the asymmetry in one paragraph.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) For the scaling, you need $m'-m\in K \Rightarrow rm'-rm\in K$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Ask what the quotient's operations have to be protected from, in each case.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Reflexive, symmetric and transitive because $K$ contains $0$, is closed under negation and under addition. For well-definedness: if $m_1'-m_1\in K$ and $m_2'-m_2\in K$ then $(m_1'+m_2')-(m_1+m_2)\in K$ by closure under addition, and $rm'-rm = r(m'-m)\in K$ by closure under the action.

(b) Every clause of Definition 8.1 (p. 154) holds in $M/K$ because it holds in $M$ and the operations are defined on representatives; $0_{M/K} = 0+K = K$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $\pi(m+m') = (m+m')+K = (m+K)+(m'+K)$ and $\pi(rm) = rm+K = r(m+K)$, so $\pi$ is a homomorphism, and it is onto because every coset is $m+K$. Its kernel is $\{m : m+K = 0+K\} = \{m : m\in K\} = K$. So the submodule one started with is a kernel, which is Remark 8.42.

(d) The asymmetry is about **what the quotient's operations need protecting from**. A quotient ring has two operations to define on cosets, and the multiplication is a product of two things from the *same* ring; keeping it well defined requires $I$ to absorb multiplication by all of $R$, which is more than being closed under multiplication — hence ideal, not subring, and aa-09 Problem 4(d) exhibited $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ failing exactly there.

A quotient module has an addition and a *scaling*, and the scaling multiplies something in $K$ by something in $R$ — a different place. But "closed under the action of $R$" is already part of being a submodule; there is no weaker notion of "sub-thing" that omits it. So the condition that has to be imposed by hand for rings is built into the definition for modules, and every submodule works. Put the other way: the ring case has ideals and subrings as two different notions because $R$ acts on itself and a subring need not be stable under that action, while a submodule is stable by definition.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — kernels, images, and the submodules of ℤ/nℤ)

(a) Prove Proposition 8.32 (p. 162): for a homomorphism $f : M\to N$, $\ker f$ is a submodule of $M$ and $\operatorname{im} f$ is a submodule of $N$.

(b) Prove the **correspondence theorem** for modules: the submodules of $M/K$ are exactly the $N/K$ for submodules $N$ with $K\subseteq N\subseteq M$, inclusion-preservingly.

(c) Use (b) to list all submodules of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and count the submodules of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ in general.

(d) State Theorem 8.43 (p. 167), the canonical decomposition, and check it against $f : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $a\mapsto[3a]$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) Submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ containing $12\mathbb{Z}$ are $d\mathbb{Z}$ with $d\mid12$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Factor $f$ as a surjection onto its image, an isomorphism, and an inclusion.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\ker f$ is nonempty ($f(0)=0$) and closed: $f(m+m') = f(m)+f(m') = 0$ and $f(rm) = rf(m)=0$. Likewise $\operatorname{im} f$ contains $0$ and is closed, since $f(m)+f(m') = f(m+m')$ and $rf(m) = f(rm)$.

(b) Send a submodule $\bar N\subseteq M/K$ to $\pi^{-1}(\bar N)$, a submodule of $M$ containing $\ker\pi = K$; and $N\supseteq K$ to $N/K = \pi(N)$. The two are mutually inverse because $\pi$ is surjective with kernel $K$, and both preserve inclusion. This is aa-11 Problem 4(a) with modules in place of rings, and the proof is the same one.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ are the $d\mathbb{Z}$ (aa-09 Problem 6(a)), and $12\mathbb{Z}\subseteq d\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $d\mid12$. So the submodules of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ are $d\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ for $d\in\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$ — six of them, of sizes $12,6,4,3,2,1$. In general $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly as many submodules as $n$ has positive divisors.

Note the inclusion runs opposite to the divisibility, as aa-10 Problem 6(d) warned: $d\mid e$ gives $e\mathbb{Z}\subseteq d\mathbb{Z}$, so the *largest* submodule $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ comes from $d=1$.

(d) Theorem 8.43 says every $f : M\to N$ factors as $M\twoheadrightarrow M/\ker f\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\operatorname{im} f\hookrightarrow N$. For $f(a) = [3a]_{12}$: $\ker f = \{a : 12\mid 3a\} = 4\mathbb{Z}$, and $\operatorname{im} f = \{[0],[3],[6],[9]\} = 3\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. The factorisation reads
$$\mathbb{Z}\ \twoheadrightarrow\ \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\ \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\ 3\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z},$$
and the middle isomorphism sends $[a]_4$ to $[3a]_{12}$. Counting confirms it: $|\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}| = 4 = |3\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}|$.
</details>
