## Problem 1 (easy — the definition, and what it excludes)

(a) Verify directly from the definition that $p : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$,
$p(s) = (\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s)$, is a covering space, by exhibiting an
evenly covered neighbourhood of an arbitrary point and naming its sheets.
(b) Do the same for $p : S^1 \to S^1$, $p(z) = z^n$, viewing $z$ as a complex
number of modulus 1. How many sheets? Check your answer against Proposition
1.32 by computing the index of $p_*(\pi_1(S^1))$ in $\pi_1(S^1)$, using
Theorem 1.7 to identify both groups with $\mathbb{Z}$.
(c) Now a non-example. Let $q : (0,2) \to S^1$ be $q(s) = (\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s)$.
Show $q$ is a surjective local homeomorphism but *not* a covering space, by
finding a point of $S^1$ with no evenly covered neighbourhood. Say in one
sentence what a local homeomorphism is missing.
(d) Hatcher allows $p^{-1}(U)$ to be empty — the union of an empty collection
of sheets — so a covering space need not be surjective. Give an example of a
non-surjective covering space, and say why the convention is worth having
rather than a defect to be patched.

*(Hatcher §1.3, pp. 56–57 for the definition and the two examples;
Proposition 1.32, p. 61. Theorem 1.7 is at1-04's, p. 29.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) The trouble is at $q(1)$, whose preimage is the single point $s = 1$. Look
at what the two "loose ends" of the interval near $0$ and near $2$ do to a
small neighbourhood of that point.
(d) Take the empty space, or a covering of a disconnected space that misses one
component.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The evenly covered condition has three parts and it is worth checking them
separately every time: $p^{-1}(U)$ is a union of open sets; those sets are
*disjoint*; and each maps *homeomorphically onto all of* $U$. A local
homeomorphism gives you the first and third locally, and gives you nothing at
all about the second or about the "onto all of $U$" in a uniform way. That is
the entire difference, and (c) is designed so you feel it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Take $U$ an open arc of angular width less than $2\pi/n$. Then $p^{-1}(U)$
is $n$ disjoint arcs, each of angular width $1/n$ that of $U$, each mapped
homeomorphically onto $U$. So the covering is $n$-sheeted. On $\pi_1$, the
generator upstairs is the loop going once round, whose image goes $n$ times
round, so $p_*(\mathbb{Z}) = n\mathbb{Z}$, of index $n$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. The
two counts agree, as Proposition 1.32 says they must.
(c) The point $q(1) = (1,0)$ has $q^{-1}((1,0)) = \{1\}$. ⟨your step: take any
open arc $U$ around $(1,0)$ and describe $q^{-1}(U)$ as a union of three
intervals. Two of them are half-open at an end of $(0,2)$; say what each maps
onto, and why that is fatal.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Let $x \in S^1$ and write $x = p(s_0)$. Take $U = p(J)$ where
$J = (s_0 - 1/4, s_0 + 1/4)$; this is an open arc, and $U$ is an open
neighbourhood of $x$. Since $p(s) = p(s')$ exactly when $s - s' \in \mathbb{Z}$,
$$p^{-1}(U) = \bigsqcup_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} (J + k),$$
a disjoint union because the translates of an interval of length $1/2$ by
distinct integers are disjoint. ⟨your step: check that $p$ restricted to
$J + k$ is a homeomorphism onto $U$ — injectivity, surjectivity, and
continuity of the inverse — and then name the sheets.⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy/medium — covering spaces you can draw)

Set $X = S^1 \vee S^1$, viewed as a graph with one vertex and two edges labelled
$a$ and $b$, each oriented. Hatcher calls a graph $\widetilde{X}$ *2-oriented*
if every vertex has four edge ends — one $a$-end in, one $a$-end out, one
$b$-end in, one $b$-end out — and shows that the 2-oriented graphs are exactly
the covering spaces of $X$.

(a) Draw a connected 2-oriented graph with two vertices. Check the condition at
each vertex, and say how many sheets the corresponding covering space has.
(b) Prove the counting fact that makes the whole family tractable: a finite
2-oriented graph with $V$ vertices has exactly $2V$ edges.
(c) Prove Hatcher's claim on p. 57 that every *finite* connected graph with an
even number of edge ends at each vertex can be 2-oriented when there are four
ends at each vertex, given the classical fact that such a graph has an Eulerian
circuit. (Label the circuit's edges alternately $a$ and $b$; then say why the
$a$-edges form disjoint circles, and orient them.)
(d) Now combine. A connected $n$-sheeted covering space of $X$ is a connected
2-oriented graph with $n$ vertices. Using at1-05's Example 1.22 technique —
choose a maximal tree and read off one generator per edge outside it — show
that its fundamental group is free of rank $n + 1$. Then use Proposition 1.31
and Proposition 1.32 to state what you have just proved about subgroups of the
free group $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$.

*(Hatcher §1.3, pp. 57–58 for 2-oriented graphs and the Eulerian argument;
Propositions 1.31 and 1.32, pp. 60–61. Example 1.22 is at1-05's, pp. 43–44.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Count edge *ends*: each vertex has four, and each edge has two.
(d) Rank $= E - V + 1$, which is Problem 2(d) of at1-05. Substitute $E = 2V$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Part (d) is the payoff and it is worth naming: you are proving that every
index-$n$ subgroup of a free group of rank 2 is free of rank $n + 1$, entirely
by drawing graphs. That an algebraic fact about subgroups falls out of a
picture is exactly what Hatcher means on p. 56 by saying that the fundamental
group and covering spaces are two viewpoints on one thing.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Each of the $V$ vertices has 4 edge ends, giving $4V$ ends in total. Each
edge contributes exactly 2 ends. So $E = 4V/2 = 2V$.
(d) With $E = 2V = 2n$ and $V = n$, the rank is $E - V + 1 = 2n - n + 1 = n+1$.
By Proposition 1.31 the map $p_*$ is injective, so $\pi_1(\widetilde{X})$ is
isomorphic to its image, a subgroup $H \subset \pi_1(X) = \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$;
by Proposition 1.32 that subgroup has index $n$. ⟨your step: state the
resulting theorem about free groups, and note the sanity check at $n = 1$.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $C$ be an Eulerian circuit, traversing each edge exactly once and
returning to its start. Since every vertex has four edge ends, the circuit
enters and leaves each vertex exactly twice, so it has even length and the
alternating labelling $a, b, a, b, \dots$ around $C$ closes up consistently.
⟨your step: at a fixed vertex, the circuit passes through twice, using four
edge ends; explain why the alternating labelling gives that vertex exactly two
$a$-ends and two $b$-ends. Then show the union of the $a$-edges is a disjoint
union of circles — every vertex meets exactly two $a$-ends — and finish by
choosing an orientation for each circle.⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — lifting)

(a) Prove Proposition 1.34, the unique lifting property: two lifts of the same
map that agree at one point of a connected $Y$ agree everywhere. Structure the
proof as "the agreement set is open and closed", and be explicit about which
part of the covering space definition each half uses.
(b) Deduce from Proposition 1.33 that if $Y$ is simply-connected and locally
path-connected, then every map $f : (Y, y_0) \to (X, x_0)$ lifts to every
covering space of $X$, uniquely once the lift of the basepoint is fixed.
(c) Use (b) to prove that for $n \geq 2$ every map $S^n \to S^1$ is
nullhomotopic. (Lift through $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$; then use that $\mathbb{R}$
is convex.) You may quote $\pi_1(S^n) = 0$ for $n \geq 2$ from Hatcher's
Proposition 1.14.
(d) Hatcher notes on p. 62 that the lifting criterion can fail without local
path-connectedness of $Y$. Read the proof of Proposition 1.33 and identify the
single step that consumes it. Say what that step is trying to establish.

*(Hatcher §1.3: Proposition 1.30 and path lifting, pp. 60–61; Propositions 1.33
and 1.34 with their proofs, pp. 61–62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Around a point $y$, take an evenly covered $U$ containing $f(y)$ and look
at the sheets containing $\tilde f_1(y)$ and $\tilde f_2(y)$: either they are
equal or they are disjoint, and there is no third case. That dichotomy is the
whole proof.
(c) A lift $S^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is nullhomotopic by a straight-line homotopy;
push that homotopy down.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) is a one-line application once you see that $f_*(\pi_1(Y,y_0))$ is the
trivial subgroup, which is contained in everything. The uniqueness is
Proposition 1.34, and noticing that you need both propositions — one for
existence, one for uniqueness — is part of the exercise.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Let $A = \{y \in Y : \tilde f_1(y) = \tilde f_2(y)\}$. Fix $y \in Y$, let
$U$ be an evenly covered neighbourhood of $f(y)$, and let $\widetilde U_1$,
$\widetilde U_2$ be the sheets containing $\tilde f_1(y)$, $\tilde f_2(y)$. By
continuity there is a neighbourhood $N$ of $y$ with $\tilde f_1(N) \subset \widetilde U_1$
and $\tilde f_2(N) \subset \widetilde U_2$. ⟨your step: split on whether
$\tilde f_1(y) = \tilde f_2(y)$. In one case the sheets are disjoint, giving
$N \subset Y - A$; in the other they coincide and $p$ is injective on them,
giving $N \subset A$. Conclude that $A$ is open and closed, and finish with
connectedness plus the hypothesis that $A \neq \emptyset$.⟩
(d) It is the *continuity* step, at the very end of the proof. Well-definedness
of $\tilde f$ needs only the subgroup condition; continuity needs a
path-connected open $V \subset f^{-1}(U)$ around each point, so that every
nearby point can be reached by a path staying inside $V$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Let $f : S^n \to S^1$ with $n \geq 2$, and let $p : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ be
the standard covering. $S^n$ is simply-connected by Proposition 1.14 and is
locally path-connected, so by (b) there is a lift
$\tilde f : S^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with $p \tilde f = f$. ⟨your step: $\mathbb{R}$
is convex, so the linear homotopy $\tilde f_t = (1-t)\tilde f + t\,c$ to a
constant map $c$ is a homotopy of maps $S^n \to \mathbb{R}$ — this is at1-01's
Example 1.1. Compose with $p$ and say what you get.⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium/hard — the Galois correspondence)

(a) State Theorem 1.38 in full, both halves. Then use it to list *all*
connected covering spaces of $S^1$ up to isomorphism, by listing all subgroups
of $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$. Match each to a covering space you can describe
geometrically, and check the sheet count against Proposition 1.32.
(b) Do the same for $\mathbb{R}P^2$, given $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}P^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$.
Name both covering spaces.
(c) Explain why the second half of Theorem 1.38 speaks of *conjugacy classes*
of subgroups once basepoints are ignored. Say precisely what changes when the
basepoint $\tilde x_0$ is moved within $p^{-1}(x_0)$, and connect this to
at1-03's change-of-basepoint isomorphism $\beta_h$.
(d) The construction of the universal cover needs $X$ to be path-connected,
locally path-connected and semilocally simply-connected. Show that the
shrinking wedge of circles of at1-05's Example 1.25 fails the last condition,
and hence has no simply-connected covering space. Which of the three
hypotheses does its cone $CX$ satisfy, and which does it still fail?
(e) Why is semilocal simple connectivity *necessary* and not merely convenient?
Reproduce Hatcher's one-paragraph argument on p. 63 that a space with a
simply-connected covering space must have it.

*(Hatcher §1.3: semilocal simple connectivity and its necessity, p. 63; the
construction of $\widetilde X$ as homotopy classes of paths, pp. 63–65;
Propositions 1.36 and 1.37, pp. 66–67; Theorem 1.38, p. 67. Example 1.25 is
at1-05's, p. 49.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 1$ together with
the trivial subgroup $0$. That is one list, and there is one covering space per
entry.
(d) A neighbourhood of the origin in the shrinking wedge contains whole circles
$C_n$, and each such circle carries a loop that is not nullhomotopic in the
whole space.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The reason Theorem 1.38 is called a Galois correspondence is worth taking
seriously as a navigational aid rather than as decoration: bigger subgroup
means smaller covering space, the trivial subgroup gives the universal cover
which sits on top of everything, and the whole group gives $X$ itself. Every
answer in (a) and (b) should be checked against that order-reversal.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$: the trivial subgroup, and $n\mathbb{Z}$ for each
$n \geq 1$. The trivial subgroup corresponds to the universal cover
$\mathbb{R} \to S^1$, of infinite index and infinitely many sheets. The
subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$ corresponds to $z \mapsto z^n$, of index $n$ and $n$
sheets. $1\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}$ gives the identity $S^1 \to S^1$. Since
$\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian every subgroup is its own conjugacy class, so ignoring
basepoints changes nothing here.
(d) In the shrinking wedge, every neighbourhood $U$ of the origin contains
$C_n$ entirely for all large $n$. The loop going once round such a $C_n$ lies
in $U$; it is not nullhomotopic in $X$, because the retraction $r_n$ collapsing
every other circle carries it to a generator of $\pi_1(C_n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
⟨your step: conclude that $\pi_1(U) \to \pi_1(X)$ is non-trivial for every
neighbourhood $U$ of the origin, so no simply-connected cover exists.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(e) Suppose $p : \widetilde X \to X$ is a covering space with $\widetilde X$
simply-connected, and let $x \in X$. Every point of $X$ has an evenly covered
neighbourhood, so choose $U \ni x$ with a sheet $\widetilde U \subset \widetilde X$
projecting homeomorphically onto $U$. Let $\gamma$ be a loop in $U$ at $x$.
⟨your step: lift $\gamma$ into $\widetilde U$ using the inverse of the
homeomorphism; use $\pi_1(\widetilde X) = 0$ to nullhomotope the lift; then push
the nullhomotopy down by $p$ and say what you have shown about
$\pi_1(U, x) \to \pi_1(X, x)$.⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — deck transformations as the group action)

(a) Prove Proposition 1.39(a): the covering space is normal if and only if
$H = p_*(\pi_1(\widetilde X, \tilde x_0))$ is a normal subgroup of
$\pi_1(X, x_0)$. Use the fact, established in the proof of Theorem 1.38, that
moving the basepoint from $\tilde x_0$ to $\tilde x_1$ within $p^{-1}(x_0)$
conjugates $H$ by the class of the loop that lifts to a path from $\tilde x_0$
to $\tilde x_1$.
(b) Compute the deck transformation group of $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$ and of
$z \mapsto z^n$ explicitly, describing the transformations geometrically. Check
each against Proposition 1.39(b).
(c) Hatcher's condition (*) on a group action is that every $y \in Y$ has a
neighbourhood $U$ with the images $g(U)$ pairwise disjoint. Show that (*)
implies the action is free. Then show the converse fails, using Hatcher's
example: $\mathbb{Z}$ acting on $S^1$ by rotation through an angle that is an
irrational multiple of $2\pi$. Finally, give an action that is properly
discontinuous but not free, and say which of the two failures is the reason the
hexagonal tiling group does not satisfy (*).
(d) Prove Example 1.43: the antipodal action of $\mathbb{Z}_2$ on $S^n$ is a
covering space action with orbit space $\mathbb{R}P^n$, and therefore
$\pi_1(\mathbb{R}P^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ for $n \geq 2$. Then show directly
that the generator has order two, by lifting the square of the generating loop.
(e) Example 1.41 exhibits a covering space $M_{11} \to M_3$ from a 5-fold
rotational symmetry of the genus-11 surface. State exactly what Proposition
1.39 tells you about $\pi_1(M_{11})$ as a subgroup of $\pi_1(M_3)$, and check
that the index is consistent with Proposition 1.32.

*(Hatcher §1.3: deck transformations and normal coverings, pp. 70–71;
Proposition 1.39 with its proof, p. 71; group actions, condition (*), and
Proposition 1.40, pp. 71–73; free versus properly discontinuous with the
irrational rotation and the hexagonal tiling, p. 73; Example 1.41, p. 73;
Example 1.43, p. 74)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) For the irrational rotation, every orbit is dense in $S^1$, and condition
(*) forces orbits to be discrete.
(d) The action is a covering space action because an open hemisphere is
disjoint from its own antipodal image.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
This problem is the unit's thesis in exercise form. Proposition 1.39 turns a
geometric symmetry question — can I slide one sheet onto another? — into an
algebraic one about normality, and Proposition 1.40 runs the construction
backwards, manufacturing a covering space from any sufficiently well-behaved
action. Do (b) and (d) first: they are the two examples in which you can see
both directions at once.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) For $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$ the deck transformations are the translations
$s \mapsto s + k$, $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $G(\widetilde X) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
Here $H$ is trivial and $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, so Proposition 1.39(b) gives
$N(H)/H = \mathbb{Z}/0 = \mathbb{Z}$; they agree. For $z \mapsto z^n$ the deck
transformations are the rotations by multiples of $2\pi/n$, so
$G(\widetilde X) \cong \mathbb{Z}_n$; here $H = n\mathbb{Z}$ is normal in the
abelian group $\mathbb{Z}$, so $N(H) = \mathbb{Z}$ and
$N(H)/H = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. They agree again.
(c) (*) implies free: if $g(y) = y$ then $y \in U \cap g(U)$ for the
neighbourhood $U$ given by (*), so $g$ is the identity. ⟨your step: the
converse. The irrational rotation is free because no non-zero power of an
irrational rotation is the identity; explain why density of orbits contradicts
(*).⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(d) The antipodal map $x \mapsto -x$ is a homeomorphism of $S^n$ of order two,
so it generates an action of $\mathbb{Z}_2$. For condition (*), let $y \in S^n$
and take $U$ the open hemisphere centred at $y$; the two images of $U$ under
the action are $U$ and $-U$, the opposite open hemisphere, and these are
disjoint. So the action is a covering space action, and by Proposition 1.40(a)
the quotient map $S^n \to S^n/\mathbb{Z}_2 = \mathbb{R}P^n$ is a normal
covering space.

⟨your step: for $n \geq 2$, Proposition 1.14 gives $\pi_1(S^n) = 0$; feed this
into Proposition 1.40(c) and read off $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}P^n)$. Then take a path
in $S^n$ from a point to its antipode, project it to a loop $\gamma$ in
$\mathbb{R}P^n$, and show $[\gamma]^2 = 1$ by lifting $\gamma \cdot \gamma$ and
observing that the lift is a loop.⟩
</details>
