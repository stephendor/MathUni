## Problem 1 (easy — paying a debt from an-04) [interleaves an-04]

Now that the basic facts about geometric series are available, supply a proof of Corollary 2.4.7: the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^p$ converges if and only if $p > 1$.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.7, p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The terms $1/n^p$ are non-negative and decreasing for $p > 0$, which is exactly the hypothesis of an-04's Theorem 2.4.6.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Condense: the series $\sum 2^n b_{2^n}$ with $b_n = 1/n^p$ is $\sum 2^n / 2^{np} = \sum (2^{1-p})^n$, a geometric series. Apply Example 2.7.5 to decide when it converges, then read the answer back through Theorem 2.4.6. Treat $p \le 0$ separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$2^n b_{2^n} = 2^n \cdot (2^n)^{-p} = 2^{n(1-p)} = (2^{1-p})^n$, so the condensed series is geometric with ratio $r = 2^{1-p}$, and Example 2.7.5 makes it converge exactly when $|r| < 1$. Since $r > 0$, this is $2^{1-p} < 1$, i.e. $1 - p < 0$, i.e. $p > 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For $p > 0$ the sequence $b_n = 1/n^p$ is decreasing and non-negative, so Theorem 2.4.6 applies and $\sum 1/n^p$ converges if and only if the condensed series does, which by the computation above happens exactly when $p > 1$.

Do not skip the other case, and say why it is separate: for $p \le 0$ the terms $1/n^p = n^{-p}$ do not tend to $0$, so Theorem 2.7.3 rules out convergence immediately, and Theorem 2.4.6 was never applicable because the terms are not decreasing. A proof that quotes the condensation test for all $p$ has used a hypothesis it does not have.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — the other half of a theorem)

Prove Theorem 2.7.1 part (ii): if $\sum a_k = A$ and $\sum b_k = B$, then $\sum (a_k + b_k) = A + B$.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.8, p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Do not work with the series. Work with the partial sums, which are what the series is defined to be.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Write $u_m$ for the $m$-th partial sum of $\sum(a_k + b_k)$ and show $u_m = s_m + t_m$, where $(s_m)$ and $(t_m)$ are the partial sums of the two given series. Then quote an-03's Algebraic Limit Theorem for sequences.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$u_m = \sum_{k=1}^{m}(a_k + b_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{m} a_k + \sum_{k=1}^{m} b_k = s_m + t_m$, the middle step being commutativity and associativity of a *finite* sum.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By hypothesis $(s_m) \to A$ and $(t_m) \to B$, so $(u_m) \to A + B$ by the sum rule of an-03's Theorem 2.3.3, and by the definition of a series this says $\sum (a_k + b_k) = A + B$.

The middle step of the Partial is the whole content and deserves a sentence: rearranging a finite sum is legitimate for reasons that have nothing to do with analysis, and the unit's rearrangement theorem exists precisely because that permission does not extend to infinite sums. Notice also that the theorem gives no product rule; you are not asked for one, and Problem 3 shows why products need more care.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — squaring, with and without absolute convergence)

(a) Show that if $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely, then $\sum a_n^2$ also converges absolutely.
(b) Does the proposition in (a) hold if $\sum a_n$ converges only conditionally? Prove it or give a counterexample.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.5(a), p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Absolute convergence gives you $\sum |a_n|$ convergent, hence — by Theorem 2.7.3 applied to that series — control on the size of $|a_n|$ for large $n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show $|a_n| < 1$ eventually, so that $a_n^2 = |a_n|^2 \le |a_n|$ from that point on, and apply the Comparison Test. For (b), look for a conditionally convergent series whose squares are the harmonic series.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Since $\sum |a_n|$ converges, Theorem 2.7.3 gives $|a_n| \to 0$, so there is $N$ with $|a_n| < 1$ for $n \ge N$. For such $n$, $0 \le a_n^2 \le |a_n|$. Theorem 2.7.4 then compares $\sum a_n^2$ against $\sum |a_n|$ — legitimately, since the Comparison Test's hypothesis is only required eventually.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Because $a_n^2 \ge 0$, convergence of $\sum a_n^2$ is the same as absolute convergence, so (a) is done.

For (b) the answer is no. Take $a_n = (-1)^{n+1}/\sqrt{n}$. The terms decrease in absolute value to $0$, so Theorem 2.7.7 makes $\sum a_n$ converge; the absolute series is $\sum 1/\sqrt{n}$, which diverges by Corollary 2.4.7 with $p = 1/2$, so the convergence is conditional by Definition 2.7.8. But $a_n^2 = 1/n$, and $\sum 1/n$ diverges.

The moral is worth stating: squaring destroys the cancellation that conditional convergence depends on. Every hypothesis in (a) that mentions absolute values is there for that reason.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the Ratio Test, built rather than quoted)

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ have $a_n \ne 0$ and satisfy $\lim |a_{n+1}/a_n| = r < 1$. Prove that the series converges absolutely, in three steps.

(a) Let $r'$ satisfy $r < r' < 1$; explain why such an $r'$ exists, and why there is an $N$ with $|a_{n+1}| \le |a_n| r'$ for all $n \ge N$.
(b) Why must $|a_N| \sum (r')^n$ converge?
(c) Conclude that $\sum |a_n|$ converges.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.9, p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (a), the existence of $r'$ is the density of $\mathbb{R}$ between two distinct reals; the value of $N$ comes from the definition of the limit applied with $\epsilon = r' - r$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Iterate the inequality in (a) to get $|a_{N+k}| \le |a_N| (r')^k$, compare against a geometric series using Theorem 2.7.4, and finish with Theorem 2.7.6 if you want the original series rather than the absolute one.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Apply the definition of the limit with $\epsilon = r' - r > 0$: there is $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $\bigl| |a_{n+1}/a_n| - r \bigr| < r' - r$, hence $|a_{n+1}/a_n| < r'$, hence $|a_{n+1}| \le |a_n| r'$ since $|a_n| > 0$. Induction on $k$ then gives $|a_{N+k}| \le |a_N|(r')^k$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |a_N| (r')^k$ is geometric with ratio $r' \in (0,1)$, so it converges to $|a_N|/(1-r')$ by Example 2.7.5. The terms of $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |a_{N+k}|$ are non-negative and dominated by it, so Theorem 2.7.4 gives convergence, and a series converges if and only if its tail does — adding back the finitely many terms $|a_1|, \dots, |a_{N-1}|$ changes the sum but not the convergence. Hence $\sum |a_n|$ converges, which is absolute convergence of $\sum a_n$.

Two remarks earn marks. Why the test says nothing when $r = 1$: both $\sum 1/n$ and $\sum 1/n^2$ have ratio limit $1$, and one diverges while the other converges, so no conclusion can depend only on that number. And why $r'$ is needed at all rather than using $r$ itself: the limit being $r$ does not make $|a_{n+1}/a_n| \le r$ for any $n$, only eventually close to $r$, and the strict room between $r$ and $r'$ is what converts "close to" into a usable inequality.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium — a product that behaves better than its factors)

Give an example to show that it is possible for both $\sum x_n$ and $\sum y_n$ to diverge while $\sum x_n y_n$ converges.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.4, p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You already know a divergent series and a convergent one that differ by a single exponent.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Termwise multiplication squares exponents, and Corollary 2.4.7 draws its line between $p = 1$ and $p > 1$. That is enough to build the example without any cleverness about signs.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Take $x_n = y_n = 1/n$. Then $\sum x_n$ and $\sum y_n$ are both the harmonic series, which diverges, while $\sum x_n y_n = \sum 1/n^2$ converges.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Justify each of the three claims by citation rather than by assertion: $\sum 1/n$ diverges by an-04's grouping argument, or by Corollary 2.4.7 with $p = 1$, and $\sum 1/n^2$ converges by Corollary 2.4.7 with $p = 2$.

Then say what the example shows about the shape of the theory. Theorem 2.7.1 gives an algebra of series for sums and scalar multiples and stops there; this example is one reason it stops. Termwise multiplication is not an operation under which convergence behaves, in either direction — Problem 3(b) gave a conditionally convergent series whose termwise square diverges, and this gives divergent series whose termwise product converges.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — comparing against the p-series)

(a) Show that if $a_n > 0$ and $\lim (n a_n) = l$ with $l \ne 0$, then $\sum a_n$ diverges.
(b) Assume $a_n > 0$ and that $\lim (n^2 a_n)$ exists. Show that $\sum a_n$ converges.

*(Abbott §2.7, Exercise 2.7.10, p. 68)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In both parts the hypothesis says $a_n$ behaves like a multiple of $1/n$ or of $1/n^2$ for large $n$. Turn "behaves like" into a two-sided inequality valid past some index, using the definition of the limit with a well-chosen $\epsilon$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), note $l > 0$ since $a_n > 0$ and limits preserve non-strict inequalities; then take $\epsilon = l/2$ to get $n a_n > l/2$, i.e. $a_n > (l/2)(1/n)$, and compare downwards. For (b), a convergent sequence is bounded, so $n^2 a_n \le M$ and $a_n \le M/n^2$; compare upwards.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Suppose $l < 0$. Then some $n a_n < 0$ for large $n$, contradicting $a_n > 0$; and $l \ne 0$ by hypothesis, so $l > 0$. Choosing $\epsilon = l/2$ in the definition of $\lim(n a_n) = l$ gives an $N$ with $n a_n > l/2$ for all $n \ge N$, hence $a_n > (l/2)/n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $\sum (l/2)/n$ diverges — the harmonic series scaled by a positive constant, and Theorem 2.7.1(i) says scaling cannot change convergence to divergence — the divergent half of Theorem 2.7.4 applies to $0 \le (l/2)/n \le a_n$ and $\sum a_n$ diverges.

(b) $(n^2 a_n)$ converges, so it is bounded by an-03's Theorem 2.3.2: there is $M > 0$ with $n^2 a_n \le M$ for all $n$, hence $0 \le a_n \le M/n^2$. Corollary 2.4.7 makes $\sum 1/n^2$ convergent, Theorem 2.7.1(i) scales it by $M$, and the convergent half of Theorem 2.7.4 finishes.

Notice that part (b) needs much less than part (a): only boundedness of $(n^2 a_n)$ was used, not the existence of its limit. Say so — identifying the weakest hypothesis your proof actually consumed is a habit worth more than the two marks it earns here, and an-08 will make it the whole point of a section.
</details>
