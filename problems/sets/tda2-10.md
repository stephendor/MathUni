## Problem 1 (easy — a sheaf on an interval, by hand) [interleaves la-06]

Let $X$ be the cell complex with two vertices $a, b$ and one edge $e$ joining them.
Define a cellular sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ over $X$ by $\mathcal{F}(a) = \mathcal{F}(b) = \mathbb{R}$,
$\mathcal{F}(e) = \mathbb{R}$, with both restriction maps
$\mathcal{F}(a \triangleleft e)$ and $\mathcal{F}(b \triangleleft e)$ equal to the identity.

(a) Write out the cochain complex $0 \to C^0(X;\mathcal{F}) \to C^1(X;\mathcal{F}) \to 0$,
giving the dimension of each term and the matrix of $d$ in the obvious bases.
(b) Compute $H^0(X;\mathcal{F})$ and $H^1(X;\mathcal{F})$.
(c) State what Lemma 9.5 says your $H^0$ should be, and check that your answer agrees.

*(Ghrist §9.3, Lemma 9.5, p. 184)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$C^n$ is the product of the stalks over the $n$-cells, and $d(\sigma) = \sum_{\sigma \triangleleft \tau} [\sigma:\tau]\,\mathcal{F}(\sigma \triangleleft \tau)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
With the edge oriented from $a$ to $b$, the incidence numbers are $[a:e] = -1$ and $[b:e] = +1$ (or the reverse — fix a convention and keep it). The coboundary is then a difference of the two restrictions.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$C^0 \cong \mathbb{R}^2$, $C^1 \cong \mathbb{R}$, and $d(x_a, x_b) = x_b - x_a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $C^0(X;\mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(a) \times \mathcal{F}(b) \cong \mathbb{R}^2$ and $C^1(X;\mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(e) \cong \mathbb{R}$. Orienting $e$ from $a$ to $b$, $d(x_a,x_b) = x_b - x_a$, with matrix $(-1\ \ 1)$.
(b) $H^0 = \ker d = \{(x,x)\} \cong \mathbb{R}$; $H^1 = \operatorname{coker} d = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{R} = 0$.
(c) Lemma 9.5 says $H^0(X;\mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(X)$, the space of global sections. A global section here is a choice of value at $a$ and at $b$ agreeing after restriction to $e$, i.e. a single real number — which matches $H^0 \cong \mathbb{R}$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — the sheaf that is a matrix)

Keep $X$ as in Problem 1, but now set $\mathcal{F}(a) = \mathbb{R}^m$,
$\mathcal{F}(b) = 0$, $\mathcal{F}(e) = \mathbb{R}^n$, with
$\mathcal{F}(b \triangleleft e) = 0$ and $\mathcal{F}(a \triangleleft e) = A$ for a
linear map $A : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$.

(a) Show that $H^0(X;\mathcal{F}) \cong \ker A$ and $H^1(X;\mathcal{F}) \cong \operatorname{coker} A$.
(b) Interpret each of these in terms of the linear system $Ax = y$: which one measures
uniqueness of solutions, and which measures solvability?
(c) Using Lemma 9.5, say in words what a "global section" of this sheaf is.

*(Ghrist §9.3, Lemma 9.5 p. 184 and Example 9.6 p. 185)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
With $\mathcal{F}(b) = 0$ the cochain complex is just $\mathbb{R}^m \xrightarrow{\ \pm A\ } \mathbb{R}^n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Cohomology of a two-term complex is kernel and cokernel; the sign from the incidence number does not change either.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$C^0 = \mathbb{R}^m \oplus 0$, $C^1 = \mathbb{R}^n$, $d = \pm A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $C^0 = \mathcal{F}(a) \times \mathcal{F}(b) = \mathbb{R}^m$ and $C^1 = \mathcal{F}(e) = \mathbb{R}^n$. The coboundary is $d(x) = [a:e]\,Ax + [b:e]\cdot 0 = \pm Ax$. Hence $H^0 = \ker(\pm A) = \ker A$ and $H^1 = \mathbb{R}^n / \operatorname{im}(\pm A) = \operatorname{coker} A$.
(b) $H^0 = \ker A$ is the failure of uniqueness: the homogeneous solutions. $H^1 = \operatorname{coker} A$ is the obstruction to solvability: $Ax = y$ is soluble exactly when $y$ vanishes in $\operatorname{coker} A$. This is the sense in which sheaf cohomology is an obstruction theory.
(c) By Lemma 9.5 a global section is an element of $H^0$: here, an assignment of a vector at $a$ (and the zero vector at $b$) whose restrictions along $e$ agree — that is, a vector killed by $A$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — constant and skyscraper sheaves)

(a) Let $\mathbf{G}_X$ be the constant sheaf on a compact cell complex $X$: every
stalk is $\mathbf{G}$ and every restriction map is the identity. Show that the
cellular sheaf cochain complex of $\mathbf{G}_X$ is the ordinary cellular cochain
complex, and hence that $H^{\bullet}(X;\mathbf{G}_X) \cong H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{cell}}(X;\mathbf{G})$.
(b) Let $\mathbf{G}_{\sigma}$ be the skyscraper sheaf at a cell $\sigma$: the stalk is
$\mathbf{G}$ at $\sigma$ and $0$ at every other cell. Show that its coboundary maps all
vanish, and deduce that $H^{\bullet}(X;\mathbf{G}_{\sigma})$ is $\mathbf{G}$ in grading
$\dim \sigma$ and $0$ elsewhere.
(c) What does (a) tell you about the relationship between this unit and at2-03?

*(Ghrist §9.2, the constant and skyscraper sheaves pp. 181–182; §9.3, their
cochain complexes and cohomology p. 184)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), a non-zero coboundary out of grading $n$ needs a non-zero stalk on an $n$-cell *and* on an $(n+1)$-cell.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
In (a), compare the formula $d(\sigma) = \sum_{\sigma \triangleleft \tau}[\sigma:\tau]\,\mathcal{F}(\sigma \triangleleft \tau)$ with the cellular coboundary when every restriction map is the identity.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For the skyscraper, $C^n = 0$ unless $n = \dim\sigma$, so the complex has a single non-zero term.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $C^n(X;\mathbf{G}_X) = \prod_{\dim\sigma = n}\mathbf{G} = C^n_{\mathrm{cell}}(X;\mathbf{G})$, and with every restriction the identity the coboundary reduces to $d(\sigma) = \sum_{\sigma\triangleleft\tau}[\sigma:\tau]$, which is precisely the cellular coboundary. Identical complexes give identical cohomology.
(b) $C^n(X;\mathbf{G}_\sigma) = \prod_{\dim\tau = n} \mathbf{G}_\sigma(\tau)$ is $\mathbf{G}$ when $n = \dim\sigma$ and $0$ otherwise. A coboundary $C^n \to C^{n+1}$ therefore has zero source or zero target for every $n$, so $d = 0$ throughout and the cohomology equals the cochain complex: $\mathbf{G}$ in grading $\dim\sigma$, zero elsewhere.
(c) It shows that at2-03's cellular cohomology is the special case of this construction in which the coefficient group is the same everywhere. Sheaf cohomology is what you get by letting the coefficients vary from cell to cell — the constant sheaf recovers the old theory exactly.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — relative sections)

Let $A$ be a subcomplex of $X$ and $\mathcal{F}$ a cellular sheaf on $X$.

(a) State Corollary 9.7 precisely.
(b) Take $X$ as in Problem 1 (with the identity restriction maps) and $A = \{a\}$.
Compute $H^0(X, A; \mathcal{F})$ directly from the relative cochain complex, and check
your answer against Corollary 9.7.
(c) Give the sentence that explains why Corollary 9.7 is described as being "in
keeping with the notion of cohomology as an obstruction to solving certain problems
of extension and restriction".

*(Ghrist §9.3, Corollary 9.7, p. 186)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The relative cochain complex uses only the cells of $X$ not in $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
With $A = \{a\}$ removed, $C^0(X,A;\mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(b)$ and $C^1(X,A;\mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(e)$; work out the coboundary on what remains.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
A global section vanishing at $a$ must, by the identity restriction along $e$, also vanish at $b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Corollary 9.7: for $A$ a subcomplex of $X$, $H^0(X,A;\mathcal{F})$ classifies the global sections of $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ which vanish on $A$.
(b) With $A = \{a\}$: the relative complex is $C^0 = \mathcal{F}(b) = \mathbb{R} \to C^1 = \mathcal{F}(e) = \mathbb{R}$, with $d(x_b) = x_b$ (the $a$-contribution having been quotiented away). So $H^0(X,A;\mathcal{F}) = \ker d = 0$. Corollary 9.7 predicts the same: a global section of Problem 1's sheaf is a constant $(x,x)$, and requiring it to vanish at $a$ forces $x = 0$, leaving only the zero section.
(c) Because $H^0(X,A;\mathcal{F})$ answers a *restriction* question — which global data restricts to zero on $A$ — while the higher groups obstruct *extension*: whether data given on part of the complex can be continued consistently over the rest. Cohomology in this setting is the record of what cannot be done.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — a duality for flow sheaves)

Read Lemma 9.8 and reconstruct its proof.

(a) State the lemma, including what $\tilde{X}$ and $\varphi$ denote.
(b) Give the counting argument that $C^0(\tilde X;\mathcal{F}) \cong C^1(\tilde X;\mathcal{F})$,
being explicit about the bijection between cells it relies on.
(c) Deduce $H^0(\tilde X;\mathcal{F}) \cong H^1(\tilde X;\mathcal{F})$ from (b), and say
carefully why an isomorphism of cochain groups does *not* in general imply an
isomorphism of cohomology groups — so what extra feature of this situation makes the
deduction valid here.
(d) Contrast this "Poincaré duality" with the genuine article you met in at2-07:
which hypotheses does the manifold duality theorem need that play no role at all in
Lemma 9.8?

*(Ghrist §9.4, Lemma 9.8 and its proof, p. 187)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The counting rests on each vertex of $\tilde X$ having stalk isomorphic to the sum of the stalks of its incoming edges.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), the point is that the complex here has only two non-zero terms, so $\dim H^0 - \dim H^1 = \dim C^0 - \dim C^1$ is forced by rank–nullity; with the two cochain dimensions equal, the two cohomology dimensions must agree too.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For a two-term complex $0 \to C^0 \xrightarrow{d} C^1 \to 0$: $\dim H^0 = \dim\ker d$ and $\dim H^1 = \dim C^1 - \operatorname{rank} d$, so $\dim H^0 - \dim H^1 = \dim C^0 - \dim C^1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Lemma 9.8: for a flow sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on the modified network $\tilde X$, $H^0(\tilde X;\mathcal{F}) \cong H^1(\tilde X;\mathcal{F})$, and $\dim H^0(\tilde X;\mathcal{F})$ equals the net flow value $\mathrm{val}(\varphi)$.
(b) Each vertex of $\tilde X$ carries a stalk isomorphic to the direct sum of the stalks on its incoming edges; summing over vertices therefore enumerates every edge exactly once, so $C^0(\tilde X;\mathcal{F}) \cong C^1(\tilde X;\mathcal{F})$.
(c) The Euler-characteristic identity $\dim H^0 - \dim H^1 = \dim C^0 - \dim C^1$ holds for any two-term complex by rank–nullity. Equal cochain dimensions therefore force equal cohomology dimensions. The step is valid *here* because $\tilde X$ is one-dimensional, so the complex stops at $C^1$; for a longer complex, equal dimensions in two gradings would say nothing about their cohomology, since the missing terms could absorb the difference.
(d) Manifold Poincaré duality (at2-07) requires $M$ to be a closed $R$-orientable $n$-manifold and produces the isomorphism via cap product with a fundamental class. Lemma 9.8 needs none of that — no manifold, no orientation, no fundamental class, and the isomorphism is not induced by any pairing. The shared name records an analogy of shape ($H^k \cong H^{n-k}$), not a shared mechanism.
</details>
