## Problem 1 (easy — certifying orthonormal bases)

Suppose $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$. Show that
$$(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\ (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\ (\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)$$
are both orthonormal bases of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 1(a), p. 189)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Two things per list: each vector has norm 1, and the two are orthogonal. Then a theorem from the lesson turns "orthonormal list" into "orthonormal basis" without any further work.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Compute the three quantities for each list — the two norms and the one inner product — using the Euclidean inner product and the identity $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$. Then invoke Theorem 6.28: an orthonormal list of length $\dim\mathbb{R}^2=2$ is an orthonormal basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\|(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\|^2=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$, and likewise for each of the other three vectors. For the first list, $\langle(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\rangle=-\cos\theta\sin\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For the second list, $\langle(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta\sin\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta=0$. Both lists are therefore orthonormal, both have length 2, and Theorem 6.28 makes each an orthonormal basis.

Say explicitly that you have *not* checked spanning, and why you did not need to — that is the point of the exercise. Geometrically the two lists are the rotation by $\theta$ and the reflection in the line at angle $\theta/2$; Axler's part (b) shows these are the only orthonormal bases of $\mathbb{R}^2$, which is worth stating even though it is not asked for here.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — running Gram–Schmidt)

On $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$, consider the inner product
$$\langle p,q\rangle=\int_0^1 p(x)q(x)\,dx.$$
Apply the Gram–Schmidt Procedure to the basis $1,x,x^2$ to produce an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 5, p. 190)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Follow Theorem 6.31 literally, one step at a time, and normalise before moving on — carrying an unnormalised vector into the next step is the usual source of error. Note that the interval $[0,1]$ is *not* symmetric about the origin, so unlike the lesson's example no term vanishes for free.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Step 1: compute $\|1\|^2=\int_0^1 1\,dx$ and set $e_1$ accordingly. Step 2: subtract $\langle x,e_1\rangle e_1$ from $x$ and normalise. Step 3: subtract both $\langle x^2,e_1\rangle e_1$ and $\langle x^2,e_2\rangle e_2$ from $x^2$ and normalise. Every integral is elementary; keep the arithmetic in exact fractions.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\|1\|^2=1$, so $e_1=1$. $\langle x,e_1\rangle=\int_0^1x\,dx=\tfrac12$, so the second numerator is $x-\tfrac12$, whose squared norm is $\int_0^1(x-\tfrac12)^2dx=\tfrac1{12}$. Hence $e_2=2\sqrt3\left(x-\tfrac12\right)=\sqrt3\,(2x-1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For the third: $\langle x^2,e_1\rangle=\tfrac13$ and $\langle x^2,e_2\rangle=\sqrt3\int_0^1(2x^3-x^2)dx=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}$, so the numerator is $x^2-\tfrac13-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}\cdot\sqrt3(2x-1)=x^2-x+\tfrac16$, whose squared norm is $\tfrac1{180}$. Hence $e_3=6\sqrt5\left(x^2-x+\tfrac16\right)=\sqrt5\,(6x^2-6x+1)$.

So the orthonormal basis is $1,\ \sqrt3(2x-1),\ \sqrt5(6x^2-6x+1)$ — the shifted Legendre polynomials. Verify at least one orthogonality relation and one norm at the end as an arithmetic check; a sign or factor slipped early propagates silently through the remaining steps, and the check costs two integrals.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — orthonormalising while preserving upper-triangularity)

Suppose $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to the basis $(1,0,0),\ (1,1,1),\ (1,1,2)$. Find an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (with the usual inner product) with respect to which $T$ has an upper-triangular matrix, and explain why your basis works.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 3, p. 189)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You are not told what $T$ is, and you do not need to know. Theorem 6.37 says the orthonormalised basis inherits the property — so the work is entirely the Gram–Schmidt computation, and the explanation is entirely the theorem.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply Theorem 6.31 to the given basis. Then justify: upper-triangularity with respect to $v_1,v_2,v_3$ means each $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)$ is invariant under $T$ (la-12, Theorem 5.26); Gram–Schmidt preserves those spans exactly; so the same invariance holds for the $e$'s, and Theorem 5.26 read backwards gives upper-triangularity.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$e_1=(1,0,0)$. For $v_2=(1,1,1)$: $\langle v_2,e_1\rangle=1$, so the numerator is $(0,1,1)$ with norm $\sqrt2$, giving $e_2=\left(0,\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For $v_3=(1,1,2)$: $\langle v_3,e_1\rangle=1$ and $\langle v_3,e_2\rangle=\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}$, so the numerator is $(1,1,2)-(1,0,0)-\left(0,\tfrac32,\tfrac32\right)=\left(0,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)$, with norm $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. Hence $e_3=\left(0,-\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)$.

Check the three orthogonality relations and three norms before writing the justification. Then state the argument in the order given above, and note the one thing that makes it work: Gram–Schmidt does not merely produce *some* orthonormal basis, it produces one with the *same partial spans*, and that is the only property of it being used.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — Riesz, computed)

Find a polynomial $q\in\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$ such that
$$p\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=\int_0^1 p(x)q(x)\,dx$$
for every $p\in\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$, where the inner product is the one from Problem 2.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 7, p. 190)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\varphi(p)=p(1/2)$ is a linear functional on $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$ — check that first. Theorem 6.42 then guarantees $q$ exists, and the proof's formula produces it from any orthonormal basis. You computed one in Problem 2.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use $q=\varphi(e_1)e_1+\varphi(e_2)e_2+\varphi(e_3)e_3$ — no conjugates, since everything is real — with $e_1,e_2,e_3$ from Problem 2. So you need only the three numbers $e_j(1/2)$, which requires no integration at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$e_1(1/2)=1$; $e_2(1/2)=\sqrt3(2\cdot\tfrac12-1)=0$; $e_3(1/2)=\sqrt5\left(\tfrac64-3+1\right)=-\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Hence $q=1\cdot1+0\cdot e_2-\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\cdot\sqrt5(6x^2-6x+1) = 1-\tfrac52(6x^2-6x+1) = -15x^2+15x-\tfrac32$.

Verify it on the basis $1,x,x^2$ rather than trusting the formula: $\int_0^1 q=1$, $\int_0^1 xq=\tfrac12$, $\int_0^1 x^2q=\tfrac14$, matching $p(1/2)$ for $p=1,x,x^2$ respectively — and checking on a basis suffices, since both sides are linear in $p$. That last observation is worth stating: it is why three integrals settle a claim quantified over all of $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$.

Note also that $e_2(1/2)=0$ is not an accident — $e_2$ is odd about the midpoint $x=\tfrac12$ — and spotting it saves a term.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — Gram–Schmidt on a dependent list)

What happens if the Gram–Schmidt Procedure is applied to a list of vectors that is **not** linearly independent? Answer precisely: identify the first step at which the procedure fails, prove that it fails there, and say what the procedure does at every earlier step.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 9, p. 190)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Dependence does not mean every vector is redundant. la-03's Linear Dependence Lemma identifies exactly which one is the first to be, and that index is where you should look.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Let $j$ be the least index with $v_j\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})$ — the Linear Dependence Lemma supplies it. For every earlier index the initial segment is independent, so Theorem 6.31's proof applies verbatim and the procedure behaves normally, giving $\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k)=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ for $k<j$. Then examine the numerator at step $j$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
At step $j$ the numerator is $v_j-\langle v_j,e_1\rangle e_1-\dots-\langle v_j,e_{j-1}\rangle e_{j-1}$. Since $\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_{j-1})=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})$ contains $v_j$, Theorem 6.30 applied within that subspace shows the subtracted sum equals $v_j$ exactly.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So the numerator is $0$, its norm is $0$, and the procedure divides by zero: it is undefined at step $j$ and cannot continue. The output up to step $j-1$ is a perfectly good orthonormal list with the correct partial spans.

Three things earn the marks. Identify $j$ by the Linear Dependence Lemma rather than assuming the redundant vector is the last one — it need not be. Prove the numerator is $0$ rather than asserting it; the argument is that $v_j$ lies in the span of an orthonormal list and Theorem 6.30 reconstructs it exactly from its coefficients there. And say what the natural repair is — skip $v_j$ and continue — noting that the repaired procedure produces an orthonormal basis of $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ rather than a list of length $m$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — counting the orthonormal lists)

Suppose $V$ is a **real** inner product space and $v_1,\dots,v_m$ is a linearly independent list in $V$. Prove that there exist exactly $2^m$ orthonormal lists $e_1,\dots,e_m$ in $V$ such that
$$\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_j)$$
for every $j=1,\dots,m$.

*(Axler §6.B, Exercise 10, p. 191)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Build the list one vector at a time and count the choices available at each step. The Gram–Schmidt output is one such list, so at least one exists; the work is showing each step offers exactly two options.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Suppose $e_1,\dots,e_{j-1}$ have been fixed and satisfy the span conditions. The requirement on $e_j$ is that it be a unit vector in $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)$ orthogonal to $e_1,\dots,e_{j-1}$ — and the set of such vectors, before the unit-length condition, is a subspace whose dimension you should compute. Then count the unit vectors in a $1$-dimensional real subspace.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Let $U_j=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)$, of dimension $j$. The vectors of $U_j$ orthogonal to all of $e_1,\dots,e_{j-1}$ — equivalently to $U_{j-1}$, since those span it — form a subspace of $U_j$; it has dimension $j-(j-1)=1$, because $U_{j-1}$ together with it accounts for all of $U_j$ by the orthogonal decomposition.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
A $1$-dimensional real subspace $\operatorname{span}(w)$ with $\|w\|=1$ contains exactly two unit vectors, $w$ and $-w$: any element is $cw$ with $\|cw\|=|c|$, so $|c|=1$ and over $\mathbb{R}$ that means $c=\pm1$. Hence each of the $m$ steps offers exactly two choices, and the choices are independent, giving $2^m$ lists in total.

Two points to handle with care. Justify that every valid list arises this way — that is, that the span conditions force $e_j$ into the one-dimensional subspace described, so the count is of *all* such lists and not merely of those Gram–Schmidt can produce. And say where **real** is used: over $\mathbb{C}$ the unit vectors in a $1$-dimensional subspace are the $cw$ with $|c|=1$, of which there are infinitely many, and the count would be infinite rather than $2^m$.
</details>
