## Problem 1 (easy — the two definitions, and what each is for)

(a) State Edelsbrunner's definition of a simplicial complex. It has two clauses.
Say what each one rules out, and give a finite set of simplices that satisfies
the first and fails the second.
(b) State the definition of an *abstract* simplicial complex. It has one clause.
Explain why the second clause of (a) has no counterpart here — what is it about
the abstract setting that makes the intersection condition unnecessary?
(c) A $k$-simplex has $2^{k+1} - 1$ faces. Derive that count, and say why the
subtraction is by one rather than by nothing.
(d) Define the star $\operatorname{St} \tau$, the closed star
$\overline{\operatorname{St}} \tau$, and the link $\operatorname{Lk} \tau$.
Edelsbrunner says the star is "generally not closed under taking faces" — give a
concrete $\tau$ and $K$ where it is not, and one where it is.
(e) Let $K$ triangulate a 2-manifold without boundary. Edelsbrunner asserts the
link of an edge is a 0-sphere and the link of a vertex is a 1-sphere. Verify the
edge case by a direct count, using the definition of the link and the manifold
condition.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) The second clause is about *how* two simplices are allowed to meet, not
about whether they meet.
(c) Count subsets of the vertex set.
(e) Around an interior edge of a triangulated surface, how many triangles?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Clause one is closure under faces: if a triangle is in $K$ then so are its
three edges and three vertices. Clause two forbids improper intersections: two
triangles may share an edge or a vertex or nothing, but may not cross. ⟨your
step: exhibit two triangles in $\mathbb{R}^2$ overlapping in a small quadrilateral,
and confirm that each is face-closed while the pair is not a complex.⟩
(c) A $k$-simplex is spanned by $k+1$ affinely independent points. Faces are
convex hulls of **non-empty** subsets, and a set of size $k+1$ has $2^{k+1}$
subsets including the empty one. ⟨your step: say the last sentence.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The pairing in (a)–(b) is the whole point of the problem. The geometric
definition has to police intersections because its simplices are literally
subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$ and can literally overlap. The abstract definition
cannot have that problem, because its simplices are finite sets and the
intersection of two of them *is* a set — and, by the one clause, is in the
complex whenever it is non-empty.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — realization, and the size of the ambient space)

(a) State the Geometric Realization Theorem. Then read its proof and identify
the single inequality on which everything turns.
(b) In the proof, $\operatorname{card}(\alpha \cup \alpha_0) = \operatorname{card}
\alpha + \operatorname{card} \alpha_0 - \operatorname{card}(\alpha \cap \alpha_0)
\le k + k_0 + 2$. Justify that bound. Where does the hypothesis
$\dim \alpha, \dim \alpha_0 \le d$ enter, and where does $2d + 1$ come from?
(c) The proof asks for an injection whose image is "in general position",
meaning any $2d + 2$ or fewer of the points are affinely independent. Say why
$2d+2$ is exactly the number needed, and not $2d + 1$ or $2d + 3$.
(d) Define a vertex scheme and a geometric realization. Are they inverse to each
other? Say precisely in what sense they are and in what sense they are not.
(e) The bibliographic notes assert that the $d$-skeleton of the $(2d+2)$-simplex
does not embed in $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$, and that for $d = 1$ this is $K_5$. Check
the arithmetic of that specialization: what is the $(2d+2)$-simplex for $d=1$,
what is its 1-skeleton, and what does the claim then say?

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) $\operatorname{card} \alpha = \dim \alpha + 1$ for an abstract simplex.
(c) Count the largest possible $\operatorname{card}(\alpha \cup \alpha_0)$.
(e) $2d+2 = 4$, so the simplex has $5$ vertices.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) For an abstract simplex, $\dim \alpha = \operatorname{card} \alpha - 1$, so
$\operatorname{card} \alpha = k+1$ and $\operatorname{card} \alpha_0 = k_0 + 1$.
Dropping the subtracted intersection term can only increase the value, giving
$\le k + k_0 + 2$. Since $A$ has dimension $d$, both $k$ and $k_0$ are at most
$d$, so the bound is $\le 2d + 2$. ⟨your step: now say what general position
buys you about a convex combination of points in $\alpha \cup \alpha_0$, and
finish the argument to the intersection statement.⟩
(e) $2d + 2 = 4$, so the simplex is the 4-simplex on 5 vertices; its 1-skeleton
is every vertex and every edge, i.e. the complete graph on 5 vertices. ⟨your
step: state what non-embeddability in $\mathbb{R}^{2} $ then asserts, and name
the classical result it coincides with.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Statement.* Every abstract simplicial complex of dimension $d$ has a
geometric realization in $\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}$.

*The load-bearing inequality* is $\operatorname{card}(\alpha \cup \alpha_0) \le
2d+2$. Everything else in the proof is bookkeeping around it: general position
was chosen so that *any* $2d+2$ points are affinely independent, so the
inequality is precisely the hypothesis that lets general position apply to
$\alpha \cup \alpha_0$. Once the points of $\alpha \cup \alpha_0$ are affinely
independent, convex combinations of them are unique, and uniqueness is what
forces the two image simplices to meet in the image of $\alpha \cap \alpha_0$
rather than anywhere else. ⟨your step: do (b).⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — maps, mesh, and a proof with a slip in it)

(a) Define barycentric coordinates $b_i(x)$ of a point $x \in |K|$, and say why
every point has exactly one set of them. Which fact from §III.1 about interiors
of faces is doing the work?
(b) Define a vertex map and the simplicial map it induces. Edelsbrunner
describes $b_j$ as having "the shape of a hat, increasing from zero on and
outside the link to one at $u_j$". Say why the link is the right place for the
hat to reach zero.
(c) State the Mesh Lemma. In its proof, $\tau$ and $\upsilon$ are complementary
faces of $\sigma$, and the segment joining their barycenters "splits into two
edges in $\operatorname{Sd} K$, in proportions $1 + \dim \upsilon$ to
$1 + \dim \tau$". Verify this for $\sigma$ a triangle with $\tau$ a vertex and
$\upsilon$ the opposite edge, by computing both barycenters explicitly.
(d) State the Simplicial Approximation Theorem and reproduce its proof. Then
answer this: the proof says "Choose $n$ such that each simplex in
$\operatorname{Sd}^n K$ has diameter less than half of $\lambda$. Then each star
in $K$ has diameter less than $\lambda$." Is "in $K$" right? Decide, and if it
is not, say what it should be and why the factor of one half is there.
(e) Where is compactness of $|K|$ used, and what is the name of the number
$\lambda$? State the general fact about open covers that the proof is quietly
invoking.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(c) The barycenter of a $j$-simplex is the average of its $j+1$ vertices.
(d) A vertex star is a union of simplices sharing that vertex. How far apart can
two points in it be, if each simplex has diameter under $\lambda/2$?
(e) The number is named after Lebesgue.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Put $\sigma = \operatorname{conv}\{u_0, u_1, u_2\}$, $\tau = \{u_0\}$ and
$\upsilon = \operatorname{conv}\{u_1, u_2\}$. Then
$\dim \tau = 0$, $\dim \upsilon = 1$, and
$\dim \tau + \dim \upsilon = 1 = \dim \sigma - 1$, so they are complementary.
The barycenter of $\tau$ is $u_0$ and the barycenter of $\upsilon$ is
$\frac{1}{2}(u_1 + u_2)$. ⟨your step: locate the barycenter of $\sigma$ on that
segment and read off the two proportions; check them against
$1 + \dim \upsilon : 1 + \dim \tau = 2 : 1$.⟩
(d) The conclusion needs stars in the *subdivided* complex to be small, since
that is where the vertex map is being built. ⟨your step: say why a star of a
vertex in $\operatorname{Sd}^n K$ has diameter under $\lambda$ when every
simplex of $\operatorname{Sd}^n K$ has diameter under $\lambda/2$ — and note
that this is exactly what the factor of one half is for.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) is the problem. Read the sentence twice before deciding: the temptation is
to assume the printed proof is right and to invent a reading of "star in $K$"
that makes it work. Ask instead what the *next* sentence needs, which is that
the star lie inside one $g^{-1}(N(v))$, and then ask which complex's stars the
vertex map is defined on. The factor of one half is the tell — it is there for
exactly one reason, and that reason names the complex.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium-hard — the filtration, stated exactly)

(a) State Definition 2.1 in full: filtration over $T$, simplicial filtration,
and filtration *of* $X$. The third is not the first with extra words — it adds
two conditions. Write them down and say what each excludes.
(b) Oudot says a filtration is "a special type of representation of the poset
$(T, \le)$ in the category of topological spaces". Using cat-07's Definition 1.7
and the poset-as-category construction, say exactly what functor a filtration
is, and what makes it *special* among such functors.
(c) Apply the homology functor. Write down, for each $p$, what the persistence
module $\mathsf{H}_p(\mathcal{X})$ has at index $i$ and what it has for a pair
$i \le j$. Then define the $p$-th persistent homology group and say why it is a
subquotient rather than a group in the filtration.
(d) Now the check. Example 2.2 is called "the filtration $\mathcal{X}$ of the
octahedron", indexed over $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and its decomposition begins
$\mathsf{H}_0(\mathcal{X}) \cong \mathbb{I}[1,4] \oplus \mathbb{I}[2,2]$. Read
$\bigcap_{i \in T} X_i$ off that decomposition. Is $\mathcal{X}$ a filtration
*of* the octahedron in the sense of Definition 2.1? Answer yes or no with the
reason, and give the smallest change to the indexing that would make the answer
yes.
(e) Oudot lists three situations in which the modules $\mathsf{H}_*(\mathcal{X})$
have well-defined diagrams, and attributes the first two to Theorem 1.9 and the
third to Theorem 1.13. Say which of the three a *finitely simplicial filtration
over a finite index set* satisfies, and why he says it satisfies more than one.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) One condition is about the union, one about the intersection.
(d) $\mathbb{I}[1,4]$ is a summand of $\mathsf{H}_0$ at index $1$. What does
that force about $X_1$?
(e) Count: finite index set, and finite complexes.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) A summand $\mathbb{I}[1,4]$ contributes a one-dimensional space to
$\mathsf{H}_0(X_1)$, so $\mathsf{H}_0(X_1) \neq 0$, so $X_1 \neq \emptyset$.
Since $X_1 \subseteq X_i$ for every $i \in \{1,2,3,4\}$, we get
$\bigcap_{i \in T} X_i = X_1 \neq \emptyset$. ⟨your step: compare with
Definition 2.1's condition and state the verdict; then say what
$T = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ with $X_0 = \emptyset$ does to it, and whether it changes
any interval in the decomposition. Then check Example 2.5 the same way — it is
introduced as a filtration *of* $X$, over all of $\mathbb{R}$ — and say what
finding the same thing twice does to how you should read the clause.⟩
(e) Finite $T$ gives (i) directly. ⟨your step: say why finitely simplicial also
gives (ii), and why (iii) is not available.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) *Filtration over $T$.* Given $T \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, a family of
topological spaces $X_i$ for $i \in T$ with $X_i \subseteq X_j$ whenever
$i \le j$ in $T$. That is all — no ambient space is required and no condition at
the ends.

*Simplicial.* The $X_i$ are simplicial complexes and $X_i$ is a **subcomplex**
of $X_j$ for $i \le j$; *finitely* simplicial if the complexes are finite. Note
that subcomplex is a stronger demand than subset: it is the reason Problem 1's
face-closure clause had to come first.

*Filtration of $X$.* The $X_i$ are subsets of a common space $X$, and
additionally $\bigcap_{i \in T} X_i = \emptyset$ and $\bigcup_{i \in T} X_i = X$.
The union condition says the filtration exhausts $X$; the intersection condition
says it starts from nothing. ⟨your step: say what each excludes, with an example
for each, and keep the intersection condition in mind for part (d).⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — functions, tameness, and what the strip claims)

(a) Define the sublevel set $F_i$ and the superlevel set $F^i$ of a filter
$f : X \to \mathbb{R}$, and show that $\mathcal{F}_\le$ is a filtration of $X$ in
the full sense of Definition 2.1 — all three conditions, with the argument for
each.
(b) The superlevel-sets filtration is indexed over $\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}$
rather than $\mathbb{R}$. Write out the order on $\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}$ and
say why the reindexing is forced rather than conventional.
(c) State Proposition 2.3. Oudot then argues the conditions are "tight", by
giving $X = \mathbb{Z}$ with $f : n \mapsto n$, and yet says they are not
necessary, by giving the Hawaiian earring. Explain what "tight but not
necessary" means for a pair of sufficient conditions, and say which of the two
examples establishes which half.
(d) Work Example 2.5. Show that the three-point filtration given there is not
the sublevel-sets filtration of any $f$, following Oudot's argument; then state
Proposition 2.6 and say precisely what it rescues and what it does not. Which
diagram — decorated or undecorated — survives, and why is that the weaker claim?
(e) The reflection. This unit's mission strip says: "The filtration is the input
to everything; persistence studies how homology changes along it."

  First: audit "the input to everything" against the two resources. Edelsbrunner
  §III.1 never mentions a filtration; Oudot's §1.1 gives one class of
  construction and his own section list promises three more. Say what the strip
  is entitled to claim from these pages and what it is not.

  Second: the strip says persistence studies how homology *changes* along the
  filtration. Oudot makes a sharper claim on p. 30 — that persistence gives
  finer information than pointwise homology, and says exactly why. Reproduce his
  reason in your own words, and say what would be lost if one computed
  $\mathsf{H}_*(X_i)$ for every $i$ and stopped.

  Third: Definition 1.12 and Theorem 1.13, which Chapter 2 relies on for the
  $\mathfrak{q}$-tame case, are in Chapter 1 and outside this unit's resources.
  Say what a reader of *this unit alone* is and is not entitled to assert about
  filtrations indexed over all of $\mathbb{R}$.

(a) For the intersection condition, consider $i \to -\infty$.
(b) Inclusion runs the other way for superlevel sets.
(d) "Coincides with" is doing a lot of work in Proposition 2.6 — read which of
the two diagrams it is asserted for.
</details>

$\bigcup_i F_i = X$. ⟨your step: do the intersection, and say why it is here —

(c) Sufficient conditions are *tight* when dropping any one of them breaks the

say which property is dropped in the first.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) is where the unit's honesty lives. The temptation is to read Proposition 2.6
