## Problem 1 (easy — $H^0$ from the definition)

(a) A singular $0$-simplex in $X$ is a point of $X$. Explain why a $0$-cochain
$\varphi \in C^0(X;G)$ is therefore an arbitrary function $X \to G$, with no
continuity required, and say which property of $C_0(X)$ you used.
(b) Compute $\delta\varphi(\sigma)$ for a singular $1$-simplex
$\sigma : [v_0,v_1] \to X$ and deduce that $\varphi$ is a cocycle exactly when it
is constant on path-components.
(c) Conclude that $H^0(X;G)$ is the group of functions from path-components of
$X$ to $G$, and reconcile this with the universal coefficient theorem, which for
$n = 0$ gives $H^0(X;G) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(H_0(X),G)$.
(d) Describe $\tilde{H}^0(X;G)$ in the same explicit terms, and say what the
image of $\varepsilon^*$ is.

*(Hatcher §3.1, pp. 197–199)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\delta\varphi = \varphi\partial$, and $\partial\sigma = \sigma(v_1) - \sigma(v_0)$
for a singular $1$-simplex.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), remember $H_0(X)$ is free abelian on the path-components, so a
homomorphism out of it is exactly a function on path-components. For (d), the
augmentation $\varepsilon$ sends every singular $0$-simplex to $1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\delta\varphi(\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma(v_1)) - \varphi(\sigma(v_0))$; this
vanishes for all $\sigma$ iff $\varphi$ agrees at the two ends of every path.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $C_0(X)$ is free abelian with basis the singular $0$-simplices, i.e. the
points of $X$; a homomorphism out of a free abelian group may be prescribed
arbitrarily on a basis, so $\operatorname{Hom}(C_0(X),G)$ is exactly the set of
all functions $X \to G$.
(b) $\delta\varphi(\sigma) = \varphi(\partial\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma(v_1)) - \varphi(\sigma(v_0))$.
Requiring this to vanish for every singular $1$-simplex — that is, for every path
in $X$ — says exactly that $\varphi$ is constant on path-components.
(c) $\operatorname{Im}\delta$ into $C^0$ is zero (there is nothing in degree
$-1$), so $H^0(X;G) = \operatorname{Ker}\delta$ = functions constant on
path-components = functions from the set of path-components to $G$. Since
$H_0(X)$ is free abelian on the path-components,
$\operatorname{Hom}(H_0(X),G)$ is the same group; the universal coefficient
sequence has no Ext term at $n = 0$, so it is an isomorphism, as it must be.
(d) Dualising the augmented complex, $\varepsilon^*$ sends $\varphi : \mathbb{Z} \to G$
to the function $\sigma \mapsto \varphi(1)$, a constant function; as $\varphi(1)$
ranges over $G$, the image of $\varepsilon^*$ is precisely the constant
functions $X \to G$. So $\tilde{H}^0(X;G)$ is the functions constant on
path-components modulo the functions constant on all of $X$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — transpose and read off) [interleaves at1-08]

Let $X$ be a CW complex whose cellular chain complex is
$$0 \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\;d_2 = 2\;} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\;d_1 = 0\;} \mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow 0$$
in degrees $2, 1, 0$.

(a) Compute $H_n(X;\mathbb{Z})$ for all $n$.
(b) Using Theorem 3.5, write down the cellular cochain complex as the transpose
of the above and compute $H^n(X;\mathbb{Z})$ for all $n$.
(c) Check your answer against Corollary 3.3.
(d) Repeat (b) with $G = \mathbb{Z}_2$ and with $G = \mathbb{Z}_3$, and say in
one sentence what each coefficient group can and cannot see.

*(Hatcher §3.1, Theorem 3.5, p. 203; Corollary 3.3, p. 196)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Theorem 3.5 says the cellular cochain complex is the dual of the cellular chain
complex — for finitely generated free groups, literally the transposed matrices.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
A $1 \times 1$ matrix is its own transpose, so the arrows reverse but the
multipliers stay. All the work is in reading kernels and images in the new
direction.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$, $H_2 = 0$; and
$H^0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H^1 = 0$, $H^2 = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{Im} d_1 = \mathbb{Z}$;
$H_1 = \operatorname{Ker} d_1/\operatorname{Im} d_2 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_2$;
$H_2 = \operatorname{Ker} d_2 = 0$.
(b) The dual complex runs
$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{d_1^* = 0} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{d_2^* = 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$
in degrees $0, 1, 2$. So $H^0 = \operatorname{Ker}(0) = \mathbb{Z}$,
$H^1 = \operatorname{Ker}(\times 2)/\operatorname{Im}(0) = 0$, and
$H^2 = \mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{Im}(\times 2) = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
(c) Corollary 3.3: $T_0 = 0$, $T_1 = \mathbb{Z}_2$, so
$H^2 \cong (H_2/T_2) \oplus T_1 = 0 \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$ — agreed.
(d) Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ the map $\times 2$ is zero, giving
$H^0 = H^1 = H^2 = \mathbb{Z}_2$; over $\mathbb{Z}_3$ it is invertible, giving
$H^0 = \mathbb{Z}_3$ and $H^1 = H^2 = 0$. A coefficient group detects exactly the
torsion whose order it fails to invert.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — relative cochains and the long exact sequence)

(a) Prove directly, without invoking the universal coefficient theorem, that
$$0 \longleftarrow C^n(A;G) \xleftarrow{\;i^*\;} C^n(X;G) \xleftarrow{\;j^*\;} C^n(X,A;G) \longleftarrow 0$$
is exact, where $C^n(X,A;G) = \operatorname{Hom}(C_n(X,A),G)$. Prove surjectivity
of $i^*$ and identify $\operatorname{Ker} i^*$ explicitly.
(b) Explain why $C^n(X,A;G)$ may be described as the cochains on $X$ that vanish
on singular simplices in $A$, and why the analogous description of *relative
chains* is not available.
(c) State the resulting long exact sequence of the pair $(X,A)$, and say what
makes the short exact sequence of cochain groups into a short exact sequence of
cochain *complexes*.
(d) Taking $A$ to be a point $x_0$, what identification does the reduced long
exact sequence give?

*(Hatcher §3.1, pp. 199–200)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Extend a cochain on $A$ to one on $X$ by assigning the value $0$ to every
singular simplex not in $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The basis of $C_n(X)$ splits as a disjoint union — simplices with image in $A$,
and those without — and a homomorphism out of a free abelian group is free to do
what it likes on each part of a basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\operatorname{Ker} i^*$ consists of cochains taking the value $0$ on singular
$n$-simplices in $A$, and those are exactly the homomorphisms
$C_n(X)/C_n(A) \to G$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $i^*$ restricts a cochain on $X$ to the singular simplices lying in $A$.
Given any $\psi$ on $A$, define $\bar\psi$ on $X$ by $\bar\psi = \psi$ on
simplices in $A$ and $0$ elsewhere; this is a legitimate homomorphism because
$C_n(X)$ is free on the singular $n$-simplices, and $i^*\bar\psi = \psi$, so
$i^*$ is surjective. Its kernel is the cochains vanishing on simplices in $A$,
which are precisely the homomorphisms $C_n(X)/C_n(A) = C_n(X,A) \to G$, i.e.
$C^n(X,A;G)$; and $j^*$ is injective since $C_n(X) \to C_n(X,A)$ is surjective.
(b) Because the basis of $C_n(X)$ is the disjoint union of the simplices with
image in $A$ and those without, a cochain vanishing on the first part is the
same as a cochain on the quotient. Relative *chains* are a quotient of $C_n(X)$,
not a subgroup, so there is no corresponding realisation of them inside
$C_n(X)$ — this is Hatcher's remark that relative cohomology is conceptually a
little simpler than relative homology.
(c) $\cdots \to H^n(X,A;G) \xrightarrow{j^*} H^n(X;G) \xrightarrow{i^*} H^n(A;G) \xrightarrow{\delta} H^{n+1}(X,A;G) \to \cdots$.
It is a short exact sequence of complexes because $i^*$ and $j^*$ commute with
$\delta$, which holds because $i$ and $j$ commute with $\partial$ and dualising
preserves commuting squares.
(d) It identifies $\tilde{H}^n(X;G)$ with $H^n(X,x_0;G)$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — contravariance and homotopy invariance)

(a) Given $f : X \to Y$, define $f^\sharp : C^n(Y;G) \to C^n(X;G)$ and show that
$f_\sharp \partial = \partial f_\sharp$ dualises to $\delta f^\sharp = f^\sharp \delta$,
so that $f^*$ on cohomology is defined.
(b) Prove $(fg)^* = g^* f^*$ and $\mathbb{1}^* = \mathbb{1}$, and state precisely
what it means to say $X \mapsto H^n(X;G)$ is a contravariant functor.
(c) Suppose $f \simeq g$. Starting from a chain homotopy $P$ with
$g_\sharp - f_\sharp = \partial P + P \partial$, produce a chain homotopy between
$f^\sharp$ and $g^\sharp$ and conclude $f^* = g^*$.
(d) The wedge axiom for cohomology involves a direct *product* where the homology
version has a direct *sum*. Identify the natural isomorphism responsible and
explain why the discrepancy is unavoidable.

*(Hatcher §3.1, pp. 201–202; the wedge axiom, p. 202)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Dualising reverses the order of a composite, which is exactly why the functor is
contravariant.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), dualise the whole relation at once: the dual of $\partial P + P\partial$
is $P^*\delta + \delta P^*$ — mind the order in each term.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\operatorname{Hom}(\bigoplus_\alpha A_\alpha, G) \cong \prod_\alpha \operatorname{Hom}(A_\alpha,G)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Set $f^\sharp = (f_\sharp)^*$, i.e. $f^\sharp(\varphi) = \varphi f_\sharp$.
Dualising $f_\sharp\partial = \partial f_\sharp$ and using
$(\alpha\beta)^* = \beta^*\alpha^*$ gives
$\partial^* f_\sharp^* = f_\sharp^* \partial^*$, that is
$\delta f^\sharp = f^\sharp \delta$. So $f^\sharp$ is a cochain map and induces
$f^* : H^n(Y;G) \to H^n(X;G)$.
(b) $(fg)_\sharp = f_\sharp g_\sharp$ dualises to
$(fg)^\sharp = g^\sharp f^\sharp$, whence $(fg)^* = g^* f^*$; and
$\mathbb{1}^\sharp = \mathbb{1}$ gives $\mathbb{1}^* = \mathbb{1}$. Contravariant
functor: it assigns a group to each space and to each map $X \to Y$ a
homomorphism $H^n(Y;G) \to H^n(X;G)$ — in the reverse direction — respecting
composition in reversed order and identities.
(c) Dualising $g_\sharp - f_\sharp = \partial P + P\partial$ gives
$g^\sharp - f^\sharp = P^*\delta + \delta P^*$, so $P^*$ is a chain homotopy
between $f^\sharp$ and $g^\sharp$; chain homotopic cochain maps induce the same
map on cohomology, so $f^* = g^*$. The same restricts to relative cochains,
those vanishing on simplices in the subspaces.
(d) The isomorphism is
$\operatorname{Hom}(\bigoplus_\alpha A_\alpha, G) \cong \prod_\alpha \operatorname{Hom}(A_\alpha,G)$:
a homomorphism out of a direct sum is an arbitrary family of homomorphisms out
of the summands, with no finiteness condition. So the cochain complex of a
disjoint union is the direct *product* of the individual cochain complexes, and
the product survives to cohomology. It is unavoidable because it is a fact about
Hom, not a choice of convention — the same fact that made $H^1$ of a graph a
product in at2-01.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — proving Theorem 3.5)

(a) Show that the universal coefficient theorem forces
$H^k(X^n, X^{n-1};G) = 0$ for $k \neq n$, and deduce from the long exact
sequence of the pair that $H^k(X^n;G) \cong H^k(X^{n-1};G)$ for $k \neq n, n-1$,
hence by induction that $H^k(X^n;G) = 0$ for $k > n$.
(b) Using $H^k(X, X^{n+1};G) = 0$ for $k \leq n+1$, deduce
$H^n(X;G) \cong H^n(X^{n+1};G)$ and assemble the chain of isomorphisms
$H^n(X;G) \cong \operatorname{Ker} d_n / \operatorname{Im} d_{n-1}$.
(c) Prove the second half of Theorem 3.5 — that the cellular cochain complex is
isomorphic to the dual of the cellular chain complex — by showing the relevant
square-pair of maps commutes. Say which of the vertical maps are isomorphisms
and why.
(d) Explain in one paragraph why (c) is the statement that licenses the slogan
"transpose the cellular boundary matrices", and what would go wrong with the
slogan if the cellular chain groups were not free.

*(Hatcher §3.1, Theorem 3.5 and its proof, pp. 202–203)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$H_k(X^n,X^{n-1})$ is free, concentrated in degree $n$; feed that into
Theorem 3.2 and both outer terms vanish for $k \neq n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c) the diagram has $h$ as its three vertical maps; the first and third are
isomorphisms by the universal coefficient theorem, the first square commutes by
naturality of $h$, and the second is the duality between $\delta$ and $\partial$
established for the long exact sequence of a pair.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^n(X;G) \cong H^n(X^{n+1};G) \cong \operatorname{Ker}\delta_n \cong \operatorname{Ker} d_n/\operatorname{Im}\delta_{n-1} \cong \operatorname{Ker} d_n/\operatorname{Im} d_{n-1}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $H_k(X^n,X^{n-1})$ is free abelian on the $n$-cells when $k = n$ and zero
otherwise. Theorem 3.2 gives
$0 \to \operatorname{Ext}(H_{k-1}(X^n,X^{n-1}),G) \to H^k(X^n,X^{n-1};G) \to \operatorname{Hom}(H_k(X^n,X^{n-1}),G) \to 0$.
For $k \neq n$ the Hom term vanishes for degree reasons, and the Ext term
vanishes either because $H_{k-1}$ is zero or because it is free. So the middle
group is zero. The long exact sequence of $(X^n,X^{n-1})$ then has zeros on both
sides of $H^k(X^n;G) \to H^k(X^{n-1};G)$ whenever $k \neq n, n-1$, making it an
isomorphism; induction on $n$, starting from $H^k(X^0;G) = 0$ for $k > 0$, gives
$H^k(X^n;G) = 0$ for $k > n$.
(b) $H^k(X,X^{n+1};G) = 0$ for $k \le n+1$ makes
$H^n(X;G) \to H^n(X^{n+1};G)$ an isomorphism in the long exact sequence of
$(X,X^{n+1})$. The diagram of the cellular cochain complex then supplies the
displayed chain of isomorphisms, $d_n = \delta_n j_n$ with the triangles
commuting and $d_n d_{n-1} = 0$ because $j_n \delta_{n-1} = 0$.
(c) Take the diagram whose top row is
$H^k(X^k,X^{k-1};G) \to H^k(X^k;G) \xrightarrow{\delta} H^{k+1}(X^{k+1},X^k;G)$,
whose bottom row is the same after applying $\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)$ to the
cellular chain complex, and whose verticals are $h$. The composite across the
top is the cellular coboundary $d$; the composite across the bottom is the dual
$\partial^*$ of the cellular boundary. The first and third verticals are
isomorphisms by the universal coefficient theorem (the relevant homology being
free, so the Ext term vanishes and $h$ is onto with zero kernel). The first
square commutes by naturality of $h$; the second commutes by the duality between
the connecting homomorphisms $\delta$ and $\partial$. Hence the two complexes
are isomorphic.
(d) Once the cellular cochain complex is identified with
$\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)$ applied to the cellular chain complex, and the cellular
chain groups are free of finite rank with a basis given by the cells, the dual
of a map is represented by the transposed matrix in the dual bases — so
computing cohomology is literally transposing the boundary matrices from at1-08
and rerunning the linear algebra. Without freeness there are no dual bases to
transpose in, and $\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)$ need not even preserve exactness, so
the matrix picture would break down entirely.
</details>
