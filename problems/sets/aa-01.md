## Problem 1 (easy — division with remainder, including negatives)

For each pair, find the **unique** quotient $q$ and remainder $r$ from Theorem 1.3 (so $a = bq + r$ with $0 \le r < |b|$): (a) $a=100,\ b=7$; (b) $a=-17,\ b=5$; (c) $a=-30,\ b=-4$. Then explain why "$-17 = 5\cdot(-3) - 2$" is *not* the division-with-remainder decomposition of $-17$ by $5$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The remainder must land in the window $0 \le r < |b|$. For negative $a$, the quotient is more negative than you might first guess.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Find the largest multiple of $b$ that does not exceed $a$ (for $b>0$); that multiple is $bq$, and $r = a - bq$. For $b<0$, use $|b|$ for the window and adjust the sign of $q$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $7\cdot 14 = 98 \le 100$, so $q=14$, $r=2$. (b) $5\cdot(-4)=-20\le -17$, so $q=-4$, $r=3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $|b|=4$, need $0\le r<4$. $-30 = (-4)\cdot 8 + 2 = -32 + 2$, and $0\le 2<4$, so $q=8$, $r=2$. The last part: $-2$ violates $0\le r$, so it is outside the mandated window — Theorem 1.3 pins the *unique* decomposition with the remainder in $[0,|b|)$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — induction)

Prove by induction that for all integers $n \ge 1$,
$$1^3 + 2^3 + \cdots + n^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2.$$

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Same shape as the lesson's $1+2+\cdots+n$ proof: check $n=1$, then add the $(n+1)^3$ term to the inductive hypothesis.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
In the step you must show $\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2 + (n+1)^3 = \left(\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}\right)^2$. Factor $(n+1)^2$ out of the left side.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Base $n=1$: LHS $=1$, RHS $=(1\cdot2/2)^2=1$. ✓ Step: LHS $=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2+(n+1)^3 = (n+1)^2\left(\frac{n^2}{4}+(n+1)\right)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$(n+1)^2\left(\frac{n^2}{4}+n+1\right) = (n+1)^2\cdot\frac{n^2+4n+4}{4} = (n+1)^2\cdot\frac{(n+2)^2}{4} = \left(\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}\right)^2$, which is the formula at $n+1$. Conclude by induction.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — well-ordering as an existence tool)

Prove, using the well-ordering principle, that **every integer $n \ge 2$ is a product of (one or more) primes.** (This is the *existence* half of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic — uniqueness is aa-02.)

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Mirror the lesson's "every $n\ge2$ has a prime divisor" proof, but push it all the way to a full factorisation. Take the least counterexample.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Let $S$ be the set of integers $\ge 2$ that are *not* products of primes; assume $S\ne\varnothing$ and take its least element $m$ by well-ordering. Split on whether $m$ is prime.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $m$ is prime, it is a product of one prime — so $m\notin S$, contradiction. So $m$ is composite: $m = de$ with $1<d,e<m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $d,e<m$ and $d,e\ge2$, minimality of $m$ gives $d,e\notin S$: each is a product of primes. Concatenating those two prime factorisations expresses $m=de$ as a product of primes, so $m\notin S$ — contradiction. Hence $S=\varnothing$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — well-ordering, "bounded below ⟹ least element")

Prove that **every nonempty set $T \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ that is bounded below** (there is an integer $L$ with $L \le t$ for all $t\in T$) **has a least element.** (Well-ordering is stated only for sets of *nonnegative* integers — you must reduce to that case.)

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Shift $T$ up by a fixed integer so that it becomes a set of nonnegative integers, apply well-ordering, then shift back.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Consider $T' = \{ t - L : t \in T\}$. Show $T'$ is a nonempty set of nonnegative integers.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
For $t\in T$, $t\ge L$ so $t-L\ge 0$; thus $T'\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\ge0}$ and $T'\ne\varnothing$. By well-ordering $T'$ has a least element $m_0 = t_0 - L$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Claim $t_0$ is least in $T$: for any $t\in T$, $t-L\ge m_0 = t_0-L$, so $t\ge t_0$. Hence $t_0$ is a least element of $T$. (This is why the lesson could speak of "the largest multiple of $b$ not exceeding $a$" — bounded-below sets of integers really do attain their extremes.)
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — using Lemma 1.2)

Using Definition 1.1 and Lemma 1.2, prove: if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$, then $a = \pm b$. (Assume $a,b \ne 0$.)

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Lemma 1.2 says a divisor of a nonzero number is no larger in absolute value than it. Apply it in both directions.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
From $a\mid b$ (with $b\ne0$) get $|a|\le|b|$; from $b\mid a$ (with $a\ne0$) get $|b|\le|a|$. Combine.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The two inequalities force $|a|=|b|$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$|a|=|b|$ means $a=\pm b$. (Concretely: $b=ac$ and $a=bd$ give $a=acd$, so $cd=1$ with $c,d\in\mathbb{Z}$; hence $c=d=1$ or $c=d=-1$, i.e. $b=\pm a$.) Either route is acceptable; the Lemma-1.2 route is the intended one.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — division with remainder as a proof engine)

Prove that $3 \mid (n^3 - n)$ for every integer $n$. Use Theorem 1.3 to split into cases by the remainder of $n$ on division by $3$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
By Theorem 1.3, every $n$ is exactly one of $3k$, $3k+1$, $3k+2$. Handle each case.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Write $n^3-n = (n-1)n(n+1)$ (three consecutive integers) — or substitute $n=3k+r$ directly and expand. Either shows the product is a multiple of 3.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $n=3k$: $n$ itself is a multiple of 3, so $(n-1)n(n+1)$ is. If $n=3k+1$: then $n-1=3k$ is a multiple of 3. 
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If $n=3k+2$: then $n+1 = 3k+3 = 3(k+1)$ is a multiple of 3. In every case one of the three consecutive factors $n-1,\,n,\,n+1$ is divisible by 3, hence so is their product $n^3-n$. Theorem 1.3 is what guarantees these three remainder-cases are exhaustive.
</details>
