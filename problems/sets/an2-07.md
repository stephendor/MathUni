**What the linear structure buys, in one sentence.** A norm is a metric that
respects the vector operations, and Proposition 5.1.3 makes that precise in one
direction. The payoff is not a new kind of convergence — it is that questions
an2-01 to an2-06 could only ask about *one* metric at a time become comparable:
**Theorem 5.1.7** says that on ℝⁿ the question of which norm you use never
matters, and **Example 4** at p. 137 says that on $C([0,1],\mathbf{R})$ it always
might.

differentiation is an *unbounded* linear operator, and by **Theorem 5.4.5**
unbounded means discontinuous. He says so himself, naming
**Proposition 4.3.5** as what the unboundedness cost him. Problem 4 is that
comparison.

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) $V$ is only ever used as a source of vectors $\mathbf{u}$ at which to
evaluate. Every limit taken in the proof is a limit in $W$: $\{A_n(\mathbf{u})\}$
is Cauchy in $W$ for each fixed $\mathbf{u}$, and that is where completeness is
spent. Linearity and boundedness of the limit are algebraic and estimate-level
facts. So no completeness — indeed no topological hypothesis beyond having a norm
— is required of $V$. A striking special case: $\mathcal{L}(V,\mathbf{R})$, the
dual space, is a Banach space for *every* normed $V$.

(e) *True*: a feature space carrying a norm is a normed space, and the ones in
use are complete, so "Banach" is not false. *Understated*: a kernel is defined as
$\langle\Phi(D),\Phi(E)\rangle$, and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is not available
in a Banach space — Definition 5.3.1 is extra structure. tda2-09 says "Hilbert",
which is a Banach space whose norm comes from an inner product, and it is the
inner product that makes the kernel a kernel.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The step is "if we let $m$ go to infinity" in
$\|A_n(\mathbf{u}) - A_m(\mathbf{u})\| \le \epsilon\|\mathbf{u}\|$, giving
$\|A_n(\mathbf{u}) - A(\mathbf{u})\| \le \epsilon\|\mathbf{u}\|$. Lindström names
the licence himself: **Proposition 5.1.4**(i), that the norm is continuous along
convergent sequences.

Replacing $\le$ by $<$ breaks nothing that matters but cannot be *claimed*: a
limit of strict inequalities is only weakly strict, so the conclusion is
$\|A_n - A\| \le \epsilon$ and not $< \epsilon$ — which still gives convergence,
since $\epsilon$ was arbitrary. This is the third time the same $\le$-versus-$<$
point has appeared in `an2`: Lindström flags it himself inside
**Proposition 4.2.3** (an2-05), and it recurs unflagged at the end of
Bernstein's proof of **Proposition 4.10.3** (an2-06).

(d) Theorem 5.4.8 is about the *codomain* — a hypothesis on $W$ alone.
Proposition 5.1.8 is about a *construction* — completeness passes to a finite
product. **Theorem 4.6.4** is the genuinely new fact: nothing in §5.1 or §5.4
proves that $C([a,b],\mathbf{R})$ is complete, and its proof is a separate
argument in Chapter 4. The M-test rests on the third kind — completeness of a
supremum-norm function space — which is precisely why an2-06 had to prove it by
hand.
