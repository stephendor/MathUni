# pw-05 — Proof style: writing and critiquing

**Module:** Proof Workshop · **Unit:** pw-05
**Sources:** Cummings, *Proofs: A Long-Form Mathematics Textbook* — §2.1, Fact 2.1 (every integer is even or odd) p. 36, Definition 2.2 (even, odd) p. 36; §2.4, Theorem 2.11 (the division algorithm, with uniqueness) p. 46; Chapter 1 Pro-Tips p. 27, Chapter 2 Pro-Tips pp. 65–66, Chapter 7 Pro-Tips pp. 237–238.

Submit your written solutions via `/grade pw-05`.

This set is marked on your **critiques** as much as on your proofs. A critique names the defect, says which of the four obligations from the pw-05 lesson it breaks, and says what the defect costs — whether the line is false, unjustified, or merely unclear. "This proof is wrong" is not a critique.

## Problem 1 (easy — grade a proof) [interleaves pw-01]

Here is a submitted proof. Find every defect, name the obligation each one breaks, and state for each whether it makes a line **false**, a step **unjustified**, or the argument **incomplete**.

> **Claim.** The product of two even integers is divisible by 4.
>
> **Proof.** Let $a$ and $b$ be even. Then $a = 2k$ and $b = 2k$, so $ab = 4k^2$, which is divisible by 4. ∎

Then write a correct proof.

*(Cummings, Definition 2.2, p. 36; the reused-letter defect is the one Chapter 2 Pro-Tips warns about, pp. 65–66)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Read the two applications of the definition as if you were a compiler: what does the second one assert, given the first?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Go through the four obligations in order: is every variable introduced with its type and quantifier? Is every non-arithmetic step named? Is anything left to the reader? Does the proof end where it claimed?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Two defects. The letter $k$ is reused, which silently adds the hypothesis $a = b$; and $k$'s type is never given, so "$a = 2k$" is not Definition 2.2. Decide for each whether it makes a line false or the argument incomplete — they are not the same verdict.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The reused letter makes the argument **incomplete**: everything written is true of the pairs it covers, and it covers only $a = b$. The missing type makes the invocation of Definition 2.2 **unjustified** rather than false. Correct proof: let $a, b$ be arbitrary even integers; by Definition 2.2 there are integers $k, m$ with $a = 2k$, $b = 2m$; then $ab = 4km$ with $km$ an integer by Fact 2.1, so $4 \mid ab$; as $a, b$ were arbitrary, the claim holds. Note it is one line longer and covers infinitely more cases.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the missing fact)

Each step below is a step a student wrote. For each, either name the definition, fact or theorem that licenses it, or state that no such result licenses it and say what is missing.

(a) "$n$ is an integer and $n$ is not even, so $n$ is odd."
(b) "$n$ is odd, so $n$ is not even."
(c) "$a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, so $a \mid c$."
(d) "$n^2$ is odd and $n^2$ is even, contradiction."

*(Cummings, Fact 2.1 p. 36 and Theorem 2.11 p. 46)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Two of these four lean on the same asymmetry, and it is the one the pw-05 lesson's Error 4 turned on. Which direction does Fact 2.1 actually give you?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Fact 2.1 asserts that every integer has *at least* one parity. Nothing in it asserts *at most* one. Sort the four steps by which of the two they need.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) needs "at least one parity" — that is exactly Fact 2.1. (b) and (d) need "at most one parity", which Fact 2.1 does not provide. (c) needs the definition of divisibility and a construction, not a named theorem about parity at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The exclusivity in (b) and (d) follows from the uniqueness clause of Theorem 2.11 (p. 46): with $m = 2$, the remainder $r$ satisfying $0 \le r < 2$ is unique, so no integer can be written both as $2q$ and as $2q' + 1$. Cite it that way rather than writing "clearly". For (c), write the two-line construction out: $b = ad$, $c = be$, so $c = a(de)$ with $de$ an integer.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — technique laundering)

> **Claim.** For all integers $n$, if $3n + 2$ is odd then $n$ is odd.
>
> **Proof.** Assume for contradiction that $3n + 2$ is odd and that $n$ is even. Then $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, so $3n + 2 = 6k + 2 = 2(3k + 1)$, which is even. This contradicts our assumption that $3n + 2$ is odd. Therefore $n$ is odd. ∎

(a) Is this argument valid? (b) Which proof technique is it *really*? (c) Rewrite it under that label. (d) In two or three sentences, say what the contradiction wrapper cost — not whether it is allowed, but what information it destroyed.

*(Cummings, Chapter 7 Pro-Tips, pp. 237–238)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Trace which assumptions are actually used. Is "$3n+2$ is odd" used anywhere in the derivation, or only at the final line?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Cummings's diagnostic: if you assume $P$ and $\lnot Q$ and your argument establishes $\lnot P$ (or $Q$) outright, without using the other assumption, then you have written a direct or contrapositive proof and wrapped it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The derivation goes from "$n$ is even" to "$3n + 2$ is even" and never touches the assumption that $3n+2$ is odd. That is the contrapositive: $\lnot Q \Rightarrow \lnot P$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Valid. (b) A proof by contrapositive. (c) "Let $n$ be an arbitrary integer. We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is even then $3n+2$ is even. …" (d) The wrapper destroyed the *single target*. A contrapositive proof aims at one statement and a slip leaves you visibly short of it; a contradiction proof accepts any falsehood, so a slip can manufacture its own contradiction and end looking exactly like a success. Cite pp. 237–238 for that asymmetry rather than asserting it.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — write, then critique yourself) [interleaves pw-02]

(a) Prove by induction that $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = n(n+1)/2$ for every $n \ge 1$.

(b) Then critique your own proof, in a paragraph, as if marking a stranger's. Address specifically: did you state the inductive hypothesis with its quantifier? Did you say which values of $n$ the base case covers and which the induction step covers? Is there a step where you asked the reader to supply a fact?

(c) Finally, state which technique you used and, per the pw-05 lesson, whether an arithmetic slip in your induction step would have announced itself.

*(Cummings, Chapter 1 Pro-Tips on writing too little rather than too much, p. 27; the four-part induction scaffold is pw-02's)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The mathematics here is the easiest in the set; every mark is in (b) and (c). Write (a) first, then leave it for ten minutes before critiquing it — you will find more.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Mark your own proof against the four obligations, in order, and quote the offending phrase rather than describing it. A critique that quotes is checkable; one that paraphrases is not.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The most common self-critique findings here: "assume it holds for $k$" without saying $k \ge 1$ is an integer; "adding $k+1$ to both sides" without noting the inductive hypothesis is what licenses the substitution; and a conclusion that omits "as $n$ was arbitrary" or its induction equivalent.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The induction step is a direct argument with one target — the closed form at $k+1$ — so a slip leaves you unable to reach $(k+1)(k+2)/2$ and is self-announcing. Say that in the terms the lesson used, and note the contrast with what would have happened had you dressed the same argument as a contradiction.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — plant and defend)

Write a proof of a **true** statement of your choice from pw-01 through pw-04, containing exactly **three** planted defects: one that makes a written line false, one that leaves a step unjustified, and one that leaves the argument incomplete. Submit:

(a) the flawed proof, with no marks on it;
(b) a separate answer key naming the three defects, their type, and the obligation each breaks;
(c) the corrected proof;
(d) one paragraph on which of your three defects you think a marker is least likely to catch, and why.

Do not reuse the lesson's flawed proof or the faded example from it.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The hard part is (d), and it is where the marks are. A defect that survives a careful reader is more instructive than a spectacular one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The lesson's Error 3 is the model for a false line that leaves no downstream trace: the conclusion drawn from it stayed true, so nothing later looked wrong. Aim for that property rather than for a defect that derails the proof visibly.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Useful shapes: an algebraic regrouping that is off by a constant while the parity or sign of the result is unchanged; a "clearly" over a step that needs a uniqueness clause; a proof of the universal that fixes an object and never says it is arbitrary.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For (d), the expected answer is the false line, and the reason is the one self-check 3 measured: four of the lesson's five errors were failures to declare or name something and were visible without arithmetic, so a reader who audits the obligations first catches those and can still walk past a wrong sum. If your answer is different, defend it — a well-argued disagreement earns full marks.
</details>
