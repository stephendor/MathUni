## Problem 1 (easy — the quantifier that does the work) [interleaves an-04]

A sequence $(s_n)$ is called *pseudo-Cauchy* if, for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $|s_{n+1} - s_n| < \epsilon$.

(a) Explain precisely how this differs from Definition 2.6.1.
(b) Give an example of a divergent sequence that is pseudo-Cauchy, or argue that none exists.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.3, p. 61)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Write both conditions out with all quantifiers visible and count the free indices in each.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b), you already own a divergent sequence whose consecutive differences tend to zero: an-04 proved the partial sums of $\sum 1/n$ diverge, and the $n$-th difference of those partial sums is exactly $1/(n+1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Pseudo-Cauchy controls $|s_{n+1} - s_n|$ — one free index, and only *adjacent* terms. Definition 2.6.1 controls $|s_n - s_m|$ for all $m, n \ge N$ — two free indices, and every pair in the tail. Pseudo-Cauchy is therefore implied by Cauchy (take $m = n+1$) and, as (b) shows, is strictly weaker.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Let $s_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} 1/k$. Given $\epsilon > 0$ choose $N > 1/\epsilon$; then for $n \ge N$, $|s_{n+1} - s_n| = 1/(n+1) < 1/N < \epsilon$, so $(s_n)$ is pseudo-Cauchy. It diverges by an-04's grouping argument on the harmonic series.

Say in one sentence *why* the two conditions come apart, because that is the marked content and not the example. Adjacent terms may each move a tiny amount and still, over many steps, carry the sequence an unbounded distance; controlling every pair at once forbids that, because $|s_n - s_m|$ includes pairs arbitrarily far apart in the tail.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — a triangle inequality drill)

Assume $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are Cauchy sequences. Use a triangle inequality argument to prove directly that $c_n = |a_n - b_n|$ is a Cauchy sequence.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.4, p. 62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You need to bound $\bigl| |a_n - b_n| - |a_m - b_m| \bigr|$. The reverse triangle inequality, $\bigl||x| - |y|\bigr| \le |x - y|$, converts a difference of absolute values into an absolute value of a difference.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply the reverse triangle inequality with $x = a_n - b_n$ and $y = a_m - b_m$, regroup as $(a_n - a_m) - (b_n - b_m)$, then use the ordinary triangle inequality and the two Cauchy hypotheses at $\epsilon/2$ each.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$$\bigl| |a_n - b_n| - |a_m - b_m| \bigr| \le |(a_n - b_n) - (a_m - b_m)| = |(a_n - a_m) - (b_n - b_m)| \le |a_n - a_m| + |b_n - b_m|.$$
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Given $\epsilon > 0$, Cauchyness of $(a_n)$ gives $N_1$ with $|a_n - a_m| < \epsilon/2$ for $m,n \ge N_1$, and Cauchyness of $(b_n)$ gives $N_2$ with $|b_n - b_m| < \epsilon/2$ for $m,n \ge N_2$. Set $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$ — the step to state explicitly, since a single threshold must serve both hypotheses. For $m,n \ge N$ the displayed chain gives $|c_n - c_m| < \epsilon$.

Note the word *directly* in the problem. Theorem 2.6.4 would let you convert both sequences to convergent ones and quote the Algebraic Limit Theorem, and that proof is correct but answers a different question: the point of the exercise is that the Cauchy property is closed under this operation for reasons internal to the definition.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — sums and products, the hard way on purpose)

If $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ are Cauchy sequences, there is an easy proof that $(x_n + y_n)$ is Cauchy: by Theorem 2.6.4 both converge, the Algebraic Limit Theorem makes the sum converge, and Theorem 2.6.2 makes it Cauchy again.

(a) Give a direct argument that $(x_n + y_n)$ is Cauchy, using neither Theorem 2.6.4 nor the Algebraic Limit Theorem.
(b) Do the same for the product $(x_n y_n)$.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.5, p. 62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), the obstruction is that $x_n y_n - x_m y_m$ is not a sum of two small things until you make it one. Add and subtract a mixed term $x_n y_m$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$x_n y_n - x_m y_m = x_n(y_n - y_m) + y_m(x_n - x_m)$. Each bracket is small; each coefficient needs a bound, and Lemma 2.6.3 supplies one for both sequences at once.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Let $M > 0$ bound both $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$, which exists by Lemma 2.6.3 applied to each and then taking the larger bound. Then
$$|x_n y_n - x_m y_m| \le |x_n||y_n - y_m| + |y_m||x_n - x_m| \le M\bigl(|y_n - y_m| + |x_n - x_m|\bigr).$$
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose thresholds so that $|x_n - x_m| < \epsilon/(2M)$ and $|y_n - y_m| < \epsilon/(2M)$ past the larger of them; the display then gives $|x_n y_n - x_m y_m| < \epsilon$.

Two details carry the marks. $M$ must be strictly positive before you divide by it — if both sequences were identically zero the bound from Lemma 2.6.3 could be taken as $0$, so take $M$ to be the bound plus one and the division is always legal. And the appeal to Lemma 2.6.3 is the only place the Cauchy hypothesis is used for anything other than smallness; state it, because it is precisely the step that would be missing if you tried the same argument for pseudo-Cauchy sequences.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the first arrow reversed)

Use the Monotone Convergence Theorem to prove the Nested Interval Property: if $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ are closed intervals with $I_n \supseteq I_{n+1}$ for every $n$, then $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n \ne \emptyset$.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.6(b), p. 62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Nesting says something about $(a_n)$ and something about $(b_n)$ separately. Say what, in the vocabulary of Definition 2.4.1.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$I_{n+1} \subseteq I_n$ means $a_n \le a_{n+1}$ and $b_{n+1} \le b_n$. So $(a_n)$ is increasing and $(b_n)$ is decreasing, and every $a_n$ is below every $b_m$. Apply Theorem 2.4.2 to $(a_n)$ and show its limit lies in every interval.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above by $b_1$, so $a = \lim a_n$ exists by Theorem 2.4.2. Fix $n$. For every $k \ge n$ we have $a_k \le b_k \le b_n$, and $a_k \ge a_n$; letting $k \to \infty$ and using an-03's Theorem 2.3.4 gives $a_n \le a \le b_n$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since $n$ was arbitrary, $a \in I_n$ for every $n$, so $a \in \bigcap I_n$ and the intersection is nonempty.

The step worth writing out slowly is $a_k \le b_n$ for all $k$, including $k < n$. For $k \ge n$ it follows from nesting as above; for $k < n$ it follows because $a_k \le a_n \le b_n$. Without that, the passage to the limit only bounds $a$ by $b_n$ along a tail — which is in fact enough, since limits ignore finitely many terms, but you should say which of the two routes you are taking rather than leaving the gap silent.

Notice what has been shown at the level of the course: the Monotone Convergence Theorem was proved from the Axiom of Completeness in an-04, and here it proves the Nested Interval Property. Since the Nested Interval Property gives back the Axiom of Completeness (Problem 6), the three are equivalent.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the last arrow)

Use the Cauchy Criterion to prove the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem: every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.6(d), p. 62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You may not use Theorem 2.5.5, and you may not use anything whose proof used it — in particular not the proof of Theorem 2.6.4 given in the lesson. What you may use is the *statement* of the Cauchy Criterion. So build a subsequence and prove it is Cauchy.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Run the bisection from an-04's proof to construct the subsequence, but finish differently: instead of arguing that the interval lengths force convergence to a specific point, show that the constructed subsequence is Cauchy and let Theorem 2.6.4 supply the limit.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Let $|a_n| \le M$. Put $I_0 = [-M, M]$ and bisect repeatedly, at each stage keeping a half containing infinitely many terms of the sequence; write $I_k$ for the interval kept at stage $k$, so that $I_k$ has length $2M/2^k$. Choose $n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \cdots$ with $a_{n_k} \in I_k$, which is possible because each $I_k$ holds infinitely many terms and only finitely many indices have been used.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Now show $(a_{n_k})$ is Cauchy. Let $\epsilon > 0$ and choose $K$ with $2M/2^K < \epsilon$. If $j, k \ge K$ then $a_{n_j} \in I_j \subseteq I_K$ and $a_{n_k} \in I_k \subseteq I_K$, since the intervals are nested. Two points of an interval of length $2M/2^K$ differ by at most that length, so $|a_{n_j} - a_{n_k}| \le 2M/2^K < \epsilon$. Hence $(a_{n_k})$ is Cauchy, and Theorem 2.6.4 gives its convergence.

State clearly which fact you did *not* use, because the whole value of the exercise is in the bookkeeping: no supremum, no monotone sequence, and no appeal to Theorem 2.5.5. What the argument does use is the geometry of nested bisection, which is why this is a genuine derivation of Bolzano-Weierstrass rather than a restatement of it.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — completing the circle)

Assume the Nested Interval Property and use a bisection technique to prove the Axiom of Completeness: every nonempty set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a least upper bound.

*(Abbott §2.6, Exercise 2.6.6(a), p. 62)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You need to produce a number and then prove two things about it. Build intervals $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ in which every $b_n$ is an upper bound for $A$ and no $a_n$ is.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Start with $a_1$ not an upper bound and $b_1$ an upper bound; such a pair exists because $A$ is nonempty and bounded above. Bisect: if the midpoint is an upper bound take it as the new right endpoint, otherwise as the new left endpoint. The Nested Interval Property gives a point $s$ in every interval, and the lengths halve each time, so $s$ is the only such point.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Suppose $s$ is not an upper bound: some $x \in A$ has $x > s$. The interval lengths tend to zero, so for large $n$ we have $b_n - a_n < x - s$, and since $s \in I_n$ this forces $b_n < x$, contradicting that $b_n$ is an upper bound for $A$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Now suppose $s$ is not the *least* upper bound: some upper bound $t$ satisfies $t < s$. Again take $n$ with $b_n - a_n < s - t$; since $s \in I_n$ this gives $a_n > t$, and $t$ being an upper bound for $A$ then makes $a_n$ an upper bound too, contradicting the construction. Hence $s = \sup A$.

Three things are worth saying explicitly at the end. The construction maintains an invariant — right endpoint an upper bound, left endpoint not — and every step of the proof is a use of that invariant rather than of anything about $A$. The intervals must be *closed* for the Nested Interval Property to apply, and Abbott's Exercise 1.4.1 shows the result fails for open intervals. And with Problems 4 and 5 this closes the loop: AoC gives MCT gives NIP gives AoC, and BW and the Cauchy Criterion join the cycle from the other side, so all five statements are one statement.
</details>
