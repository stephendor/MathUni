## Problem 1 (easy–medium — simple groups)

(a) State Definition 12.25 (p. 275) and decide which of $C_6$, $C_7$, $S_3$, $\mathcal{A}_4$ are simple.

(b) Prove Example 12.26 (p. 275): a nontrivial abelian group is simple if and only if it is $C_p$ for a prime $p$.

(c) Prove Example 12.27 (p. 276): a group of order $p^r$ with $r>1$ is not simple.

(d) Why is "simple" the right word — what does a simple group fail to admit that a non-simple one does?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) In an abelian group every subgroup is normal.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Use the nontrivial centre from aa-28 Problem 6(a).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $C_7$ is simple: prime order, so its only subgroups are trivial (aa-25 Problem 2(b)). $C_6$ is not: $\langle g^2\rangle$ has order $3$ and is normal, being a subgroup of an abelian group. $S_3$ is not: $\mathcal{A}_3$ is normal of index $2$. $\mathcal{A}_4$ is not: $V$ is normal of order $4$ (aa-26 Problem 6(a)).

(b) If $|G|$ is prime then $G\cong C_p$ and has no proper nontrivial subgroups at all, so it is simple. Conversely let $G$ be abelian and simple. Take $g\ne e$; then $\langle g\rangle$ is a subgroup, normal because $G$ is abelian, and nontrivial, so it is $G$. So $G$ is cyclic. If $G\cong\mathbb{Z}$ then $2\mathbb{Z}$ is a proper nontrivial normal subgroup; if $G\cong C_n$ with $n$ composite, $n = ab$ with $1<a<n$ gives a subgroup of order $a$. So $n$ is prime.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $|G| = p^r$ with $r>1$. By aa-28 Problem 6(a), $Z(G)$ is nontrivial, and it is normal by aa-26 Problem 2(d). If $Z(G)\ne G$ we have a proper nontrivial normal subgroup and $G$ is not simple. If $Z(G) = G$ then $G$ is abelian, and by (b) an abelian simple group has prime order — but $|G| = p^r$ with $r>1$ is not prime. Either way $G$ is not simple.

(d) A non-simple group $G$ admits a nontrivial quotient $G/N$ that is smaller than $G$, so it can be studied in two pieces: the normal subgroup and the quotient. A simple group admits no such decomposition — every quotient is trivial or $G$ itself. So simple groups are the ones that cannot be broken up, which is why classifying them is the natural first step and why the classification of finite simple groups was worth decades of work.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — the normaliser)

(a) State Definition 12.24 (p. 274) and prove $N_G(H)$ is a subgroup containing $H$.

(b) Prove $H\trianglelefteq N_G(H)$, and that $N_G(H)$ is the largest subgroup of $G$ in which $H$ is normal.

(c) Prove that the number of conjugates of $H$ in $G$ is $[G:N_G(H)]$.

(d) Compute $N_{D_8}(\langle s\rangle)$ for a reflection $s$, and check (c) against the class computation of aa-24 Problem 6(d).

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $N_G(H)$ is the stabiliser of $H$ under the conjugation action of $G$ on its set of subgroups.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Orbit–stabiliser, Theorem 12.10 (p. 270).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $G$ acts on the set of its subgroups by $g\cdot H = gHg^{-1}$ — this is an action because conjugation is (aa-24 Problem 6(a)) and carries subgroups to subgroups (aa-23 Problem 3(b) applied to the automorphism $x\mapsto gxg^{-1}$). $N_G(H)$ is the stabiliser of $H$, hence a subgroup by aa-28 Problem 3(a). It contains $H$ because $hHh^{-1} = H$ for $h\in H$.

(b) $H\trianglelefteq N_G(H)$ is the definition of $N_G(H)$ read as a statement about that subgroup. And if $H\trianglelefteq K\le G$ then every $k\in K$ satisfies $kHk^{-1}=H$, so $K\subseteq N_G(H)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) The orbit of $H$ under the conjugation action is the set of conjugates of $H$, and the stabiliser is $N_G(H)$. Theorem 12.10 (p. 270) gives $|{\rm orbit}| = [G:N_G(H)]$.

(d) Take $D_8 = \langle y,x\rangle$ with $y$ of order $4$, $x$ a reflection, and $H = \langle x\rangle = \{e,x\}$. Then $y^2xy^{-2} = x$ since $y^2$ is central, so $y^2\in N_{D_8}(H)$; and $yxy^{-1} = xy^{-2} \ne x$, so $y\notin N_{D_8}(H)$. Hence $N_{D_8}(H) = \{e,x,y^2,xy^2\}$, of order $4$, and $[D_8:N_{D_8}(H)] = 2$. So $H$ has exactly two conjugates, $\{e,x\}$ and $\{e,xy^2\}$ — consistent with the class of $x$ having two elements, $\{x,y^2x\}$, in aa-24 Problem 6(d).
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — Sylow I and Cauchy)

(a) State Theorem 12.29 (p. 277) and Definition 12.31 (p. 277).

(b) Deduce Theorem 12.34 (p. 277), Cauchy's theorem, from Sylow's first theorem.

(c) Compare with aa-22 Theorem 10.22, the abelian case. What did that proof use that this one cannot?

(d) List the possible orders of Sylow subgroups of a group of order $360$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Sylow I with $k=1$ gives a subgroup of order $p$; a nonidentity element of it has order $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) $360 = 2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Sylow's first theorem: if $p^k$ divides $|G|$ then $G$ has a subgroup of order $p^k$. A $p$-Sylow subgroup is one of order $p^r$ where $p^r$ is the exact power of $p$ dividing $|G|$.

(b) Let $p$ divide $|G|$. By Sylow I with $k=1$ there is a subgroup $H$ of order $p$; any $h\ne e$ in $H$ generates it, so $|h| = p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) aa-22's proof of the abelian case used the **classification of finite abelian groups** — write $A$ as a sum of cyclic groups, find $p$ dividing one of the invariant factors, and take the obvious element. No such classification exists for general finite groups, which is exactly what makes the general Cauchy theorem harder and Sylow's machinery necessary. The abelian proof is not a special case of the general one; it is a different proof that happens to be available.

(d) $360 = 2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$. So a $2$-Sylow subgroup has order $8$, a $3$-Sylow has order $9$, and a $5$-Sylow has order $5$. Sylow I also guarantees subgroups of orders $2,4$ and $3$ — every prime power dividing $360$ — but those are not Sylow unless maximal at their prime.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — Sylow II and III)

(a) State Theorem 12.32 (p. 277) and Theorem 12.33 (p. 277).

(b) Deduce that a $p$-Sylow subgroup is normal if and only if it is the only one.

(c) Let $|G| = 15$. Show $n_3 = n_5 = 1$ and deduce that $G$ is abelian, hence cyclic.

(d) Let $|G| = 12$. What are the possibilities for $n_2$ and $n_3$, and which group realises each?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) Sylow II says all $p$-Sylows are conjugate.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) $n_3\equiv1\bmod3$ and $n_3\mid5$; $n_5\equiv1\bmod5$ and $n_5\mid3$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Sylow II: any two $p$-Sylow subgroups are conjugate, and every $p$-subgroup is contained in one. Sylow III: with $|G| = p^rm$ and $p\nmid m$, the number $n_p$ of $p$-Sylow subgroups satisfies $n_p\equiv1\bmod p$ and $n_p\mid m$.

(b) By Sylow II the $p$-Sylows form a single conjugacy class of subgroups. A subgroup is normal exactly when its conjugacy class is a single point, so $P\trianglelefteq G$ iff $n_p = 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $15 = 3\cdot5$. Then $n_3\mid5$ and $n_3\equiv1\bmod3$, leaving $n_3 = 1$ (since $5\equiv2$). And $n_5\mid3$ with $n_5\equiv1\bmod5$, leaving $n_5=1$. So the unique $P_3\cong C_3$ and $P_5\cong C_5$ are both normal. They meet trivially — orders $3$ and $5$ are coprime — so elements of one commute with elements of the other, and $|P_3P_5| = 15$, giving $G\cong C_3\times C_5\cong C_{15}$ by aa-21 Problem 5(a).

(d) $12 = 2^2\cdot3$. For $p=3$: $n_3\mid4$ and $n_3\equiv1\bmod3$, so $n_3\in\{1,4\}$. For $p=2$: $n_2\mid3$ and $n_2\equiv1\bmod2$, so $n_2\in\{1,3\}$.

$C_{12}$ has $n_2 = n_3 = 1$, both Sylows being normal in an abelian group. $\mathcal{A}_4$ has $n_3 = 4$ — the four subgroups of order $3$ counted in aa-28's subgroup list — and $n_2 = 1$, the unique one being $V$. $D_{12}$ has $n_3 = 1$ and $n_2 = 3$. So three of the four combinations occur among groups of order $12$; the fourth, $n_2 = 3$ and $n_3 = 4$, would give $3\cdot3 + 4\cdot2 + 1 = 18 > 12$ elements and is impossible.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — proving a group is not simple)

(a) Prove Corollary 12.36 (p. 278): if $|G| = p^rm$ with $r\ge1$, $m>1$, $p\nmid m$, and $n_p = 1$, then $G$ is not simple.

(b) Prove Proposition 12.38 (p. 278): if $|G| = p^rm$ with $r\ge1$ and $1<m\le p$, then $G$ is not simple.

(c) Use (b) to show no group of order $2p$ is simple for $p>2$ prime — Example 12.37 (p. 278).

(d) Show no group of order $12$, $28$ or $56$ is simple.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) $n_p\equiv1\bmod p$ and $n_p\mid m\le p$ leaves only $n_p=1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Compute the constraints on $n_p$ for the largest prime, and rule out the alternatives by counting elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $n_p=1$ the unique $p$-Sylow $P$ is normal by Problem 4(b). It is nontrivial since $r\ge1$, and proper since $m>1$. So $G$ has a proper nontrivial normal subgroup.

(b) $n_p\mid m$ and $m\le p$, so $n_p\le p$; and $n_p\equiv1\bmod p$ with $1\le n_p\le p$ forces $n_p = 1$. Then (a) applies.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $|G| = 2p$ with $p>2$: write it as $p^1\cdot2$ with $m = 2\le p$. By (b), $G$ is not simple. Example 12.28 (p. 276) reaches the same conclusion by hand, element by element; Example 12.37 (p. 278) is the remark that Sylow does it in a line.

(d) *Order $12 = 2^2\cdot3$:* $n_3\in\{1,4\}$ by Problem 4(d). If $n_3=1$, (a) applies. If $n_3=4$, the four subgroups of order $3$ meet pairwise trivially, contributing $4\cdot2 = 8$ elements of order $3$; the remaining $4$ elements must form the unique $2$-Sylow, so $n_2=1$ and (a) applies to $p=2$.

*Order $28 = 2^2\cdot7$:* $m = 4\le7$, so (b) applies directly.

*Order $56 = 2^3\cdot7$:* $n_7\mid8$ and $n_7\equiv1\bmod7$, so $n_7\in\{1,8\}$. If $n_7=1$, (a) applies. If $n_7=8$, the eight subgroups of order $7$ contribute $8\cdot6 = 48$ elements of order $7$; the remaining $56-48 = 8$ elements must form the unique $2$-Sylow, so $n_2=1$ and (a) applies. Either way $G$ is not simple — and the counting move is the same one both times.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — groups of order pq)

Let $p<q$ be primes and $|G| = pq$.

(a) Show $n_q = 1$, so the $q$-Sylow subgroup $Q$ is normal.

(b) Show $n_p\in\{1,q\}$, and that $n_p = 1$ whenever $q\not\equiv1\bmod p$.

(c) Prove Proposition 12.43 (p. 280): if $q\not\equiv1\bmod p$ then $G$ is cyclic.

(d) Show the hypothesis is needed: $6 = 2\cdot3$ has $3\equiv1\bmod2$, and there is a non-cyclic group of order $6$.

(e) Proposition 12.42 (p. 280) states that every noncommutative simple group has order at least $60$. Say which of your results above contribute to that, and what this set does not prove.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $n_q\mid p$ and $n_q\equiv1\bmod q$, with $p<q$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) With both Sylows normal and meeting trivially, $G$ is their direct product.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $n_q$ divides $p$ and is $\equiv1\bmod q$. So $n_q\in\{1,p\}$, and $p<q$ makes $p\not\equiv1\bmod q$ — since $1<p<q$ means $p-1$ is between $0$ and $q$ and nonzero. Hence $n_q=1$.

(b) $n_p$ divides $q$ and is $\equiv1\bmod p$, so $n_p\in\{1,q\}$. If $n_p = q$ then $q\equiv1\bmod p$. So $q\not\equiv1\bmod p$ forces $n_p=1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) By (a) and (b), both $P$ (order $p$) and $Q$ (order $q$) are normal. $P\cap Q$ is a subgroup whose order divides both $p$ and $q$, hence is $\{e\}$. For $a\in P$, $b\in Q$, the commutator $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ lies in $P$ (as $ba^{-1}b^{-1}\in P$ by normality) and in $Q$ (as $aba^{-1}\in Q$), hence is $e$: so $P$ and $Q$ commute elementwise. Then $PQ$ is a subgroup of order $pq = |G|$, so $G = PQ\cong P\times Q\cong C_p\times C_q$, and $\gcd(p,q)=1$ makes that $C_{pq}$ by aa-21 Problem 5(a).

(d) $p=2$, $q=3$, and $3 = 1 + 2\cdot1\equiv1\bmod2$, so the hypothesis fails. And indeed $D_6\cong S_3$ is a non-cyclic group of order $6$, by aa-25 Problem 6. So the congruence condition is not an artefact of the proof; it is exactly what excludes the non-abelian possibility, and the smallest case where it fails is the smallest non-abelian group.

(e) Problem 1(c) rules out orders $p^r$ with $r>1$; Problem 5(b) rules out $p^rm$ with $1<m\le p$; Problem 5(d)'s counting handles cases like $56$; and (c) here handles $pq$. Between them these dispose of most orders below $60$.

What this set does **not** prove is the full statement: orders such as $24$, $36$ and $48$ need arguments of their own — typically an action on the set of Sylow subgroups giving a homomorphism to a small symmetric group, whose kernel is a normal subgroup. Proposition 12.42 is quoted, not established here, and $60$ is sharp: $\mathcal{A}_5$ has order $60$ and is simple, which aa-30 proves.
</details>
