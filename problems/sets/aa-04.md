## Problem 1 (easy — invertible, or a zero-divisor)

Work in $\mathbb{Z}/60\mathbb{Z}$.

(a) Find the multiplicative inverse of $[17]_{60}$, using the Euclidean algorithm and back-substitution. Verify your answer.

(b) Show $[12]_{60}$ has no multiplicative inverse, quoting Proposition 2.16 (p. 34) for the criterion.

(c) Exhibit a nonzero class $[b]_{60}$ with $[12]_{60}\cdot[b]_{60} = [0]_{60}$, and explain why (b) and (c) are two faces of the same fact for this $n$.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Proposition 2.16 says $[\ell]_n$ is invertible exactly when $\gcd(n,\ell) = 1$. Compute the two gcds first, and only then start looking for an inverse.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $\gcd(60,17) = 1$, so Theorem 1.8 (p. 8) gives $m, n$ with $60m + 17n = 1$; then $[n]_{60}$ is the inverse. (c) Look for $b$ with $60 \mid 12b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$60 = 17\cdot 3 + 9$, $17 = 9 + 8$, $9 = 8 + 1$. Back: $1 = 9 - 8 = 9 - (17 - 9) = 2\cdot 9 - 17 = 2(60 - 3\cdot 17) - 17 = 2\cdot 60 - 7\cdot 17$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) So $-7\cdot 17 \equiv 1 \bmod 60$, i.e. $[17]^{-1} = [-7] = [53]$. Check: $17\cdot 53 = 901 = 15\cdot 60 + 1$. ✓

(b) $\gcd(60,12) = 12 \ne 1$, so Proposition 2.16 denies an inverse.

(c) $[12]\cdot[5] = [60] = [0]$ with $[5] \ne [0]$. The two are the same fact because $60$ is not prime: Theorem 2.15 (p. 32) says that for $n > 1$, "every nonzero class is invertible" and "there are no zero-divisors" are *both* equivalent to $n$ being prime — so once one fails for $n = 60$, so must the other. What (b) and (c) exhibit is one composite $n$ failing both, not two independent misfortunes.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — the definition that has to be checked)

(a) Prove Claim 2.12 (p. 28) for multiplication: if $[a]_n = [a']_n$ and $[b]_n = [b']_n$ then $[ab]_n = [a'b']_n$. Cite Lemma 2.9 (p. 26) or reprove it.

(b) Definition 2.10 (p. 27) defines the operations by picking representatives, which is exactly why (a) is needed. Show the need is real: partition $\mathbb{Z}$ into $A = \{x : x \ge 0\}$ and $B = \{x : x < 0\}$, and show that "$[x] + [y] := [x+y]$" does **not** define an operation on $\{A, B\}$.

(c) Aluffi's own Example 2.11 (p. 27) makes the same point with a different partition. State in one sentence what property of congruence mod $n$ the partition in (b) lacks.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), find two pairs of representatives, one from $A$ and one from $B$ each time, whose sums land in different classes.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Compute $a + b$ for a couple of choices with $a \in A$, $b \in B$, and compare which class the answer falls in.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $a' - a = nk$ and $b' - b = n\ell$, so $a'b' - ab = a'b' - a'b + a'b - ab = a'(b'-b) + b(a'-a) = n(a'\ell + bk)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Take $a = 5 \in A$, $b = -1 \in B$: $a + b = 4 \in A$. Now take $a = 1 \in A$, $b = -5 \in B$: $a + b = -4 \in B$. Both pairs represent the same pair of classes $(A, B)$, so the proposed rule assigns $A + B$ two different values and defines nothing.

(c) The partition into nonnegatives and negatives is not compatible with translation — the property isolated in aa-03 Problem 6 — and congruence mod $n$ is, because its classes are the *cosets* $a + n\mathbb{Z}$ of a set closed under addition and negation. Well-definedness is not a formality about notation; it is the statement that the partition and the operation were built from the same thing.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — three conditions, one prime)

Theorem 2.15 (p. 32) states that for $n > 1$ the following are equivalent: (i) $n$ is prime; (ii) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero-divisors; (iii) every nonzero class in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has a multiplicative inverse.

(a) Prove (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii).

(b) Prove (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i), by contraposition.

(c) Aluffi's proof of (i) $\Rightarrow$ (iii) is the only one of the three that reaches outside $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Say which two results from chapter 1 it uses and what each one contributes.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) If $ab = 0$ and $a \ne 0$, multiply by $a^{-1}$. (b) If $n$ is composite, write $n = de$ with $1 < d, e < n$ and look at $[d][e]$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (b) you must check that $[d]_n$ and $[e]_n$ are genuinely nonzero, which is where $1 < d, e < n$ is used rather than decorative.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $b = 1\cdot b = (a^{-1}a)b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0$. Only associativity and the ring properties of Theorem 2.13 (p. 30) are used.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Suppose $n$ is not prime. As $n > 1$, it is composite, so $n = de$ with $1 < d < n$ and $1 < e < n$. Then $[d]_n \ne [0]_n$ and $[e]_n \ne [0]_n$, because $0 < d, e < n$ and the classes $[0], \dots, [n-1]$ are distinct. But $[d][e] = [de] = [n] = [0]$, so $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has zero-divisors, contradicting (ii).

(c) It uses **Lemma 1.18** (p. 13) to turn "$p \nmid \ell$" into $\gcd(p,\ell) = 1$, and **Theorem 1.8** (p. 8) to turn that gcd into an equation $1 = qp + k\ell$, whose reduction mod $p$ exhibits $[k]_p$ as the inverse of $[\ell]_p$. The first supplies coprimality, the second converts coprimality into an actual inverse. Bézout is the entire content: it is what makes a *statement about divisors* into a *construction of an element*.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — Fermat, and the binomial coefficients that make it work)

Let $p$ be a positive prime.

(a) Prove that $p \mid \binom{p}{k}$ for every $0 < k < p$. (Use that $\binom{p}{k}k!(p-k)! = p!$ and that a prime dividing a product divides a factor.)

(b) Prove Theorem 2.18 (p. 35), $[a]_p^{\,p} = [a]_p$, for all $a \ge 0$ by induction on $a$.

(c) Extend to all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Treat $p = 2$ separately and say why it needs saying.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) The step is $(a+1)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}a^k$, and (a) kills every middle term mod $p$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $p \mid p!$, and $p \nmid k!(p-k)!$ because every factor of both factorials is a positive integer smaller than $p$. Then use that $p$ is prime, via Theorem 1.21 (p. 14) and Corollary 1.11 (p. 10).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $p$ divides $\binom{p}{k}k!(p-k)!$. If $p$ divided $k!(p-k)!$, then being prime it would divide one of the factors of that product of integers each $< p$ and $> 0$ — impossible. So $p \mid \binom{p}{k}$ by Corollary 1.11.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Base $a = 0$: $0^p = 0$. Step: modulo $p$,
$$(a+1)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}a^k \equiv a^p + 1 \equiv a + 1,$$
the middle terms vanishing by (a) and the last step by the inductive hypothesis.

(c) For $a < 0$ write $a = -b$ with $b > 0$. If $p$ is odd, $(-b)^p = -b^p \equiv -b = a$. If $p = 2$, $(-b)^2 = b^2 \equiv b$, and $b \equiv -b \bmod 2$, so the conclusion still holds — but by a different route, since $(-1)^p = -1$ is false at $p = 2$. It needs saying because "$p$ odd" is an assumption easy to make silently while writing $(-1)^p = -1$, and $2$ is a prime.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — Wilson, both ways)

Let $p > 1$ be an integer.

(a) Suppose $p$ is prime. Show that the only classes in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ equal to their own inverse are $[1]_p$ and $[-1]_p$ — and that for $p$ **odd** these are two distinct classes, while at $p = 2$ they are one and the same.

(b) Deduce **Wilson's theorem**: if $p$ is prime then $(p-1)! \equiv -1 \bmod p$. Handle $p = 2$ before the pairing argument, and say why it cannot be left to the end. Check the result at $p = 7$.

(c) Prove the converse: if $n > 1$ and $(n-1)! \equiv -1 \bmod n$, then $n$ is prime. Check that $n = 4$ does not satisfy the hypothesis.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $x = x^{-1}$ means $x^2 = 1$, i.e. $(x-1)(x+1) = 0$. Now use Theorem 2.15 (p. 32). (b) Pair every other class with its inverse.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) The product $[1][2]\cdots[p-1]$ collapses because all classes other than $\pm 1$ pair off into inverse pairs, each contributing $1$. (c) If $n$ is composite it has a divisor $d$ with $1 < d < n$; that $d$ appears as a factor of $(n-1)!$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) In $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with $p$ prime there are no zero-divisors (Theorem 2.15), so $(x-1)(x+1) = 0$ forces $x = 1$ or $x = -1$. Both do satisfy $x^2 = 1$. They are distinct exactly when $[1]_p \ne [-1]_p$, i.e. when $p \nmid 2$, i.e. when $p$ is odd.

(b) At $p = 7$: $6! = 720 = 102\cdot 7 + 6$, and $[6]_7 = [-1]_7$. ✓
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) Take $p = 2$ first, directly: $(2-1)! = 1$ and $1 \equiv -1 \bmod 2$. ✓

Now let $p$ be odd. Every class $[a]$ with $1 \le a \le p-1$ is invertible (Proposition 2.16, p. 34, since $\gcd(p,a) = 1$), and its inverse is again in that list. By (a) exactly two of them are their own inverse, $[1]$ and $[-1] = [p-1]$, and they are distinct because $p$ is odd. The remaining $p - 3$ classes split into $(p-3)/2$ pairs $\{x, x^{-1}\}$ with $x \ne x^{-1}$, each pair contributing $1$ to the product. So $(p-1)! \equiv 1\cdot(-1)\cdot 1^{(p-3)/2} = -1$.

$p = 2$ cannot be left to the end as a check on the answer, because the pairing argument is not merely uninformative there — it is false. At $p = 2$ the list $1, \dots, p-1$ has one entry, $[1]$ and $[-1]$ are the same class rather than two, and "the remaining $p-3$ classes" is a count of $-1$ things. An argument that produces the right number from a false premise has not proved anything, and verifying the conclusion afterwards does not repair it.

(c) Suppose $n$ is composite, say $n = de$ with $1 < d < n$. Then $d$ is one of the factors $1, 2, \dots, n-1$, so $d \mid (n-1)!$. The hypothesis says $n \mid (n-1)! + 1$, and $d \mid n$, so $d \mid (n-1)! + 1$ as well; subtracting, $d \mid 1$, contradicting $d > 1$. Hence $n$ is prime.

At $n = 4$: $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \bmod 4$, not $-1 \equiv 3$. So the composite $4$ does not sneak through — as the argument just showed it could not, its divisor $2$ appearing in $3!$.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — RSA has to decrypt every message, not just the coprime ones)

Let $p \ne q$ be positive primes, $n = pq$, $k = (p-1)(q-1)$, and let $e, d$ be **positive** integers with $\gcd(e,k) = 1$ and $de \equiv 1 \bmod k$. This is the setting of Theorem 2.19 (p. 38), with the exponents pinned down.

(a) Explain why such a $d$ exists, and why insisting it be positive costs nothing.

(b) Prove that $a^{de} \equiv a \bmod p$ for **every** integer $a$ — including those divisible by $p$.

(c) Do the same mod $q$, and deduce $a^{de} \equiv a \bmod n$ for every integer $a$. Say exactly where you use $p \ne q$.

(d) Many presentations prove only the case $\gcd(a, n) = 1$. Which step of your argument is the one they are skipping, and is the omitted case rare enough not to matter?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) is Proposition 2.16 (p. 34) applied in $\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}$. (b) splits into $p \mid a$ and $p \nmid a$; only the second needs Fermat.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Write $de = 1 + mk$; since $d, e \ge 1$ we have $de \ge 1$, so $m \ge 0$ and $de - 1 = m(p-1)(q-1)$ with a nonnegative $m$. For (c), you have $p \mid (a^{de} - a)$ and $q \mid (a^{de} - a)$; conclude with the aa-02 result that coprime divisors multiply.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\gcd(e, k) = 1$, so $[e]_k$ is invertible in $\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}$ by Proposition 2.16, and any representative of its inverse satisfies the congruence. A class mod $k$ has representatives of every size, so one of them is positive and nothing is lost by taking it — but something is lost by not saying so. With $d$ negative, $de$ can be negative, $a^{de}$ is not an integer power of $a$ at all, and the identity $de = 1 + mk$ below would have $m < 0$. Aluffi's statement leaves the sign open because his proof works in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $[a]_n^{\,e}$; the argument here is about integers and needs the exponents to be ones.

(b) If $p \mid a$ then $p \mid a^{de}$ too (as $de \ge 1$), so both sides are $\equiv 0$ and the congruence holds.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) If $p \nmid a$, then $\gcd(p,a) = 1$ by Lemma 1.18 (p. 13), so $[a]_p$ is invertible and Theorem 2.18 (p. 35) gives $a^{p} \equiv a$, hence $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$. Then
$$a^{de} = a^{1 + m(p-1)(q-1)} = a\cdot\left(a^{p-1}\right)^{m(q-1)} \equiv a\cdot 1 = a \pmod p.$$

(c) The same argument with $q$ in place of $p$ gives $a^{de} \equiv a \bmod q$. So $p$ and $q$ both divide $c = a^{de} - a$. Since $p \ne q$ are primes, $\gcd(p,q) = 1$, and aa-02 Problem 4 gives $pq \mid c$, i.e. $a^{de} \equiv a \bmod n$. The hypothesis $p \ne q$ is used exactly there: for $p = q$ the two divisibilities give only $p \mid c$, and $n = p^2 \nmid c$ in general.

(d) They skip the $p \mid a$ branch of (b) — the branch where Fermat is unavailable because $[a]_p$ is not invertible. It is not skippable on the grounds of rarity, even though the multiples of $p$ or $q$ among $0, \dots, n-1$ number only $p + q - 1$ out of $pq$: a scheme that decrypts all but a vanishing fraction of messages is still a scheme that fails, and an adversary who can choose the plaintext is not sampling uniformly. The right reason the case is harmless is that it is *true*, which is what (b) shows — not that it is unlikely.
</details>
