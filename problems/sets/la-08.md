## Problem 1 (easy — proof, invertibility of a composite)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $ST$ is invertible if and only if both $S$ and $T$ are invertible.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 9, p. 89)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction is the lesson's guided proof, essentially unchanged. For the other, translate "$ST$ is invertible" into injectivity and surjectivity, and ask what each of those forces about $S$ and about $T$ separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
($\Leftarrow$) Exhibit $T^{-1}S^{-1}$ as an inverse, as in the lesson. ($\Rightarrow$) $ST$ surjective forces $S$ surjective, since $\operatorname{range}(ST)\subseteq\operatorname{range}S$; and $ST$ injective forces $T$ injective, since $Tv=0$ gives $(ST)v=0$. Then Theorem 3.69 upgrades each one-sided condition to invertibility — and that upgrade is where finite-dimensionality is spent.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\operatorname{range}(ST)\subseteq\operatorname{range}S$ because every $(ST)v=S(Tv)$ is an output of $S$; so $\operatorname{range}(ST)=V$ forces $\operatorname{range}S=V$. If $Tv=0$ then $(ST)v=S0=0$, so injectivity of $ST$ gives $v=0$, i.e. $\operatorname{null}T=\{0\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Theorem 3.69 then makes $S$ invertible (from surjectivity) and $T$ invertible (from injectivity). Say explicitly where the hypothesis $\dim V<\infty$ is used: only in those two appeals, and without it the statement is false — on $\mathbf{F}^\infty$ let $T$ prepend a zero and $S$ be the backward shift, so $ST=I$ is invertible while neither factor is.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, coordinates are an isomorphism)

Suppose $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a basis of $V$. Prove that the map $T:V\to\mathbb{F}^{n,1}$ defined by
$$Tv = \mathcal{M}(v)$$
is an isomorphism of $V$ onto $\mathbb{F}^{n,1}$, where $\mathcal{M}(v)$ is the matrix of $v$ with respect to the basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 14, p. 89)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Three things need checking, and the first is the one most solutions skip: that $T$ is a *linear map* at all. Only then do injectivity and surjectivity make sense as tests.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Linearity: write $v$ and $w$ in the basis and use uniqueness of coefficients (la-04, Theorem 2.29) to see that the coefficients of $v+w$ are the sums of the coefficients. Injectivity: use Theorem 3.16 — what vector has the zero column as its coordinates? Surjectivity: given a column, build a vector whose coordinates it is. Then Theorem 3.56 assembles the three into invertibility.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $v=\sum a_jv_j$ and $w=\sum b_jv_j$ then $v+w=\sum(a_j+b_j)v_j$ and $\lambda v=\sum(\lambda a_j)v_j$, so $\mathcal{M}(v+w)=\mathcal{M}(v)+\mathcal{M}(w)$ and $\mathcal{M}(\lambda v)=\lambda\mathcal{M}(v)$ by the entrywise definitions of matrix addition and scaling. $\mathcal{M}(v)=0$ means every $a_j=0$, so $v=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
For surjectivity, the column with entries $c_1,\dots,c_n$ is $\mathcal{M}(c_1v_1+\dots+c_nv_n)$. Hence $T$ is injective and surjective, so invertible by Theorem 3.56, i.e. an isomorphism.

Two remarks worth including. Uniqueness of the coefficients is what makes $\mathcal{M}$ a *function*, before any question of linearity arises — the same point la-05 made about Theorem 3.5. And this result is the precise content of "a finite-dimensional space is just $\mathbb{F}^n$ after relabelling": the relabelling is $T$, and it depends on the basis, so there is no canonical version of it.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, one-sided inverses suffice)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $ST=I$ if and only if $TS=I$.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 10, p. 89)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
By symmetry it is enough to prove one implication and then swap the names. Assume $ST=I$; what does that immediately give you about $T$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
$ST=I$ makes $T$ injective, hence invertible by Theorem 3.69. Once $T^{-1}$ exists you can *identify* $S$: multiply $ST=I$ on the right by $T^{-1}$ and use associativity. Then $TS$ is a product you already know.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $Tv=0$ then $v=Iv=(ST)v=S(Tv)=S0=0$, so $\operatorname{null}T=\{0\}$ and $T$ is invertible by Theorem 3.69. Now $S = SI = S(TT^{-1}) = (ST)T^{-1} = IT^{-1} = T^{-1}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Hence $TS = TT^{-1} = I$. The reverse implication is the same argument with $S$ and $T$ exchanged, and it is legitimate to say so rather than write it twice — but say *why* it is legitimate, namely that the hypothesis and conclusion swap into each other under that exchange.

Note that the result says something genuinely strong: in finite dimensions a one-sided inverse is automatically two-sided, so Definition 3.53's second equation is redundant for operators. It is not redundant in general, and the lesson's two infinite-dimensional operators are the reason — with $T$ prepending a zero to a sequence and $S$ the backward shift, $ST=I$ on $\mathbf{F}^\infty$ while $TS\ne I$.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, every map on columns is a matrix)

Prove that every linear map from $\mathbb{F}^{n,1}$ to $\mathbb{F}^{m,1}$ is given by a matrix multiplication: if $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}^{n,1},\mathbb{F}^{m,1})$, then there exists an $m$-by-$n$ matrix $A$ with $Tx=Ax$ for every $x\in\mathbb{F}^{n,1}$.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 15, p. 89)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You are not being asked to invent $A$. Definition 3.32 already assigns a matrix to $T$ once bases are chosen; the work is to check that multiplying by it reproduces $T$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Take the standard bases of $\mathbb{F}^{n,1}$ and $\mathbb{F}^{m,1}$ — the columns with a single 1. Put $A=\mathcal{M}(T)$. Theorem 3.65 gives $\mathcal{M}(Tx)=\mathcal{M}(T)\mathcal{M}(x)$ for every $x$, so the claim reduces to identifying $\mathcal{M}(x)$ with $x$ itself, which is exactly what the standard basis of a space of columns makes true.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
With respect to the standard basis, the coordinates of a column are its own entries, so $\mathcal{M}(x)=x$ and $\mathcal{M}(Tx)=Tx$. Substituting into Theorem 3.65 gives $Tx=Ax$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
That completes the proof, and the sentence carrying the weight is "$\mathcal{M}(x)=x$ with respect to the standard basis" — it should be justified, not asserted, since it is the only step where the special choice of space and basis is used. It is worth noticing what the exercise is really saying: on spaces of columns the relabelling of Problem 2 is the identity, which is why matrices and linear maps feel like the same object there and only there.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — the non-invertible operators are not a subspace)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $\dim V>1$. Prove that the set of non-invertible operators on $V$ is **not** a subspace of $\mathcal{L}(V)$.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 2, p. 88)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A set fails to be a subspace by failing one of the three conditions of la-02's Theorem 1.34. The zero operator is non-invertible (since $\dim V>0$), so the identity condition holds — look at closure under addition instead.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Find two non-invertible operators whose sum is invertible. The cleanest choice is a pair that splits a basis between them: one that keeps some basis vectors and kills the rest, and the one that does the opposite. Their sum is then the identity. This is where $\dim V>1$ is needed — with $\dim V=1$ there is no way to split the basis into two nonempty parts.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be a basis, $n\ge2$. Define $S$ by $Sv_1=v_1$ and $Sv_j=0$ for $j\ge2$, and $T$ by $Tv_1=0$ and $Tv_j=v_j$ for $j\ge2$ — both well defined by la-05's Theorem 3.5.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Neither is invertible: $v_2\in\operatorname{null}S$ and $v_1\in\operatorname{null}T$, both nonzero, so neither is injective (Theorem 3.16), hence neither is invertible (Theorem 3.56). But $(S+T)v_j=v_j$ for every $j$, so $S+T=I$ on a basis and therefore on all of $V$ — and $I$ is invertible. So the set is not closed under addition and is not a subspace.

Two points for the write-up. State where $n\ge2$ is used — it is what makes both null spaces nontrivial, and with $n=1$ one of the two operators would be the identity. And note that $S+T=I$ needs the "agree on a basis implies equal" step, which is the uniqueness half of Theorem 3.5, not something to wave through.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — the operators that commute with everything)

Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $T$ is a scalar multiple of the identity **if and only if** $ST=TS$ for every $S\in\mathcal{L}(V)$.

*(Axler §3.D, Exercise 16, p. 89)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
One direction is a line. For the other, work vector by vector: first show that every nonzero $v$ satisfies $Tv=\lambda_v v$ for *some* scalar depending on $v$, and only then show the scalar cannot actually depend on $v$. Those are two separate arguments and both are needed.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For the first part, argue by contradiction: if $v$ and $Tv$ were linearly independent, extend them to a basis (la-04, Theorem 2.33) and use la-05's Theorem 3.5 to build an $S$ with $Sv=v$ and $S(Tv)=0$. Compute $STv$ and $TSv$ and find they differ. For the second part, compare $\lambda_v$ and $\lambda_w$ by applying $T$ to $v+w$, treating the independent and dependent cases separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
With such an $S$: $S(Tv)=0$ while $T(Sv)=Tv\ne0$ (as $v,Tv$ independent forces $Tv\ne0$), so $ST\ne TS$ — contradiction. Hence for each $v\ne0$ there is $\lambda_v$ with $Tv=\lambda_vv$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Now fix $v\ne0$ and let $w\ne0$. If $v,w$ are independent, apply $T$ to $v+w$: on one side $\lambda_{v+w}(v+w)$, on the other $\lambda_vv+\lambda_ww$, and independence forces $\lambda_v=\lambda_{v+w}=\lambda_w$. If instead $w=cv$ with $c\ne0$, then $Tw=cTv=c\lambda_vv=\lambda_vw$, so $\lambda_w=\lambda_v$. Either way the scalar is the same for all nonzero $v$; call it $\lambda$. Then $Tv=\lambda v$ for every $v$, including $v=0$, so $T=\lambda I$. The converse is immediate: $S(\lambda I)=\lambda S=(\lambda I)S$.

Three things earn the marks. The construction of $S$ must use both halves of la-05's Theorem 3.5 — existence to build it, and the freedom to prescribe values on a basis arbitrarily. The dependent case $w=cv$ genuinely needs separate treatment, because the independence argument does not apply to it. And it is worth remarking why the result matters: it says the centre of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is as small as it could be, which is the first sign that operators are a rich structure rather than a commutative one.
</details>
