## Problem 1 (easy — a persistence module is a functor) [interleaves cat-07]

(a) State Definition 12.1 and Definition 12.2.
(b) State Definition 12.4. Which category is the source, which the target, and
what does functoriality say in this case, spelled out in the language of
tda1-03's filtration?
(c) State Definition 12.5, and say which two conditions "finite type" imposes.
(d) State Definition 12.3, and explain in one sentence why natural
transformations are exactly the right notion of morphism between persistence
modules.

*(Dey §12.1, Definitions 12.1–12.5, pp. 367–369)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A poset is a category with one morphism $x \to y$ exactly when $x \leq y$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Functoriality on a poset category says the assignment respects composition of
the relations $x \leq y \leq z$ and sends each identity to an identity — which
is precisely "the diagram of vector spaces commutes".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
A $\mathbb{P}$-indexed persistence module is a functor
$M : \mathbb{P} \to \mathbf{Vec}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 12.1: a category $\mathcal{C}$ is a set of objects
$\operatorname{Obj}\mathcal{C}$ together with a set of morphisms
$\hom(x,y)$ for each pair, an identity $\mathbb{1}_x \in \hom(x,x)$ for every
object, and a composition sending $f \in \hom(x,y)$, $g \in \hom(y,z)$ to
$g \circ f \in \hom(x,z)$. Definition 12.2: a functor $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$
assigns objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms, respecting composition
and identities.
(b) Definition 12.4: for a poset category $\mathbb{P}$, a
$\mathbb{P}$-indexed persistence module is a functor $M : \mathbb{P} \to \mathbf{Vec}$;
$M$ is pointwise finite-dimensional when it takes values in $\mathbf{vec}$.
Source: the poset of parameter values, with a unique morphism $x \to y$ whenever
$x \leq y$. Target: vector spaces. Functoriality says $M$ assigns a vector space
to each parameter value and a linear map to each comparison, with the map for
$x \leq z$ equal to the composite through any intermediate $y$ — exactly the
statement that a filtration's induced maps compose correctly.
(c) Definition 12.5: $M$ has finite type if it is pointwise finite-dimensional
and all morphisms $M(x \leq y)$ are isomorphisms outside a finite subset of
$\mathbb{P}$. Two conditions: finite dimension at each point, and only finitely
many places where anything changes.
(d) Definition 12.3: a natural transformation $\eta : F \Rightarrow G$ is a
family $\{\eta_x : F(x) \to G(x)\}$ making the evident squares commute. They are
the right morphisms because a map of persistence modules must be a linear map at
each parameter value that is compatible with every structure map — and
commutativity of those squares is precisely naturality.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — the shift functor and interleaving)

(a) State Definition 12.6 and describe the shift functor $(\cdot)_{\to\delta}$
concretely: where does $M_{\to\delta}$ put the vector space that $M$ had at
$x$?
(b) State Definition 12.7 in full, naming both commutativity conditions.
(c) State Definition 12.8, and say what $\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) = \infty$ means.
(d) Take $d = 1$ and compare with tda1-06. Which parts of the definition change
when $d$ increases, and which do not?

*(Dey §12.2, Definitions 12.6–12.8, pp. 369–370)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\vec{\delta} = (\delta,\dots,\delta)$ — the shift is by the same amount in
every coordinate.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), read the definition and ask which symbols mention $d$ at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Triangular commutativity relates the two composites $\psi \circ \phi$ and
$\phi \circ \psi$ to the module's own structure maps; rectangular commutativity
says each of $\phi$ and $\psi$ is itself a map of modules up to the shift.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 12.6: for $\delta \in \mathbb{R}$, write
$\vec{\delta} = (\delta,\dots,\delta) = \delta \cdot \vec{e}$, and define the
shift functor $(\cdot)_{\to\delta}$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$-modules. $M_{\to\delta}$
places at $x$ the space $M$ had at $x - \vec{\delta}$ — the module translated
diagonally by $\delta$ in every coordinate at once.
(b) Definition 12.7: a $\delta$-interleaving between $d$-parameter modules $M$
and $N$ is a pair of families of linear maps
$\phi = \{\phi_x : M_x \to N_{x + \vec{\delta}}\}$ and
$\psi = \{\psi_x : N_x \to M_{x+\vec{\delta}}\}$ satisfying *triangular
commutativity* — the composites return the module's own structure map shifted by
$2\delta$ — and *rectangular commutativity*,
$\phi_y \circ \rho^M_{x \to y} = \rho^N_{x+\vec\delta \to y+\vec\delta} \circ \phi_x$
and likewise for $\psi$.
(c) Definition 12.8: $\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) = \inf_\delta\{M$ and $N$ are
$\delta$-interleaved$\}$. If they are not $\delta$-interleaved for any
$\delta \in \mathbb{R}^+$ we call them $\infty$-interleaved and set
$\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) = \infty$.
(d) Nothing in the definition changes. The shift $\vec{\delta}$ is a vector
rather than a scalar and the index poset is $\mathbb{R}^d$ rather than
$\mathbb{R}$, but the two commutativity conditions are word for word the same.
That is the sense in which the interleaving distance "extends verbatim":
unlike a bottleneck distance, it never mentions intervals, so it never needed
them to exist.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the price of that generality)

(a) State Theorem 12.1 exactly, including the constant.
(b) Explain what the theorem does and does not rule out. In particular, does it
say computing $\mathsf{d}_I$ exactly is NP-hard, or something stronger, or
something weaker?
(c) Contrast with tda1-06: for one-parameter modules, what makes the
interleaving distance computable, and which theorem supplies the bridge?
(d) Dey and Wang note that the hardness statement assumes the input modules are
represented by graded matrices as in Chapter 11, and that these coincide with
the modules of finite type. Why does the choice of representation need stating
at all in a complexity claim?

*(Dey §12.2, Theorem 12.1, p. 370)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The theorem is about computing an $r$ with
$\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) \leq r < 3\mathsf{d}_I(M,N)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
An approximation-hardness result is stronger than exact-computation hardness:
it says even a crude answer is hard.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
In one parameter the isometry theorem identifies $\mathsf{d}_I$ with the
bottleneck distance between barcodes, which is a matching problem.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Theorem 12.1: given two modules $M$ and $N$ given by graded matrix
representations, the problem of computing a real $r$ so that
$\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) \leq r < 3\,\mathsf{d}_I(M,N)$ is NP-hard.
(b) It rules out a polynomial-time algorithm (unless P = NP) even for a
3-approximation, which is strictly stronger than saying exact computation is
hard — exact computation is the special case $r = \mathsf{d}_I$. It does not
rule out approximations with a factor of 3 or worse, nor algorithms that are
fast on restricted classes of input, which is exactly the escape route §12.4
takes with interval decomposable modules.
(c) In one parameter the module decomposes into intervals, and the interleaving
distance coincides with the bottleneck distance between the resulting barcodes —
a matching problem solvable in polynomial time. The bridge is the isometry
between the two distances, and it exists only because the interval
decomposition does.
(d) Complexity is a property of a problem together with an encoding of its
input. A module presented as a graded matrix, as a filtration, or as an abstract
functor may have wildly different input sizes, so a hardness claim is empty
until the encoding is pinned down. Naming graded matrices also ties the claim to
the objects Chapter 11 actually computes with.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — matching distance)

(a) Write down the restriction $M|_\ell$ of a two-parameter module to a line
$\ell$ of positive slope, using Dey's parameterisation
$\lambda(x) = (1/(1+s^2))(x, sx+t)$, and say what kind of object it is.
(b) Write down the weight $w(\ell)$ in both cases, $s \geq 1$ and $0 < s < 1$,
and state Definition 12.9.
(c) Dey and Wang say the weight "is introduced to make the matching distance
stable with respect to the interleaving distance". State exactly what §12.3
does and does not display about the relationship between $\mathsf{d}_m$ and
$\mathsf{d}_I$.
(d) Why is restricting to lines a reasonable idea at all? Say what is gained
computationally, and identify precisely what information about a two-parameter
module a family of one-dimensional restrictions could in principle miss.

*(Dey §12.3, the weight and Definition 12.9, pp. 370–371)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$M|_\ell$ is indexed by a single real parameter.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (c), quote what is written and resist supplying the inequality you expect;
the section states stability without displaying a direction.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\mathsf{d}_m(M,N) = \sup_{\ell \in \Lambda}\{w(\ell)\cdot\mathsf{d}_I(M|_\ell, N|_\ell)\}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For a line $\ell : y = sx + t$ with $s > 0$, the restriction is
$M|_\ell(x) = M(\lambda(x))$ with the linear maps induced from $M$. It is a
one-parameter persistence module — which is the entire point, since one-parameter
modules have barcodes.
(b) $w(\ell) = 1/\sqrt{1+s^2}$ for $s \geq 1$ and $w(\ell) = 1/\sqrt{1 + 1/s^2}$
for $0 < s < 1$. Definition 12.9:
$\mathsf{d}_m(M,N) = \sup_{\ell\in\Lambda}\{w(\ell)\cdot\mathsf{d}_I(M|_\ell, N|_\ell)\}$,
the supremum over all lines of positive slope.
(c) §12.3 states that the weight is introduced to make the matching distance
stable with respect to the interleaving distance, and that a polynomial-time
algorithm for computing $\mathsf{d}_m$ for two-parameter modules follows. It
does not display an inequality between $\mathsf{d}_m$ and $\mathsf{d}_I$ in
either direction. So the correct report is: stability is asserted, the precise
comparison is not given in this section.
(d) Each restriction is one-parameter, hence has a barcode and a computable
interleaving distance; taking a supremum over a suitably chosen finite family
of lines then makes the whole quantity computable in polynomial time, which
Theorem 12.1 says $\mathsf{d}_I$ itself is not. What restrictions can miss is
anything that is invisible along every single line — the module's behaviour is
probed only on one-dimensional slices, and two non-isomorphic modules can agree
on all of them, which is the multiparameter analogue of the incompleteness
already met in tda2-03.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — when the bottleneck distance comes back)

(a) State Definition 12.12 and Definition 12.13. What does it mean for $M$ and
$N$ to be $\delta$-matched, and how is $\mathsf{d}_b$ obtained from that?
(b) State Fact 12.2 and explain which of the two distances is the coarser
measurement, giving the direction of the inequality and its intuitive reading.
(c) State Definition 12.14, Definition 12.15 and Definition 12.16, and explain
what makes the interval decomposable class the natural place for a bottleneck
distance to live. Then state Definition 12.17 and say how a rectangle is a
special case of an interval.
(d) State Proposition 12.7. Combining it with (b), explain the overall strategy
of §12.4: what has to be true of the input for $\mathsf{d}_b$ to be a useful
proxy for $\mathsf{d}_I$, and what a practitioner must therefore verify before
reporting a bottleneck distance between two-parameter modules.

*(Dey §12.4, Definitions 12.12–12.17, Fact 12.2 and Proposition 12.7, pp. 374–379)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A matching in Definition 12.12 is a *partial* bijection — not every summand need
be matched.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (d), note the two distances agree in the one-parameter case, so the
question is exactly how much of that agreement survives.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Fact 12.2: $\mathsf{d}_I \leq \mathsf{d}_b$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Definition 12.12: a matching $\mu : A \to B$ between multisets is a partial
bijection $\mu : A' \to B'$ with $A' \subseteq A$, $B' \subseteq B$; we write
$\operatorname{im}\mu = B'$ and $\operatorname{coim}\mu = A'$.
Definition 12.13: with $M \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^m M_i$ and
$N \cong \bigoplus_{j=1}^n N_j$ decomposed into indecomposables and
$I = \{1,\dots,m\}$, $J = \{1,\dots,n\}$, $M$ and $N$ are $\delta$-matched if
there is a matching $\mu : I \to J$ under which matched summands are
$\delta$-interleaved and unmatched summands are themselves $\delta$-interleaved
with the zero module. The bottleneck distance is the infimum of such $\delta$.
(b) Fact 12.2: $\mathsf{d}_I \leq \mathsf{d}_b$. The bottleneck distance is the
coarser — larger — measurement, because it insists that the interleaving be
realised summand by summand under a single matching, whereas an interleaving of
the whole modules is free to mix summands. Any matching therefore exhibits an
interleaving, but not conversely.
(c) Definition 12.14: an interval is a non-empty $I \subset \bar{\mathbb{R}}^d$
that is convex in the order sense — $p, q \in I$ and $p \leq r \leq q$ imply
$r \in I$ — and connected in the order sense, any two points being joined by a
finite chain within $I$ whose consecutive terms are comparable.
Definition 12.15: an interval module is one with $M_x = \mathbf{k}$ for
$x \in I_M$ and $\mathbf{0}$ otherwise, the structure maps being identities
within $I_M$. Definition 12.16: a module is interval decomposable if it
decomposes into interval modules. This is the natural home for a bottleneck
distance because a matching needs summands drawn from a class one can name and
compare — exactly what tda2-03 said is unavailable in general.
Definition 12.17: a $k$-rectangle is a product
$[a_1,b_1]\times\cdots\times[a_d,b_d]$ in which exactly $k$ of the factors are
non-degenerate; a $0$-rectangle is a point. A rectangle is an interval of a
particularly simple shape — order-convex and order-connected automatically —
so rectangle-decomposable modules are a subclass of interval decomposable ones.
(d) Proposition 12.7: $\mathsf{d}_I(M,N) = \mathsf{d}_I(\overline{M},\overline{N})$.
The strategy of §12.4 is to trade generality for computability: on the interval
decomposable class the summands are nameable, a matching makes sense, and
polynomial-time computation of the bottleneck distance becomes possible, with
Fact 12.2 relating the result back to the interleaving distance from above. A
practitioner reporting a bottleneck distance between two-parameter modules must
therefore verify that both modules are interval decomposable — which is a
property of the modules, not of the algorithm — and must remember that
$\mathsf{d}_b$ bounds $\mathsf{d}_I$ from above rather than equalling it, so a
small $\mathsf{d}_b$ is evidence of closeness while a large one is not by
itself evidence of difference.
</details>
