# at1-09 — Exact sequences: Mayer–Vietoris and coefficients

**Module:** Algebraic Topology I · **Unit:** at1-09
**Sources:** Hatcher, *Algebraic Topology* — §2.2 "Computing Homology"
(pp. 149–155): Mayer–Vietoris sequences, their derivation from the short exact
sequence $0 \to C_n(A \cap B) \to C_n(A) \oplus C_n(B) \to C_n(A+B) \to 0$, the
explicit boundary map, the reduced version and the van Kampen analogy
(pp. 149–150); the variant for deformation retracts and CW subcomplexes, and
Examples 2.46 and 2.47 (pp. 150–151); homology with coefficients in an abelian
group $G$, the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ simplification, and the extension of cellular
homology to coefficients (p. 153); Lemma 2.49 and Example 2.50 on
$\mathbb{R}P^n$ over a field (p. 154); Example 2.51, a map trivial on
$\mathbb{Z}$-homology and non-trivial on $\mathbb{Z}_m$-homology (p. 155).

As with at1-08, this unit reaches back into §2.1 for machinery its own
`resources` line does not name: the long exact sequence of a short exact
sequence of chain complexes, Proposition 2.21 on small simplices, and the
five-lemma. They are cited where used and recorded in the lesson footer.

Interleaves at1-08, whose cellular chain complexes are what the coefficient
groups get substituted into, and at1-07, whose $\mathbb{R}P^2$ computation is
the case $n = 2$ of Problem 5.

## Problem 1 (easy — the sequence and where it comes from)

(a) State the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, being precise about the hypothesis on
$A$ and $B$. Hatcher does not say "$X = A \cup B$"; he says $X$ is the union of
the *interiors* of $A$ and $B$. Give an example of subspaces with
$X = A \cup B$ for which the sequence fails, and say which step of the
derivation breaks.
(b) Write down the short exact sequence of chain complexes
$0 \to C_n(A \cap B) \xrightarrow{\varphi} C_n(A) \oplus C_n(B)
\xrightarrow{\psi} C_n(A+B) \to 0$ with $\varphi(x) = (x, -x)$ and
$\psi(x,y) = x+y$, and verify exactness at all three places, following Hatcher.
(c) Why is the third term $C_n(A+B)$ rather than $C_n(X)$? Name the result from
§2.1 that repairs the difference, and say what would go wrong without it.
(d) Describe the boundary map $\partial : H_n(X) \to H_{n-1}(A \cap B)$
explicitly: represent a class by a cycle $z$, split it as $x + y$, and check
that $\partial x = -\partial y$. Explain why $x$ and $y$ need not be cycles
individually, and why that does not matter.
(e) Why does the sign in $\varphi(x) = (x, -x)$ have to be there? Show that
$\varphi(x) = (x, x)$ would not give an exact sequence.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(a) Try $X = S^1$ with $A$ and $B$ the closed upper and lower semicircles, then
try to make them cover $X$ by their interiors.
(c) Barycentric subdivision is the mechanism; the statement you want is about
chains that are small with respect to a cover.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $\operatorname{Ker}\varphi = 0$: a chain in $A \cap B$ that vanishes as a
chain in $A$ is the zero chain. $\operatorname{Im}\varphi \subset
\operatorname{Ker}\psi$ because $\psi\varphi = 0$. Conversely if $x + y = 0$
then $x = -y$, so $x$ is a chain lying in both $A$ and $B$, hence in
$A \cap B$, and $(x,y) = (x,-x)$. Exactness at $C_n(A+B)$ is the definition of
$C_n(A+B)$. ⟨your step: check that $\partial$ carries $C_n(A+B)$ into
$C_{n-1}(A+B)$, which is what makes these chain complexes at all.⟩
(c) $C_n(A+B)$ is the subgroup of $C_n(X)$ of chains that are sums of chains in
$A$ and chains in $B$ — in general a proper subgroup. Proposition 2.21 (§2.1)
says the inclusion induces isomorphisms on homology, so the long exact sequence
of the short exact sequence above has $H_n(X)$ in the third slot after all.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Everything in Problem 1 is bookkeeping with one genuine idea in it: the
sequence is not proved directly, it is *manufactured* from a short exact
sequence of chain complexes by a purely algebraic machine you already have. Get
clear on which parts are topology (only (a) and (c)) and which are algebra
(everything else).
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — first computations)

(a) Example 2.46: take $X = S^n$ with $A$ and $B$ the two hemispheres, so
$A \cap B \simeq S^{n-1}$. Write out the reduced Mayer–Vietoris sequence, note
that the middle terms vanish, and deduce
$\tilde H_i(S^n) \cong \tilde H_{i-1}(S^{n-1})$. Run the induction from
$\tilde H_*(S^0)$ and recover the homology of $S^n$.
(b) Where in (a) did you use *reduced* homology? Redo the $n = 1$, $i = 1$ step
with unreduced groups and say what goes wrong.
(c) Hatcher notes that the $H_1$ terms of the reduced sequence give
$H_1(X) \cong (H_1(A) \oplus H_1(B))/\operatorname{Im}\Phi$ when $A \cap B$ is
path-connected, and calls this the abelianised van Kampen theorem. Compare it
term by term with Theorem 1.20 from at1-05. Which of van Kampen's hypotheses
has no counterpart here, and which piece of Hatcher's remark is doing the work
that "free product" did there?
(d) The comparison in (c) quietly uses that $H_1$ is the abelianisation of
$\pi_1$. Say where Hatcher proves that, and state honestly whether this unit
has established it.
(e) Use Mayer–Vietoris to compute $H_*(S^1 \vee S^1)$, choosing $A$ and $B$ so
that $A \cap B$ is contractible. Check your answer against at1-05's $\pi_1$.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) $\tilde H_0$ of a point is 0; $H_0$ of a point is $\mathbb{Z}$. That
difference is exactly what makes the induction close.
(e) Take $A$ and $B$ to be open neighbourhoods of the two circles, each
deformation retracting onto one of them.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) With $A$ and $B$ contractible, the reduced sequence has
$\tilde H_i(A) \oplus \tilde H_i(B) = 0$ in every degree, so the connecting map
$\tilde H_i(S^n) \to \tilde H_{i-1}(S^{n-1})$ is squeezed between two zeros and
is therefore an isomorphism. Starting from $\tilde H_0(S^0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$
and $\tilde H_i(S^0) = 0$ otherwise, induction gives
$\tilde H_i(S^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for $i = n$ and $0$ otherwise.
(c) Van Kampen needs the triple intersections path-connected to control the
kernel; Mayer–Vietoris has no triple-intersection hypothesis, because
abelianising has already collapsed the difficulty. ⟨your step: say what $\Phi$
is here, and why quotienting by its image is the abelian shadow of the normal
subgroup $N$.⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — the Klein bottle)

(a) Decompose the Klein bottle $K$ as a union of two Möbius bands $A$ and $B$
glued along their boundary circles. Justify the decomposition: say why the two
bands are subcomplexes with the required neighbourhood property, and identify
$A \cap B$ up to homotopy.
(b) Show that $A$, $B$ and $A \cap B$ are each homotopy equivalent to a circle,
and write down the interesting segment of the reduced Mayer–Vietoris sequence.
(c) Show that $\Phi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ is
$1 \mapsto (2, -2)$. The content is that the boundary circle of a Möbius band
wraps twice around its core circle; prove that, do not assert it.
(d) Deduce $H_2(K) = 0$ and $H_1(K) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$,
choosing $(1,0)$ and $(1,-1)$ as a basis for $\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ and
saying why that is a legitimate change of basis. Confirm the higher groups
vanish.
(e) at1-08 computed $H_*(N_g)$ from a cellular chain complex, and $K = N_2$.
Check the two answers agree, and then say which of the two computations you
would rather do for $N_5$, and why.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(c) Draw the band as a square with one pair of opposite sides identified with a
flip; follow the boundary edge around and count how many times it traverses the
core.
(d) A change of basis of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is legitimate exactly when the matrix
has determinant $\pm 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) A Möbius band deformation retracts onto its core circle; the intersection
is the common boundary circle. So the segment reads
$0 \to H_2(K) \to H_1(A \cap B) \xrightarrow{\Phi} H_1(A) \oplus H_1(B) \to
H_1(K) \to \tilde H_0(A \cap B) = 0$, that is,
$0 \to H_2(K) \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\Phi} \mathbb{Z}^2 \to H_1(K) \to 0$.
(d) $\Phi$ is injective since $(2,-2) \neq 0$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free,
so $H_2(K) = 0$ by exactness. ⟨your step: compute the cokernel of $\Phi$ in the
new basis and read off $H_1(K)$.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
This is the model Mayer–Vietoris computation and it is worth doing slowly.
Notice that all the topology is in one number — the 2 in $(2,-2)$ — and that
that number is a degree, computed exactly as in at1-08. Mayer–Vietoris does not
replace the degree calculations of the previous unit; it reorganises what you
do with them.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — coefficients)

(a) Define $C_n(X; G)$ for an abelian group $G$ and check that Hatcher's
boundary formula still gives $\partial^2 = 0$. Note carefully what is *not*
assumed about $G$: it is an abelian group, not a ring and not a field.
(b) Hatcher says the case $G = \mathbb{Z}_2$ is "particularly simple", for two
reasons. State both — one about what a chain *is*, one about signs — and say
what he means by the conclusion that $\mathbb{Z}_2$ homology "is often the most
natural tool in the absence of orientability".
(c) Compute $H_n(\mathrm{pt}; G)$ and deduce $\tilde H_n(S^k; G)$. Say which
step of the $\mathbb{Z}$-coefficient argument you had to change. (The answer
should be: none.)
(d) State Lemma 2.49 and give Hatcher's proof. Then say precisely why it is
needed: which claim about cellular homology with coefficients would be
unjustified without it?
(e) Hatcher writes that all of §2.1 "carries over directly to general
coefficient groups $G$ with no change in the proofs", and that the same is true
of Mayer–Vietoris. Say what that means for the following question: *does
choosing a coefficient group change which exact sequences are available to
you?*

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(d) The cellular boundary maps are supposed to have the *same* integer
coefficients $d_{\alpha\beta}$ as over $\mathbb{Z}$. Lemma 2.49 is what
licenses that.
(e) Read Hatcher's sentence twice. The answer is short and it matters for
Problem 5(e).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) A chain with coefficients in $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is a sum of singular simplices
with coefficients 0 or 1, so discarding the zero terms makes a chain a finite
"union" of simplices. And since $-1 = 1$, the boundary formula has no signs.
Signs are the algebraic record of orientation, so with no signs there is no
orientation to keep track of.
(e) No. The machinery of §2.1 and the Mayer–Vietoris sequence exist over every
abelian group $G$, with the same proofs. ⟨your step: so what *does* the choice
of $G$ change? Answer from Example 2.50, not in general terms.⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — what the coefficient choice buys and costs)

(a) Example 2.50. Write down the cellular chain complex of $\mathbb{R}P^n$ with
coefficients in a field $F$, using at1-08's boundary maps and Lemma 2.49.
(b) Suppose $\operatorname{char} F = 2$. Show $H_k(\mathbb{R}P^n; F) \cong F$
for all $0 \leq k \leq n$. Hatcher calls this "a more uniform answer than with
$\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients"; say exactly what has become uniform.
(c) Suppose $\operatorname{char} F \neq 2$. Show that the maps
$F \xrightarrow{2} F$ are isomorphisms and deduce that
$H_k(\mathbb{R}P^n; F)$ is $F$ for $k = 0$ and for $k = n$ odd, and zero
otherwise. Tabulate all three answers — $\mathbb{Z}$, characteristic 2,
characteristic $\neq 2$ — for $n = 4$, and check the $n = 2$ column against
at1-07.
(d) Example 2.51. Let $X = M(\mathbb{Z}_m, n)$ and $f : X \to X/S^n = S^{n+1}$
the quotient map. Show $f_*$ is trivial on reduced homology with $\mathbb{Z}$
coefficients, then use the long exact sequence of the pair $(X, S^n)$ with
$\mathbb{Z}_m$ coefficients to show $f_*$ is injective and non-zero. Conclude
that $f$ is not homotopic to a constant map — a conclusion $\mathbb{Z}$
coefficients could not reach.
(e) Now the reflection, and be careful, because there are two different things
here and the temptation is to merge them. Using (b), (c) and Problem 4(e),
answer three questions separately. First: does the choice of coefficient group
change which theorems and exact sequences are available? Second: does it change
the answers those theorems produce? Third: is there a *best* coefficient
choice, or only choices with different costs? Support the third answer by
naming, from your table in (c), one thing visible over $\mathbb{Z}$ that is
invisible over a field of characteristic 2, and one thing visible over
characteristic 2 that is invisible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) In characteristic 2 the map $F \xrightarrow{2} F$ is the zero map, so every
boundary map in the complex vanishes.
(e) For the third question, compare the $\mathbb{Z}$ column with the
characteristic-2 column in degree 4, and the characteristic-2 column with the
$\mathbb{Q}$ column in degree 1.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By at1-08, $d_k$ is multiplication by $1 + (-1)^k$, and Lemma 2.49 says
those same integers are the coefficients over $G$. So the complex is
$\cdots \xrightarrow{0} F \xrightarrow{2} F \xrightarrow{0} F
\xrightarrow{2} F \xrightarrow{0} F \to 0$.
(b) In characteristic 2 every map in the complex is zero, so the homology is
the chain complex itself: $F$ in every degree $0$ through $n$. What has become
uniform is the dependence on $k$ and on the parity of $n$ — over $\mathbb{Z}$
the answer splits into four cases, and here there are none. ⟨your step: say
what price was paid for that uniformity, in one sentence, before doing (c).⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) For $n = 4$ the three columns are:

| $k$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ | $\operatorname{char} 2$ | $\mathbb{Q}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $\mathbb{Z}$ | $F$ | $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| 1 | $\mathbb{Z}_2$ | $F$ | 0 |
| 2 | 0 | $F$ | 0 |
| 3 | $\mathbb{Z}_2$ | $F$ | 0 |
| 4 | 0 | $F$ | 0 |

Degree 4 is the one to stare at: over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is 0, and over a field of
characteristic 2 it is $F$. A non-zero group has appeared in a degree where the
integral homology vanishes entirely. ⟨your step: explain where that class comes
from — which boundary map stopped being injective, and why — and then answer
the three questions of (e) in order, without letting the answer to one stand in
for the answer to another.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(e) is the point of the unit and the place where the mission strip needs
care. Two claims that sound alike are being kept apart: *the exact sequences do
not depend on $G$* (Problem 4(e), and Hatcher says so in one sentence) and *the
computed groups depend on $G$ very much* (Example 2.50). A coefficient choice is
a choice about what you are willing not to see. Characteristic 2 buys
uniformity and orientation-freedom; it pays by making torsion and free rank
indistinguishable, and by manufacturing a class in degree $n$ that is not there
over $\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
