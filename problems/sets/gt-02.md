## Problem 1 (easy — proof, uniqueness)

Prove that the identity element $e$ in a group $G$ is unique, and that the inverse of a given element $g\in G$ is also unique.

*(Aluffi, Exercise 11.1, p. 265 — the two theorems fully worked/faded in the gt-02 lesson; here to be reproduced independently as a complete proof)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For uniqueness proofs in algebra, the standard trick is: assume two objects both satisfy the defining property, then show they must be equal by combining them cleverly.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For the identity: suppose $e_1, e_2$ both satisfy axiom (ii). Compute $e_1\cdot e_2$ two ways. For inverses of a fixed $g$: suppose $h_1, h_2$ both satisfy axiom (iii) for $g$. Compute $h_1\cdot g\cdot h_2$ two ways (the "sandwich" trick).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Identity: $e_1\cdot e_2 = e_2$ (using $e_1$ as identity) and $e_1\cdot e_2=e_1$ (using $e_2$ as identity); the left-hand sides match, so $e_1=e_2$. Inverses: $(h_1\cdot g)\cdot h_2 = e\cdot h_2=h_2$, and $h_1\cdot(g\cdot h_2)=h_1\cdot e=h_1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By associativity (axiom (i)), $(h_1\cdot g)\cdot h_2 = h_1\cdot(g\cdot h_2)$, so the two computed values $h_2$ and $h_1$ must be equal: $h_1=h_2$. Combine both halves (identity uniqueness, inverse uniqueness) into one clean write-up, citing axioms (i)–(iii) explicitly at each step.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — proof, homomorphism basics)

Let $\varphi: G\to H$ be a group homomorphism. Prove that $\varphi(e_G)=e_H$, and that for all $g\in G$, $\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g)^{-1}$.

*(Aluffi, Exercise 11.2, p. 265)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Start from $e_G\cdot e_G=e_G$ (the identity is its own... what?) and apply $\varphi$ to both sides.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Use $\varphi(e_G\cdot e_G)=\varphi(e_G)\cdot\varphi(e_G)$ (homomorphism property) combined with $e_G\cdot e_G=e_G$, then cancel. For the inverse part, apply $\varphi$ to $g\cdot g^{-1}=e_G$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\varphi(e_G) = \varphi(e_G\cdot e_G)=\varphi(e_G)\cdot\varphi(e_G)$. Let $x=\varphi(e_G)$; then $x=x\cdot x$, so (cancelling $x$ on one side, using the group $H$'s own axioms) $x=e_H$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$\varphi(e_G)=e_H$, proved by cancellation in $H$ (multiply both sides of $x=x\cdot x$ by $x^{-1}$ in $H$). For inverses: $\varphi(g)\cdot\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g\cdot g^{-1})=\varphi(e_G)=e_H$, so $\varphi(g^{-1})$ satisfies the defining property of $\varphi(g)^{-1}$ in $H$; by uniqueness of inverses (Problem 1), $\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g)^{-1}$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof) [interleaves gt-01]

Using only axioms (i)–(iii) of Definition 11.1 and the two uniqueness results (identity, inverse), show that if $g\cdot g=g$ for some element $g$ of a group $G$, then $g$ must be the identity $e$.

*(Aluffi §11.1, cf. Exercise 11.1's context, p. 230, cited directly as the gt-02 lesson's "you try"; interleaves gt-01's informally-discovered "do-nothing" idea, now proved formally)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
You want to isolate $g$ from $g\cdot g=g$. What element, combined with $g$ on the correct side, would simplify $g\cdot g$ directly to $e$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Combine both sides of $g\cdot g=g$ with $g^{-1}$ (which exists by axiom (iii)), and simplify using associativity (axiom (i)) and the identity axiom (ii).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Multiply both sides on the right by $g^{-1}$: $(g\cdot g)\cdot g^{-1} = g\cdot g^{-1}$. By associativity, the left side is $g\cdot(g\cdot g^{-1}) = g\cdot e_G = g$. The right side is $e_G$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So $g=e_G$. This confirms gt-01's informal "do-nothing" action is exactly the unique element satisfying $g\cdot g=g$ — the formal fingerprint of gt-01's discovered identity, now derived purely from the four axioms rather than by inspecting a rectangle.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — proof, structural classification)

Prove that a group of order 4 is necessarily commutative (abelian). (Hint: show there exist two non-identity elements $g\ne h$ with $gh\ne e$; then consider what $hg$ can be.)

*(Aluffi, Exercise 11.10, p. 265)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
A group of order 4 has exactly one identity and three non-identity elements. Think about what the "Latin square" property of Cayley tables (every row/column contains each element exactly once) forces.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Case on whether some non-identity element has order 4 (cyclic case — automatically abelian) or every non-identity element has order 2 (like the Klein four-group in gt-02's own worked comparison). Handle both cases.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If some element $g$ has order 4, then $G=\{e,g,g^2,g^3\}$ is cyclic, generated by a single element, and powers of a single element always commute with each other: $g^i\cdot g^j = g^{i+j}=g^j\cdot g^i$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
If instead every non-identity element has order 2 (so $x^2=e$ for all $x\in G$, i.e. every element is its own inverse), take any two distinct non-identity elements $g,h$. Consider $(gh)^2=e$ (since $gh\in G$ also has order dividing 2), i.e. $ghgh=e$. Multiply on the left by $g^{-1}=g$ and on the right by $h^{-1}=h$: $hg = g(ghgh)h = g\cdot e\cdot h$... work through carefully to isolate $hg=gh$. Both cases (cyclic order 4, or Klein-four-type) give a commutative group, exhausting all groups of order 4. **Any logically valid alternative case analysis earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — proof problem, structure theorem)

Let $G$ be a finite group, and assume every element of $G$ is its own inverse (i.e. $g^2=e$ for all $g\in G$). Prove that $G$ is abelian.

*(Aluffi, Exercise 11.19, p. 266 — a sharper, general version of the "every element order 2" case used in Problem 4)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Consider the product $gh$ for arbitrary $g,h\in G$. What is $(gh)^2$, given the hypothesis? What does that tell you about $(gh)^{-1}$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Since every element (including $gh$) squares to $e$, $(gh)^{-1}=gh$. But you also know $(gh)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}$ in general (a standard group fact — prove or cite this). Combine both expressions for $(gh)^{-1}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(gh)^{-1}=gh$ (given hypothesis applied to $gh$). Also, $(gh)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}$ in any group (check: $(gh)(h^{-1}g^{-1}) = g(hh^{-1})g^{-1}=g\cdot e\cdot g^{-1}=e$, confirming $h^{-1}g^{-1}$ is indeed the inverse of $gh$).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since every element is its own inverse, $g^{-1}=g$ and $h^{-1}=h$, so $h^{-1}g^{-1}=hg$. Combining: $gh = (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} = hg$. Therefore $gh=hg$ for all $g,h\in G$: $G$ is abelian. Note this is strictly stronger than Problem 4 (which only needed order exactly 4) — it holds for **any** finite group with this property, of any size. **Any logically valid alternative proof earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — composed, order of an element)

In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (integers mod 6, under addition), compute the order $|g|$ of every element $g\in\{[0],[1],[2],[3],[4],[5]\}$, and verify Lagrange-flavoured intuition informally: check that every order you find divides 6. (Formal Lagrange's theorem is deferred to gt-06 — here, just compute and observe the pattern.)

*(composed — extends Definition 10.12 and the gt-02 lesson's worked $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ order computations to a fresh modulus, previewing gt-06's theorem without proving it)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The order of $[a]$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},+)$ is the smallest positive $k$ such that $k\cdot[a]=[0]$ (repeated addition of $[a]$ to itself).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Compute the cyclic subgroup $\langle[a]\rangle$ generated by each element (all its multiples mod 6) and count its size, exactly as the gt-02 lesson did for $[1]_4$ and $[2]_4$ in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$|[0]|=1$ (trivial). $|[1]|$: $[1],[2],[3],[4],[5],[0]$ — all six elements before returning to $[0]$, so $|[1]|=6$. $|[2]|$: $[2],[4],[0]$ — returns after 3 steps, so $|[2]|=3$. $|[3]|$: $[3],[0]$ — returns after 2 steps, so $|[3]|=2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$|[4]|$: $[4],[2],[0]$ — order 3 (same cyclic subgroup as $[2]$, generated in the opposite direction). $|[5]|$: $[5],[4],[3],[2],[1],[0]$ — order 6 (like $[1]$). Full table: $|[0]|=1, |[1]|=6, |[2]|=3, |[3]|=2, |[4]|=3, |[5]|=6$. Every one of $\{1,6,3,2,3,6\}$ divides 6 — consistent with (though not yet a proof of) Lagrange's theorem, which gt-06 will establish rigorously: the order of any element must divide the order of the group.
</details>
