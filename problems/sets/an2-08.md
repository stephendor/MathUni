**This unit pays the module's largest debt.** Five earlier `an2` units cite
**Theorem 4.6.4** — *X compact and Y complete imply* $(C(X,Y),\rho)$ *is a
complete metric space* — as their supplier of completeness, each recording it as
"an2-08's page, printed 102". Open that page and you find the theorem
**stated with no proof**: Lindström writes that it "sums up the results above".
The summary is sound and Problem 3 assembles it. But no lesson may write
"Lindström proves Theorem 4.6.4", and Problem 3(e) is where you find out who does.

**Two books, two questions about the same object.** Lindström asks how to make
$C(X,Y)$ a *metric* space and get completeness. Munkres asks which parts of that
survive when the metric is thrown away — and the answer, **Corollary 46.9**, is
that the topology does not depend on the metric of $Y$ at all. That is
an2-02's distinction, now one level up: on a space of functions as on a space of
points, completeness is a property of the metric and the topology is coarser data.

**One function does duty twice.** an2-05 used $g_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$ to show
pointwise convergence does not preserve continuity. Munkres's Example 1 at
p. 282 is the same sequence, saying that $\mathcal{C}(I,\mathbf{R})$ is **not
closed** in $\mathbf{R}^I$ in the topology of pointwise convergence — while
Lindström's **Proposition 4.6.1** says $C_b(X,Y)$ **is** closed in $B(X,Y)$ under
$\rho$. Same subset, two topologies, opposite answers, one witness. Problem 2.

<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Theorem 46.11 makes the two descriptions **equivalent** exactly when $X$ is
locally compact Hausdorff. The free direction is *joint continuity implies a
continuous path of maps*: from $f : X \times I \to Y$ continuous, $F$ is
continuous with no hypothesis on $X$. The direction needing local compactness is
the converse — a continuous $F : I \to \mathcal{C}(X,Y)$ need not come from a
jointly continuous $f$ in general. So **joint continuity is the stronger
description**, which is exactly why top-04 insisted on it: a homotopy defined as
a continuous path of maps is a weaker object unless $X$ is well behaved, and the
definition that always works is the one on the product.

(e) The statement is in the **uniform** topology: $\|f-g\|_\infty$ is
$\rho(f,g)$, and by Theorem 46.7 that is the finest of the three. It presupposes
that the supremum is finite — guaranteed by compactness of the domain
(Proposition 4.6.3) and not otherwise. In the topology of compact convergence the
bound would become a family of bounds, one per compact $C \subset X$, and would
say nothing about behaviour at infinity; for compact $X$ the two topologies
coincide and nothing is lost, which is the case the corpus is always in.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) Theorem 4.6.4 requires **$X$ compact**. Lindström's warning: the supremum
"can be infinite when $f$ and $g$ are just assumed to be continuous. As a metric
is not allowed to take infinite values, this creates problems for the theory."
Exercise 1: $f(x) = x$ and $g(x) = 0$ on $\mathbf{R}$ give
$\sup|f - g| = \infty$.

Two repairs. **Restrict the space** to $C_b(X,Y)$ — costs you the functions that
are unbounded, which is most of them on a non-compact domain. **Truncate the
metric on $Y$**, $\bar d = \min(d,1)$, which is Exercise 4 to §4.6 and gives a
complete metric $\bar\rho$ on all of $C(X,Y)$ — costs you the numerical value of
the distance, since every separation beyond 1 now reads as 1, so no estimate of
the form $\|f-g\|_\infty \le \epsilon$ with $\epsilon > 1$ survives. Munkres uses
the second repair as his default and records the relation
$\tilde\rho = \min(\rho,1)$ on $\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ at p. 268.
</details>
