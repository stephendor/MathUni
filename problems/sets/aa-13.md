## Problem 1 (easy–medium — degree, and when it behaves)

(a) Let $R$ be an integral domain and $f, g \in R[x]$ nonzero. Prove $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$, with $\deg$ as in Definition 7.1 (p. 131).

(b) Show this fails over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, with an explicit pair.

(c) Prove that if $R$ is a domain then $R[x]$ is a domain, and that its units are exactly the units of $R$.

(d) Show (c) fails over $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$: find a unit of $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})[x]$ of positive degree.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The coefficient of $x^{\deg f + \deg g}$ in $fg$ is the product of the two leading coefficients.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(d) Try $1 + 2x$ and square it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) Write $f = a_dx^d + \cdots$ and $g = b_ex^e+\cdots$ with $a_d, b_e \ne 0$. Every term of $fg$ has degree at most $d+e$, and the coefficient of $x^{d+e}$ is exactly $a_db_e$, which is nonzero because $R$ is a domain. So $\deg(fg) = d+e$.

(b) Over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: $([2]x)([3]x) = [6]x^2 = 0$. Two polynomials of degree $1$ whose product is the zero polynomial, which has no degree at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) (b)'s computation is the general obstruction: if $R$ is a domain then by (a) a product of nonzero polynomials has a degree and so is nonzero, making $R[x]$ a domain. For the units, if $fg = 1$ then $\deg f + \deg g = \deg 1 = 0$, so both degrees are $0$ and $f, g$ are constants — units of $R$. Conversely a unit of $R$ is a unit of $R[x]$.

(d) $(1+2x)^2 = 1 + 4x + 4x^2 = 1$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})[x]$, since $4 = 0$ there. So $1+2x$ is its own inverse and is a unit of degree $1$. The nilpotent $2$ is what makes this possible, and it is the same defect as (b): a coefficient ring with zero-divisors gives $R[x]$ elements whose degree does not control them.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — long division, and everything it buys)

(a) Prove Proposition 7.3 (p. 132): if $f, g\in R[x]$ with $f\ne0$ and the **leading coefficient of $f$ a unit**, then $g = qf + r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg f$.

(b) Where is the hypothesis on the leading coefficient used? Show it cannot be dropped: divide $x$ by $2x$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

(c) Deduce Theorem 7.2 (p. 132): $k[x]$ is a Euclidean domain for $k$ a field.

(d) Deduce Corollary 7.7 (p. 134) and Corollary 7.8 (p. 134) in a line each.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Induct on $\deg g$. If $\deg g \ge \deg f$, subtract a suitable multiple of $f$ to kill the leading term of $g$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) The valuation is $v = \deg$; every nonzero element of a field is a unit, so (a) applies to every nonzero divisor.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) If $g = 0$ or $\deg g<\deg f$, take $q=0$, $r=g$. Otherwise write $f = a_dx^d+\cdots$, $g = b_ex^e+\cdots$ with $e\ge d$; since $a_d$ is a unit, $g - b_ea_d^{-1}x^{e-d}f$ has degree $<e$, and the inductive hypothesis applies to it.

(b) The inverse $a_d^{-1}$ is the only place. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, dividing $x$ by $2x$ would need $x = q(x)\cdot 2x + r$ with $r$ constant; comparing coefficients of $x$ gives $1 = 2q_0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, impossible. So long division is not available in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ — which is exactly why $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a Euclidean domain, and consistent with its not being a PID (aa-11 Problem 6(c)).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Take $v(f) = \deg f$, a function $k[x]\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\ge0}$. For $g$ and $f\ne0$, the leading coefficient of $f$ is a nonzero element of a field, hence a unit, so (a) gives $g = qf+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg f$. That is Definition 6.24 (pp. 112–113) exactly.

(d) $k[x]$ is a PID because Euclidean domains are PIDs — Theorem 6.26 (p. 113). And $k[x]$ is a UFD because PIDs are UFDs — Theorem 6.35 (p. 117). Neither needs a new idea: chapter 6 was built so that these would be corollaries, and the only work specific to polynomials was (a).
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — roots and factors)

(a) Prove the **remainder theorem** of Example 7.5 (p. 133): dividing $g(x)$ by $x-a$ leaves remainder $g(a)$.

(b) Deduce the **factor theorem**: $a$ is a root of $g$ in the sense of Definition 7.6 (p. 134) if and only if $(x-a)\mid g(x)$.

(c) Prove that a nonzero $g\in R[x]$ of degree $d$ over an integral domain $R$ has at most $d$ roots in $R$.

(d) Show (c) fails over $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$: find a degree-2 polynomial with four roots. Which hypothesis fails?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Write $g(x) = q(x)(x-a) + r$ with $r$ constant, and evaluate at $a$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Induct on $d$, peeling off one factor $(x-a)$ at a time and using that $R$ is a domain to know the remaining roots are roots of the cofactor.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The leading coefficient of $x-a$ is $1$, a unit in any ring, so Proposition 7.3 applies and gives $g(x) = q(x)(x-a)+r(x)$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<1$ — either way $r$ is a constant. Evaluating at $a$: $g(a) = q(a)\cdot 0 + r = r$.

(b) $(x-a)\mid g$ means the remainder is $0$, which by (a) means $g(a)=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Induct on $d$. A nonzero constant has no roots. For $d\ge1$: if $g$ has no root we are done; otherwise let $a$ be a root, so $g = (x-a)h$ with $\deg h = d-1$ by Problem 1(a). If $b\ne a$ is any root of $g$ then $0 = g(b) = (b-a)h(b)$, and $b-a\ne0$, so $h(b)=0$ because $R$ is a domain. By induction $h$ has at most $d-1$ roots, so $g$ has at most $d$.

(d) Over $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ take $g(x) = x^2-1$. Then $1^2=1$, $3^2=9=1$, $5^2=25=1$ and $7^2=49=1$, so all four odd classes are roots of a degree-2 polynomial.

The hypothesis that fails is that $R$ is a domain, and it fails at precisely the step marked above: $g(b) = (b-a)h(b) = 0$ no longer forces $h(b)=0$. With $a=1$, $g = (x-1)(x+1)$, and at $b=3$ both factors $2$ and $4$ are nonzero while their product is $8 = 0$. Every counterexample to (c) is a zero-divisor sighting.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the quotient as a vector space)

Let $k$ be a field and $f\in k[x]$ nonzero of degree $d$.

(a) Prove Corollary 7.10 (p. 135): every coset of $(f)$ in $k[x]$ has exactly one representative that is $0$ or of degree $<d$.

(b) Deduce Theorem 7.11 (p. 135): $k[x]/(f)$ is a $d$-dimensional vector space over $k$, with basis the classes of $1, x, \dots, x^{d-1}$.

(c) Count the elements of $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})[x]/(x^3+1)$, and of $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]/(x^4+x+1)$.

(d) Proposition 7.9 (p. 134) states that $q$ and $r$ are *unique*. Which half of (a) is that, and what would go wrong without it?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) Existence is Proposition 7.3; uniqueness is the difference of two such representatives.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) By (b) a coset is a tuple of $d$ coefficients from $k$, so count tuples.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) *Existence:* given $g$, divide by $f$ to get $g = qf + r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<d$; then $g + (f) = r + (f)$. *Uniqueness:* if $r$ and $r'$ both qualify and $r + (f) = r'+(f)$, then $f \mid (r-r')$ while $r-r'$ is $0$ or of degree $<d$; a nonzero multiple of $f$ has degree at least $d$ by Problem 1(a), so $r-r'=0$.

(b) The map sending $(c_0,\dots,c_{d-1})$ to the class of $c_0+c_1x+\cdots+c_{d-1}x^{d-1}$ is onto by existence in (a) and one-to-one by uniqueness, and it is $k$-linear because the operations on cosets are defined on representatives.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})[x]/(x^3+1)$: $d = 3$ and $|k| = 5$, so $5^3 = 125$ elements — Example 7.12 (p. 136). $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]/(x^4+x+1)$: $d=4$ and $|k|=2$, so $2^4 = 16$ elements.

(d) Proposition 7.9 is the uniqueness half. Without it, existence alone would give each coset *at least* one low-degree representative and possibly several, so the map in (b) would be onto and not injective — $k[x]/(f)$ would be a quotient of $k^d$ rather than a copy of it, and the count in (c) would be an upper bound instead of an answer. Note where uniqueness came from in (a): from degree behaving multiplicatively, which is Problem 1(a), which needs $k$ to be a domain. The three parts of this problem are three consequences of the same fact.
</details>

## Problem 5 (medium–hard — building fields by decree)

Let $k$ be a field and $f\in k[x]$ nonzero.

(a) Prove that $k[x]/(f)$ is a field if and only if $f$ is irreducible.

(b) Take $k = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $f = x^2+x+1$. Show $f$ is irreducible, write out the addition and multiplication tables of $k[x]/(f)$, and confirm it is a field with four elements.

(c) Show $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})[x]/(x^3+1)$ is **not** a field, by exhibiting a zero-divisor.

(d) Recover aa-09 Problem 5(d) — $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$ — as an instance of (a), and say what (a) adds to the earlier argument.

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $k[x]$ is a PID by Corollary 7.7 (p. 134), so use Proposition 6.33 (p. 117) and Definition 6.2 (p. 106).
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) A degree-2 polynomial over a field is irreducible exactly when it has no root (Proposition 7.15, p. 137); check $f(0)$ and $f(1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $k[x]$ is a PID. In a PID, $q$ is irreducible if and only if $(q)$ is a maximal ideal — Proposition 6.33 (p. 117) for one direction and Lemma 6.22 (p. 112) with every ideal principal for the other. And $(f)$ is maximal exactly when $k[x]/(f)$ is a field, by Definition 6.2 (p. 106). Chaining the two gives the claim.

(b) $f(0) = 1$ and $f(1) = 1+1+1 = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, so $f$ has no root; a factorisation of a degree-2 polynomial would produce one. So $f$ is irreducible and the quotient is a field. By Theorem 7.11 it has $2^2 = 4$ elements: $0, 1, \bar x, \bar x + 1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b, cont.) In the quotient $\bar x^2 = \bar x + 1$, since $x^2+x+1 = 0$ means $x^2 = -x-1 = x+1$ in characteristic $2$. Then $\bar x^3 = \bar x(\bar x+1) = \bar x^2+\bar x = 1$, so $\bar x$ has multiplicative order $3$ and the three nonzero elements are $1, \bar x, \bar x^2$ — a cyclic group of order $3$, as it must be. Addition is componentwise mod $2$ on the pair $(c_0,c_1)$.

(c) $x^3+1 = (x+1)(x^2-x+1)$ over $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ — indeed over any ring, since $(-1)^3+1 = 0$ makes $-1$ a root and the factor theorem applies. So $f$ is reducible, and the classes of $x+1$ and $x^2-x+1$ are nonzero (each has degree $<3$, so it is its own representative by Corollary 7.10) with product $0$. A zero-divisor, so not a field.

(d) $x^2+1$ has no real root, so it is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, so $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$ is a field by (a). What (a) adds is that the *field-ness* now comes for free from irreducibility, whereas aa-09 obtained it by recognising the answer as $\mathbb{C}$ and using that $\mathbb{C}$ is a field. That is a real gain: over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ in (b) there is no ring waiting to be recognised, and the construction produces a field nobody handed us.
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — interpolation is the Chinese Remainder Theorem)

Let $k$ be a field and $a_1,\dots,a_n\in k$ **distinct**.

(a) Show the ideals $(x-a_i)$ of $k[x]$ are pairwise comaximal: $(x-a_i) + (x-a_j) = (1)$ for $i\ne j$.

(b) Show $(x-a_1)\cdots(x-a_n)$ generates the product of those ideals, and deduce from Theorem 5.52 (p. 97) that
$$k[x]\big/\big((x-a_1)\cdots(x-a_n)\big)\ \cong\ k^n .$$

(c) Identify the isomorphism concretely, and deduce **Lagrange interpolation**: for any $b_1,\dots,b_n\in k$ there is exactly one polynomial of degree $<n$ with $g(a_i)=b_i$ for every $i$.

(d) Check the count in (c) against Theorem 7.11 (p. 135), and say which of "exactly one" is existence and which is uniqueness in the language of (b).

(e) Where is the hypothesis that the $a_i$ are distinct used, and what is the statement if two of them coincide?

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) The difference $(x-a_i)-(x-a_j) = a_j-a_i$ is a nonzero constant, hence a unit.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) Each factor $k[x]/(x-a_i)$ is $k$ via evaluation at $a_i$ — aa-10 Problem 2(c).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) The ideal $(x-a_i)+(x-a_j)$ contains $a_j - a_i$, which is a nonzero element of $k$ and therefore a unit. An ideal containing a unit is everything, by aa-09 Problem 3(a). So the sum is $(1)$.

(b) In a commutative ring $(a)(b) = (ab)$ — aa-10 Problem 4(c) for $\mathbb{Z}$, and the same computation in general. Iterating, the product of the $n$ ideals is generated by the product of the generators. Applying Theorem 5.52 repeatedly to the pairwise comaximal ideals gives
$$k[x]/\big(\textstyle\prod_i(x-a_i)\big)\ \cong\ \prod_i k[x]/(x-a_i).$$
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(c) Evaluation at $a_i$ is a surjective homomorphism $k[x]\to k$ with kernel $(x-a_i)$, so $k[x]/(x-a_i)\cong k$ — aa-10 Problem 2(c). Composing, the isomorphism of (b) is
$$g(x) + \big(\textstyle\prod_i(x-a_i)\big)\ \longmapsto\ \big(g(a_1),\dots,g(a_n)\big).$$
Given $b_1,\dots,b_n$, surjectivity produces a coset with those values and injectivity says there is only one such coset; and by Corollary 7.10 (p. 135) each coset has exactly one representative of degree $<n$, since $\prod_i(x-a_i)$ has degree $n$. That representative is the interpolating polynomial.

(d) The vector space $k[x]/(\prod(x-a_i))$ has dimension $n$ over $k$ by Theorem 7.11, and $k^n$ has dimension $n$; the isomorphism of (b) is $k$-linear, so the dimensions had to agree, and this is a check on (b) rather than a new fact. In the language of (b), **existence of an interpolating polynomial is surjectivity of the Chinese Remainder map, and uniqueness is injectivity** — the same pairing as in aa-10 Problem 5(d), where a congruence system had exactly one solution mod $105$.

(e) Distinctness is used exactly once, in (a), to make $a_j-a_i$ a unit. If two of the $a_i$ coincide the ideals are equal, the sum is not $(1)$, and the conclusion is false in both directions at once: prescribing $g(a_1)=b_1$ and $g(a_1)=b_1'$ with $b_1\ne b_1'$ has no solution, and if $b_1 = b_1'$ the solution is no longer unique among polynomials of degree $<n$, because the degree bound has been inflated by a repeated factor. The right statement for repeated points involves derivatives and is Hermite interpolation, which is a different theorem.
</details>
