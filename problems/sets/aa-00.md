## Problem 1 (easy — checking the rules informally)

Place a penny and a nickel side by side on a table. Consider just one action: swapping the places of the two coins. Is this a group? Check each of Carter's four rules (1.5–1.8) explicitly and explain your conclusion.

*(Carter §1.5, Exercise 1.1, p. 7)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
List the total number of distinct arrangements reachable from the start — is it finite, and does the list of rules (fixed list, reversibility, determinism, combinations-are-actions) all check out?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Work through Rules 1.5–1.8 one at a time against this exact scenario, the way aa-00's lesson did for the rectangle.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Rule 1.5 (fixed list): yes, just one action — swap. Rule 1.6 (reversible): swapping twice returns you to the start, so swap undoes itself. Rule 1.7 (deterministic): yes, swap always does the same thing. Rule 1.8 (combinations are actions): swap-then-swap is the do-nothing action, itself a legitimate action.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
All four rules hold, so this is a group — in fact the smallest interesting one, isomorphic to the rectangle's "one flip" sub-behaviour: two configurations (swapped, not-swapped), do-nothing as identity, swap as its own inverse. State explicitly that this is a group of order 2.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — checking the rules, three actions)

Three walls in your bedroom hold pieces of art, one per wall (a fourth wall has a window, unusable). Two allowed actions: swap the left and centre pictures, or swap the centre and right pictures. (a) List all arrangements reachable from the start. (b) Check Rules 1.5–1.8. (c) Does "swap left/centre" undo itself? Does "swap left/centre, then swap centre/right" undo itself, or does undoing it require a different combination?

*(Carter §1.5, Exercise 1.4, p. 7 — cited directly in the aa-00 lesson's "you try")*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Label the starting arrangement $(1,2,3)$ for (left, centre, right). Apply "swap L/C", then "swap C/R", tracking where each numbered picture ends up.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Systematically apply every combination of the two generators until no new arrangement appears — this mirrors the rectangle's "explore until closed" method from the lesson.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Starting from $(1,2,3)$: swap L/C gives $(2,1,3)$; swap C/R gives $(1,3,2)$; combining reaches further arrangements such as $(2,3,1)$ and $(3,1,2)$. Continue until the process closes.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
The full reachable set has 6 arrangements (all permutations of 3 objects) — this group is $S_3$, previewed here informally before gt-11 studies symmetric groups properly. Each single swap undoes itself (Rule 1.6, swap-then-swap-same-pair returns to start). But "swap L/C then swap C/R" does **not** undo itself: applying it twice gives a 3-cycle, not the identity — its undo is the *other* order, "swap C/R then swap L/C". Verify this explicitly by tracking labels through both compositions.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof-style reasoning about the rules) [interleaves aa-00]

Does Rule 1.8 (any sequence of consecutive actions is also an action) imply that every group must contain infinitely many actions? Explain your reasoning carefully, referencing the finite rectangle group from Segment 1 of the aa-00 lesson as a check on your answer.

*(Carter §1.5, Exercise 1.5, p. 7; interleaves aa-00's own Segment 1 rectangle-exploration content with Segment 2's rule-checking, i.e. an earlier idea within this same unit, since aa-00 is a root unit)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
The rectangle group has exactly four configurations. Does Rule 1.8 force new configurations to keep appearing forever, or can the combinations "loop back" onto ones already found?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Rule 1.8 only says combinations *count as* actions — it doesn't say each new combination must be a *previously unseen* action. Use the rectangle's closure at four configurations as a concrete counterexample to the "must be infinite" claim.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
In the rectangle group: flip-horizontal-then-flip-horizontal is a combination (covered by Rule 1.8), but it equals the do-nothing action, already on the list. No genuinely new action appears past the fourth configuration.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
No — Rule 1.8 does not imply infinitude. It guarantees that *every* combination is *some* action already recognised as legitimate, but combinations are free to coincide with actions already found (as flip-then-flip-same-direction always does). The rectangle group is a concrete finite counterexample: exactly four actions, closed under all combinations, satisfying Rules 1.5–1.8 completely. State this as a direct disproof of the implication, citing the finite example as sufficient.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — comparing structures)

For each of Exercises 1.1 through 1.4 (the coin swap, the coin-plus-dime scenario, the marble-pocket scenario, and the three-wall art scenario) that actually described a group, determine exactly how many actions there are in that group — counting all combinations obtainable via Rule 1.8, not just the generators.

*(Carter §1.5, Exercise 1.6, p. 8)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
First decide which of the four scenarios fail to be groups at all (check Rule 1.6, reversibility, especially) before counting actions for the ones that qualify.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The "coin-plus-dime" scenario (swap penny/nickel, dime never touched) and the "marbles between pockets" scenario are worth checking against Rule 1.6 particularly carefully — does every action have a genuine undo within the fixed list plus combinations?
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Coin swap alone: 2 actions (do-nothing, swap) — a group. Coin-plus-dime: still just 2 actions, since the dime is inert; still a group. Marbles: moving a marble left-to-right is not undone by moving a *different* marble right-to-left in general — check whether this fails Rule 1.6 or whether it's still fine because the marbles are indistinguishable.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Coin swap and coin-plus-dime: 2 actions each, both groups of order 2. Marbles (5 marbles, left pocket to right and back as the two actions): since marbles are indistinguishable, "moving one left-to-right" is genuinely undone by "moving one right-to-left" — this is a group, but bounded by how many marbles are in each pocket (6 configurations: 0 through 5 marbles in the right pocket), forming a group only if you also allow it to model correctly — discuss carefully whether the action set stays fixed regardless of the current configuration (Rule 1.7, determinism) or effectively changes once a pocket is empty. Three-wall art: 6 actions, from Problem 2. A full solution states the action-count for each scenario and flags any scenario where a rule fails outright.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — proof problem, structural comparison)

Prove that the rectangle's symmetry group and the two-light-switch group have "the same structure" in the precise sense used in the aa-00 lesson: exhibit an explicit correspondence between the four configurations of each, and verify that the correspondence is compatible with how actions combine (i.e. that corresponding actions produce corresponding results).

*(composed — formalises the informal claim in Carter §2.5, pp. 19–20, cited in aa-00's lesson and its self-check 2, ahead of gt-05's formal definition of isomorphism)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Label the rectangle's four configurations solved/H/V/both, and the switches' four configurations off-off/on-off/off-on/on-on. Try the obvious pairing.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Pair: solved ↔ off-off, H-flip ↔ on-off, V-flip ↔ off-on, both ↔ on-on (or any consistent labelling). Then check that applying "flip horizontal" to the rectangle and "flip switch 1" to the switches always land on corresponding configurations, for every starting configuration — not just from solved.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
From "solved", flip-H reaches "H-flip", matching off-off → on-off (flip switch 1). From "H-flip", flip-H again reaches "solved" (back to start), matching on-off → off-off (flip switch 1 again, since flipping the same switch twice returns it to off).
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Systematically check all four configurations against both generating actions (flip-H/flip switch-1, flip-V/flip switch-2): in every case, the rectangle's move and the switches' corresponding move land on paired configurations. Since this holds for every configuration and every generator (and hence, by Rule 1.8, for every combination too), the pairing is structure-preserving — this is precisely the informal content of "these are the same group", made rigorous here without yet using the formal word "isomorphism" (deferred to gt-05). **Any correct explicit pairing, fully verified across all four configurations and both generators, earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — composed, distinguishing groups of the same size)

The hook's "dance move" group (quarter-turn, half-turn, three-quarter-turn, stand-still) also has four configurations, like the rectangle. Explain, using the "undo" structure discovered in aa-00 (Discovered fact 2), why this dance-move group is *not* simply a relabelling of the rectangle's Klein four-group — i.e. identify a genuine structural difference between the two, even though both have exactly four elements.

*(composed — extends the aa-00 lesson's explicit preview: "That one has a different map… a preview that not all groups of size 4 look alike")*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
In the rectangle group, every non-identity action undoes itself (apply it twice, get back to start). Check whether this holds for the quarter-turn action.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Apply "turn again" (a quarter-turn) twice, three times, four times, and see when you first return to "standing still".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Quarter-turn once: 90°. Twice: 180°. Three times: 270°. Four times: 360° = back to start. So the quarter-turn action does **not** undo itself after one repetition — it takes four applications to return to standing still.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Contrast with the rectangle: every non-identity action (H-flip, V-flip, 180°-rotation) returns to "solved" after exactly *two* applications — a completely different "undo" pattern. This is a genuine structural difference (later formalised as differing "orders" of elements, in gt-02): the dance-move group has an element whose repeated application cycles through all four configurations before returning, while the rectangle group has no such element. Two groups of the same size can therefore have incompatible internal "clockwork" — state this conclusion explicitly as the answer.
</details>
