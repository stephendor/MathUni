## Problem 1 (easy — running the checklist, and finding which condition bites)

Abbott states the three conditions (Definition 8.2.1, p. 222) and then hands you
the verification. Do it. For each candidate below, either prove it is a metric or
name the **first** condition of Definition 8.2.1 it fails and give a specific
counterexample — two named points, or two named functions, and the numbers.

(a) Abbott's Exercise 8.2.1, on $X = \mathbf{R}^2$ with
$x = (x_1, x_2)$, $y = (y_1, y_2)$:

  (i) $d(x,y) = \sqrt{(x_1-y_1)^2 + (x_2-y_2)^2}$;
  (ii) $d(x,y) = 1$ if $x \neq y$, and $d(x,x) = 0$;
  (iii) $d(x,y) = \max\{|x_1-y_1|, |x_2-y_2|\}$;
  (iv) $d(x,y) = |x_1 x_2 + y_1 y_2|$.

(b) Abbott's Exercise 8.2.2, on $C[0,1]$:

  (i) $d(f,g) = \sup\{|f(x)-g(x)| : x \in [0,1]\}$;
  (ii) $d(f,g) = |f(1) - g(1)|$;
  (iii) $d(f,g) = \int_0^1 |f-g|$.

(c) For (b)(iii), the verification of condition (i) uses a hypothesis that is not
in the formula. Identify it, and produce a set of functions on $[0,1]$ — closed
under the operations you need — on which the same formula is **not** a metric.
This is the difference between a metric and a pseudometric, and you have just
built the example.

(d) Tally your answers. Across all seven candidates, count how many failures were
failures of (i), how many of (ii), how many of (iii). Write down the count. You
will be asked to explain it in Problem 5(e).

*(Abbott, p. 222, for Definition 8.2.1 and Exercises 8.2.1–8.2.3, all three set
as exercises and none worked in the text; Lindström, p. 44, for
Definition 3.1.1, which is the same definition with the conditions named.)*

## Problem 2 (easy–medium — three metrics that do not look like distance)

Lindström's Examples 3–5 (pp. 45–46) and his Exercises for Section 3.1.

(a) The **Manhattan metric** (Example 3),
$d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = |x_1-y_1| + |x_2-y_2|$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Prove it is
a metric, and prove the three inequalities
$$\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty \leq \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \leq \|\mathbf{v}\|_1 \leq 2\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty$$
where $\|\cdot\|_1, \|\cdot\|_2, \|\cdot\|_\infty$ are the Manhattan, Euclidean
and max norms. Each of those three is a one-line computation. Then prove the
sharper bound $\|\mathbf{v}\|_1 \leq \sqrt{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_2$ — the only one
of the four that is Cauchy–Schwarz in disguise — and say what it improves.
Problem 5(c) uses the chain and the sharper bound.

(b) The **Hamming metric** (Example 4). Lindström writes only "it is not hard to
check that $d$ is a metric" and makes it Exercise 1. Check it. The triangle
inequality is the interesting one: prove it index by index, by showing that for
each $n$ the indicator $[x_n \neq z_n]$ is at most
$[x_n \neq y_n] + [y_n \neq z_n]$, and then summing. Say in one sentence why
summing a valid inequality termwise is legitimate here and what would go wrong
if the metric were defined as a maximum over $n$ instead of a sum — is it still
a metric then?

(c) The **sup metric on functions** (Example 5, $d_1$), Lindström's Exercise 2.
Prove $d_1(f,g) = \sup\{|f(x)-g(x)| : x \in [a,b]\}$ is a metric on $C[a,b]$.
Two things to say explicitly and neither is decoration: why the supremum is
**finite** (which hypothesis on the domain and which on $f, g$?), and why
$d_1(f,g) = 0$ forces $f = g$.

(d) Lindström's Exercise 5: let $X$ be the set of **bounded** real sequences and
$d(\{x_n\},\{y_n\}) = \sup\{|x_n - y_n| : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. Show this is a
metric. Then say precisely where the word "bounded" was used, and give an
unbounded pair for which the formula returns something that is not a real number.

(e) Prove Proposition 3.1.4, the **Inverse Triangle Inequality**:
$|d(x,y) - d(x,z)| \leq d(y,z)$. Lindström proves it (p. 47) — reproduce the
proof and then say which of the three conditions of Definition 3.1.1 it uses and
which it does not. Then use it to show that for fixed $z$, the function
$x \mapsto d(x,z)$ is continuous from $X$ to $\mathbb{R}$.

*(Lindström pp. 45–46 for Examples 3, 4, 5; p. 47 for Definition 3.1.2,
Definition 3.1.3 and Proposition 3.1.4, the last of which is proved in the body;
p. 48 for the exercises, which are set and not worked.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
For (b), $[x_n \neq z_n] = 1$ forces at least one of $[x_n \neq y_n]$ and
$[y_n \neq z_n]$ to be 1 — there is nothing to prove when the left side is 0.
For (c), a continuous function on a closed bounded interval is bounded; that is
the hypothesis doing the work, and it is a hypothesis about $[a,b]$ as much as
about $f$.
For (e), the continuity claim is Proposition 3.1.4 read as a Lipschitz bound
with constant 1, so $\delta = \epsilon$ works.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(a) $\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \leq \|\mathbf{v}\|_1$: square both sides and compare
$a^2 + b^2$ with $(|a|+|b|)^2$. $\|\mathbf{v}\|_1 \leq 2\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty$:
each of the two terms is at most the max. $\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty \leq
\|\mathbf{v}\|_2$: the max of two numbers is at most the square root of the sum
of their squares. The Cauchy–Schwarz one is the sharper bound
$\|\mathbf{v}\|_1 \leq \sqrt{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_2$, which you get by pairing
$(|a|,|b|)$ with $(1,1)$.
(c) For the triangle inequality: for each $x$,
$|f(x)-h(x)| \leq |f(x)-g(x)| + |g(x)-h(x)| \leq d_1(f,g) + d_1(g,h)$, so the
right-hand side is an upper bound for the set whose supremum is $d_1(f,h)$.
That last move — "an upper bound for every member is an upper bound for the
supremum" — is the whole proof, and it is the same move you will need for
matchings in tda1-05.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) A maximum over $n$ is still a metric; it is the sup metric of (d) restricted
to sequences of length $K$, and the same termwise argument gives the triangle
inequality with "sum" replaced by "max on both sides". What changes is the
geometry, not the axioms: the Hamming metric counts how many symbols were
corrupted, the max metric only records whether any were.

(d) Boundedness is what makes the supremum a real number rather than $+\infty$.
Take $x_n = n$ and $y_n = 0$: the set $\{|x_n - y_n|\} = \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ has no
real upper bound, so $d$ is not $[0,\infty)$-valued and Definition 3.1.1's
codomain fails before any of the three conditions is tested.

(e) The proof uses the Triangle Inequality twice and **symmetry** once — at the
step $d(z,y) = d(y,z)$. It does not use positivity at all.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) *Triangle inequality.* Fix an index $n$. If $x_n = z_n$ the left-hand
indicator is $0$ and there is nothing to prove. If $x_n \neq z_n$, then $y_n$
cannot equal both $x_n$ and $z_n$, so at least one of $[x_n \neq y_n]$,
$[y_n \neq z_n]$ equals 1, and the right-hand side is at least 1. Hence
$[x_n \neq z_n] \leq [x_n \neq y_n] + [y_n \neq z_n]$ for every $n$. Summing over
$n = 1, \dots, K$ — a finite sum of valid inequalities — gives
$d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) \leq d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) + d(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})$. ∎

(e) Since $|d(x,y)-d(x,z)|$ is the larger of $d(x,y)-d(x,z)$ and
$d(x,z)-d(x,y)$, it is enough to bound both by $d(y,z)$. The Triangle Inequality
gives $d(x,y) \leq d(x,z) + d(z,y)$, so $d(x,y)-d(x,z) \leq d(z,y) = d(y,z)$.
⟨your step: get the other inequality the same way, and mark the exact character
at which symmetry was used⟩

## Problem 3 (medium — convergence, and the trick that makes every metric space one-dimensional)

(a) State Definition 3.2.1 and prove **Lemma 3.2.2**: $\{x_n\}$ converges to $a$
in $(X,d)$ if and only if $\lim_{n\to\infty} d(x_n,a) = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}$.
Then say what this lemma buys: name the class of questions about convergence in
an arbitrary metric space that it converts into questions about a sequence of
**real numbers**, and name one question about a sequence that it does **not**
convert (look at Definition 8.2.3, Abbott's Cauchy condition, and count how many
free variables each definition has).

(b) Prove **Proposition 3.2.3**: a sequence in a metric space cannot converge to
more than one point. Lindström proves this at p. 49 and then remarks: "Observe
how we used the conditions in Definition 3.1.1 in the proof above. So far they
are all we know about metric spaces." Take him at his word — annotate the proof
so that every appeal to (i), (ii) or (iii) is marked, and confirm that nothing
else is used.

(c) Now break it. In Munkres's three-point space with topology
$\{\varnothing, X, \{b\}, \{a,b\}, \{b,c\}\}$ on $X = \{a,b,c\}$, the constant
sequence $x_n = b$ converges to all three points. Proposition 3.2.3 forbids this
in a metric space. Resolve the apparent conflict in one sentence — and then, in a
second sentence, say which *specific* condition of Definition 3.1.1 the proof of
3.2.3 used at the final step, so that you can name what a topology lacks.

(d) Lindström's Exercise 1 to Section 3.2: in a **discrete** metric space, show
that $\{x_n\}$ converges to $a$ if and only if there is an $N$ with $x_n = a$ for
all $n \geq N$. Then say what this makes of Lemma 3.2.2 in that space: the
sequence $d(x_n, a)$ is a sequence of what, and "converging to 0" means what?

(e) Prove **Proposition 3.2.5**: $f$ is continuous at $a$ if and only if
$f(x_n) \to f(a)$ for every sequence $x_n \to a$. Lindström proves the reverse
direction by contraposition, constructing $x_n$ with $d_X(x_n,a) < 1/n$ but
$d_Y(f(x_n), f(a)) \geq \epsilon$. Identify the choice being made infinitely
often, and say what principle licenses it. Then use 3.2.5 to prove
Proposition 3.2.6, that a composition of continuous functions is continuous —
Lindström's own application, and three lines.

*(Lindström p. 48 for Definition 3.2.1; p. 49 for Lemma 3.2.2 and
Proposition 3.2.3 with proof; pp. 49–50 for Definition 3.2.4 and
Proposition 3.2.5 with proof; p. 51 for Proposition 3.2.6 and Definition 3.2.7;
Abbott p. 223 for Definition 8.2.2 and Definition 8.2.3. Exercise 1 to

## Problem 4 (medium–hard — continuity, three definitions and one that mentions no numbers)

Lindström gives continuity three times: with $\epsilon$ and $\delta$
(Definition 3.2.4), with sequences (Proposition 3.2.5), and with preimages of
open sets (Proposition 3.3.10). This problem is about why the third one is worth
the trouble.

(a) State Definition 3.3.1 (open ball), Definition 3.3.2 (open and closed via
boundary points), and Abbott's Definition 8.2.6 ($\epsilon$-neighbourhood). Note
that Lindström defines "open" by *boundary points* and Abbott by
$\epsilon$-neighbourhoods, and that Proposition 3.3.3 is the bridge. Prove
Proposition 3.3.3.

(b) Prove **Lemma 3.3.6**: every open ball is an open set. Lindström's proof
takes $\epsilon = r - d(x,a)$ and applies the Triangle Inequality. Reproduce it,
and then say why this lemma is not a tautology despite the names — what exactly
would be broken if it failed? Then prove the closed-ball half, which Lindström
leaves as Exercise 5.

(c) Prove **Proposition 3.3.10**: $f : X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if
$f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ for every open $V \subseteq Y$. Then answer the
question the statement raises: the definition of continuity at a point mentioned
$\epsilon$ and $\delta$; this statement mentions neither, nor any distance at
all. List every ingredient the statement uses, and confirm that "open set" is the
only one.

(d) The converse trap. Lindström's Example 1 (p. 57) takes $f(x) = x^2$ and
$g(x) = \arctan x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and observes that $f(\mathbb{R}) = [0,\infty)$
is not open while $g(\mathbb{R}) = (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ is not closed. Say what this
shows about *images* under continuous maps, and why Proposition 3.3.10 is
therefore stated with **preimages** and could not be stated with images.

(e) Prove **Proposition 3.3.12**: arbitrary unions of open sets are open, and
finite intersections of open sets are open. Lindström leaves both to
Exercise 11, and asks there for an example showing the intersection of
infinitely many open sets need not be open. Give that example. Then prove
Proposition 3.3.13 for closed sets, or derive it from 3.3.12 and
Proposition 3.3.4 — and say which derivation is shorter and why the word
"finite" moves from one clause to the other.

*(Lindström p. 52 for Definition 3.3.1; p. 53 for the three positions of a point
and Definition 3.3.2; p. 54 for Proposition 3.3.3, Proposition 3.3.4 with proof,
Proposition 3.3.5 left to the reader, and Lemma 3.3.6 with the open half proved;
