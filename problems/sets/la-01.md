## Problem 1 (easy — computation)

Find $x \in \mathbb{R}^4$ such that
$$(4,-3,1,7) + 2x = (5,9,-6,8).$$

*(Axler §1.A, Exercise 10, p. 11)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Treat this exactly like solving $a + 2x = b$ in ordinary algebra, but coordinate by coordinate.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Isolate $x$: $2x = (5,9,-6,8) - (4,-3,1,7)$, then divide by 2 componentwise.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$(5,9,-6,8)-(4,-3,1,7) = (1,12,-7,1)$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$x = \tfrac12(1,12,-7,1) = (\tfrac12, 6, -\tfrac72, \tfrac12)$. Check by substituting back into the original equation.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — proof, using axioms)

Prove that $-(-v) = v$ for every $v \in V$, where $V$ is a vector space.

*(Axler §1.B, Exercise 1, p. 17)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
What does it mean, by definition, for $-v$ to be "the additive inverse of $v$"? And what would it mean for $v$ itself to be an additive inverse of $-v$?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Show that $v$ satisfies the defining property of "the additive inverse of $-v$", then invoke uniqueness of additive inverses.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
By definition of $-v$ as the additive inverse of $v$: $v + (-v) = 0$. This equation also says exactly that $v$ is *an* additive inverse of $-v$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Since additive inverses are unique (this follows from the vector space axioms via the same cancellation-style argument as Theorem 1.25's uniqueness-of-identity proof), and $v + (-v) = 0$ exhibits $v$ as an additive inverse of $-v$, we conclude $v = -(-v)$, the unique additive inverse of $-v$. Write out the uniqueness lemma explicitly if it hasn't been proved yet in your solution.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — proof, using axioms)

Suppose $a \in \mathbb{F}$, $v \in V$, and $av = 0$. Prove that $a = 0$ or $v = 0$.

*(Axler §1.B, Exercise 2, p. 17)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Split into two cases on whether $a = 0$ or $a \ne 0$ — this is a direct proof by cases, the same idiom pw-01 introduced for integers, now applied to a vector space.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
If $a = 0$, you're immediately done. If $a \ne 0$, then $a$ has a multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$ in the field $\mathbb{F}$ — use it to isolate $v$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
If $a \ne 0$: multiply both sides of $av = 0$ by $a^{-1}$: $a^{-1}(av) = a^{-1}0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
By associativity of scalar multiplication, $a^{-1}(av) = (a^{-1}a)v = 1v = v$. And $a^{-1}0 = 0$ (scalar times the zero vector is the zero vector — a fact analogous to Theorem 1.29's $0v=0$, provable the same way). So $v = 0$. Combined with the $a=0$ case, this covers both branches of the "or".
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — proof, the empty set) [interleaves pw-01]

The empty set is not a vector space. It fails to satisfy only one of the requirements listed in Definition 1.19 — which one, and why? Give a direct proof (in the sense of pw-01) that this requirement fails.

*(Axler §1.B, Exercise 4, p. 17)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Every one of Definition 1.19's clauses is phrased as "for all $v\in V$…" or "there exists…". Which clause can the empty set never satisfy, purely because it has no elements at all?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The additive identity clause demands "there exists $0 \in V$ such that…". If $V = \varnothing$, no element of $V$ exists at all, so this existential claim is vacuously false.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
State the additive identity axiom precisely, then observe $\varnothing$ contains no elements whatsoever.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
Direct proof: assume $V = \varnothing$ satisfies Definition 1.19's additive-identity clause. Then there exists some $0 \in \varnothing$. But no element belongs to $\varnothing$, by definition of the empty set — contradiction with the assumption, so the clause cannot hold. (Note this is really argued *by contradiction*, even though the problem asks you to frame it "directly" — comment on this tension explicitly: the cleanest proof that an existential statement fails is often to assume it holds and derive an immediate absurdity.)
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — proof, direct sum construction)

Suppose
$$U = \{(x,x,y,y) \in \mathbb{F}^4 : x,y \in \mathbb{F}\}.$$
Find a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{F}^4$ such that $\mathbb{F}^4 = U \oplus W$, and prove your answer is correct.

*(Axler §1.C, Exercise 20, p. 25 — flagged as the "you try" in the la-02 lesson; included here as a harder synthesis problem since it draws on la-01's axioms plus subspace intuition previewed at the end of la-01)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$U$ ties coordinates 1–2 together and coordinates 3–4 together. What "independent wiggle room" is missing?
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Try $W = \{(x,-x,y,-y) : x,y\in\mathbb{F}\}$ or similar — you need $U+W=\mathbb{F}^4$ and $U\cap W=\{0\}$ (the two-subspace intersection test).
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
A convenient choice: $W = \{(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{F}^4 : b=0, d=0\}$, i.e. $W=\{(a,0,c,0):a,c\in\mathbb{F}\}$. Check $U\cap W$: if $(x,x,y,y)=(a,0,c,0)$ then $x=0$ and $y=0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
So $U \cap W = \{0\}$, giving directness by the two-subspace test. For the sum: any $(p,q,r,s)\in\mathbb{F}^4$ can be written as $(q,q,s,s) + (p-q,0,r-s,0)$, the first summand in $U$, the second in $W$. So $U+W=\mathbb{F}^4$, and together with directness, $\mathbb{F}^4=U\oplus W$. **Any logically valid alternative choice of $W$ (with full justification) earns full marks.**
</details>

## Problem 6 (hard — proof, function-space vector space)

Let $\infty$ and $-\infty$ denote two distinct objects not in $\mathbb{R}$. Addition and scalar multiplication are extended to $\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\cup\{-\infty\}$ as in Axler's exercise (ordinary arithmetic on reals; $t\infty$, $t(-\infty)$, and sums with $\pm\infty$ defined by the natural sign rules, with $\infty+(-\infty)=0$). Is $\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\cup\{-\infty\}$ a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$? Justify your answer fully.

*(Axler §1.B, Exercise 6, p. 17)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Try to find a single vector space axiom from Definition 1.19 that this structure violates — you do not need to check all eight if one clearly fails.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Test associativity of addition using $\infty$ and $-\infty$ together with an ordinary real number, or test the additive inverse requirement.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Consider $(\infty + (-\infty)) + \infty$ versus $\infty + ((-\infty)+\infty)$. Both parenthesisations should give the same value if associativity holds — compute each using the given rules.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
$(\infty+(-\infty))+\infty = 0+\infty=\infty$. But also try $\infty + (-\infty+\infty) = \infty+0=\infty$ — these happen to agree, so look instead at whether every element has an additive inverse, or test associativity with three copies of $\infty$: $(\infty+\infty)+(-\infty) = \infty+(-\infty)=0$, versus $\infty+(\infty+(-\infty))=\infty+0=\infty$. These disagree ($0\ne\infty$), so associativity fails. Conclude: this is **not** a vector space, since Definition 1.19's associativity clause fails for this specific triple. **Any correct identification of a failing axiom, fully justified with an explicit counterexample, earns full marks.**
</details>
