## Problem 1 (easy — cochains and coboundaries over $\mathbb{Z}_2$)

Work over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ throughout, as Dey §2.5.4 does.

(a) Define a $p$-cochain, and explain why specifying $\phi(\sigma) \in \{0,1\}$
on every $p$-simplex $\sigma$ determines $\phi$ on all of $\mathsf{C}_p$.
(b) Define the coboundary $\delta\phi$ by $\delta\phi(c) = \phi(\partial c)$ and
check that $\delta\phi$ is a homomorphism.
(c) Prove Fact 2.13, that $\delta_p \circ \delta_{p-1} = 0$, and say what it
implies about $\mathsf{B}^p$ and $\mathsf{Z}^p$.
(d) State Definition 2.29 and say which two subspaces of $\mathsf{C}^p$ it
quotients.

*(Dey §2.5.4, pp. 55–56)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\mathsf{C}_p$ is a vector space with the $p$-simplices as a basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(c) is a one-line dualisation of $\partial \circ \partial = 0$ — write out
$\delta\delta\phi(c)$ and push the two $\delta$'s onto $c$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$\delta\delta\phi(c) = \delta\phi(\partial c) = \phi(\partial\partial c) = \phi(0) = 0$.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) A $p$-cochain is a homomorphism $\phi : \mathsf{C}_p \to \mathbb{Z}_2$. Since
$\mathsf{C}_p$ has the $p$-simplices as a basis, $\phi$ is determined by its
values on them, and a chain $c = \sum_i \alpha_i \sigma_i$ receives
$\phi(c) = \sum_i \alpha_i \phi(\sigma_i)$. Conversely any assignment of $0$s and
$1$s to simplices extends to a homomorphism.
(b) $\delta\phi(c + c') = \phi(\partial(c+c')) = \phi(\partial c + \partial c') = \phi(\partial c) + \phi(\partial c') = \delta\phi(c) + \delta\phi(c')$,
using that $\partial$ and $\phi$ are both homomorphisms.
(c) $\delta\delta\phi(c) = \delta\phi(\partial c) = \phi(\partial\partial c) = \phi(0) = 0$
for every chain $c$, so $\delta_p \circ \delta_{p-1} = 0$. Hence every
coboundary is a cocycle: $\mathsf{B}^p \subseteq \mathsf{Z}^p$.
(d) Definition 2.29: since $\mathsf{B}^p$ is a subgroup of $\mathsf{Z}^p$, the
quotient $\mathsf{H}^p = \mathsf{Z}^p/\mathsf{B}^p$ is well defined and is the
$p$-th cohomology group. The two subspaces are the cocycles $\mathsf{Z}^p$ and
the coboundaries $\mathsf{B}^p$.
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy — Example 2.2 by hand)

Reproduce and extend Dey's Example 2.2, working over $\mathbb{Z}_2$.

(a) Let $K$ be the complex on vertices $a, b, c$ with edges $ab$, $bc$, $ac$ and
**no** triangle. Show the $1$-cochain $\phi$ with support $\{ac\}$ is a cocycle,
and show it is not a coboundary by writing out the three equations a candidate
$0$-cochain $\phi'$ would have to satisfy.
(b) Now add the triangle $abc$. Show that the cochain with support $\{ac\}$ is no
longer a cocycle, and that the cochain with support $\{ab, ac\}$ is both a
cocycle and a coboundary — exhibit the $0$-cochain producing it.
(c) In (a), how many distinct classes are there in $\mathsf{H}^1(K)$? Justify by
counting cocycles and coboundaries.
(d) Dey says two cochains $\phi$ and $\phi'$ are cohomologous when
$[\phi] + [\phi'] = [0]$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$. Explain why over $\mathbb{Z}_2$
this is the same as $[\phi] = [\phi']$.

*(Dey §2.5.4, Example 2.2 and Figure 2.14, pp. 56–57)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
$\delta_0\phi'(uv) = \phi'(u + v) = \phi'(u) + \phi'(v)$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
For (a), the three equations are a linear system over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ in the
three unknowns $\phi'(a), \phi'(b), \phi'(c)$; show it is inconsistent by adding
equations together.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Adding the three required equations gives $0 = 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$, since each
vertex value appears exactly twice on the left.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) There is no triangle, hence no non-zero $2$-cochain, so $\delta_1\phi = 0$
automatically and $\phi$ is a cocycle. If $\phi = \delta_0\phi'$ then
$\phi'(a)+\phi'(c) = 1$, $\phi'(a)+\phi'(b) = 0$, $\phi'(b)+\phi'(c) = 0$. Adding
all three, each of $\phi'(a), \phi'(b), \phi'(c)$ appears twice and cancels over
$\mathbb{Z}_2$, giving $0 = 1$ — a contradiction. So $\phi$ is not a coboundary.
(b) With $abc$ present, $\delta_1\phi(abc) = \phi(ab + ac + bc) = \phi(ac) = 1 \neq 0$,
so the support-$\{ac\}$ cochain is not a cocycle. For $\phi$ with support
$\{ab, ac\}$: $\delta_1\phi(abc) = \phi(ab) + \phi(ac) + \phi(bc) = 1 + 1 + 0 = 0$,
a cocycle; and taking $\phi'$ with $\phi'(a) = 1$, $\phi'(b) = \phi'(c) = 0$ gives
$\delta_0\phi'(ab) = 1$, $\delta_0\phi'(ac) = 1$, $\delta_0\phi'(bc) = 0$, so
$\phi = \delta_0\phi'$ is a coboundary.
(c) In (a) every $1$-cochain is a cocycle, so $\dim \mathsf{Z}^1 = 3$. The
coboundaries are the image of $\delta_0$ on the $8$ possible $0$-cochains; the
kernel of $\delta_0$ is the constants (dimension $1$, as $K$ is connected), so
$\dim \mathsf{B}^1 = 3 - 1 = 2$. Hence $\dim \mathsf{H}^1 = 1$ and there are two
classes, the trivial one and $[\phi]$.
(d) Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ every element is its own additive inverse, so
$[\phi] + [\phi'] = [0]$ rearranges to $[\phi] = -[\phi'] = [\phi']$.
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — why compute cohomology at all)

(a) State the field-coefficient form of the universal coefficient theorem from
Hatcher §3.1 and deduce that $\dim H^k(X;F) = \dim H_k(X;F)$ for a field $F$,
whenever the dimensions are finite.
(b) Dey observes that $\mathsf{H}^p(K)$ is *not equal* to
$\mathsf{Hom}(\mathsf{H}_p(K), \mathbb{Z}_2)$ although the two are isomorphic
over $\mathbb{Z}_2$. Explain the distinction being drawn, and why it matters that
the isomorphism is not an equality.
(c) State Fact 2.14 and explain how it differs from the corresponding statement
for homology.
(d) Given (a), what does computing cohomology instead of homology buy you at a
single scale, and what must be true of the *maps* in a filtration for the
advantage to persist across scales? You are not asked to prove the persistent
statement — say precisely what would have to be supplied.

*(Dey §2.5.4, pp. 55, 57; Hatcher §3.1, p. 198)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Over a field the Ext term of the universal coefficient sequence vanishes.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) is about the difference between a canonical construction and a chosen
isomorphism; (d) is about the difference between a degreewise statement and a
statement about a diagram.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
$H^n(X;F) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_F(H_n(X;F), F)$, and a finite-dimensional
vector space has the same dimension as its dual.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Hatcher: with field coefficients the $\operatorname{Ext}_F$ terms vanish, so
$H^n(X;F) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_F(H_n(X;F), F)$ — cohomology is the exact dual
of homology. A finite-dimensional vector space and its dual have equal
dimension, so $\dim H^k(X;F) = \dim H_k(X;F)$.
(b) $\mathsf{H}^p(K)$ is defined as $\mathsf{Z}^p/\mathsf{B}^p$, a quotient of
spaces of cochains; $\mathsf{Hom}(\mathsf{H}_p(K),\mathbb{Z}_2)$ is the dual of
the homology. These are different constructions that happen to be isomorphic
over a field. It matters because computations are performed on the cochain
model — reductions, matrix operations, representative cocycles — and the
representative you obtain is a cocycle, not a functional on homology; only the
isomorphism class transfers.
(c) Fact 2.14: a simplicial map $f : K_1 \to K_2$ induces
$f^* : \mathsf{H}^p(K_2) \to \mathsf{H}^p(K_1)$ for every $p \geq 0$ — in the
*opposite* direction to the induced map on homology. It comes from the cochain
map $f^{\#}(\phi)(c) = \phi(f_{\#}(c))$.
(d) At a single scale it buys nothing in the answer — the dimensions agree by
(a) — so any advantage is computational. For an advantage across scales one
needs the diagram to correspond, not merely the individual groups: the maps
induced by the filtration's inclusions on cohomology must be the duals of those
on homology, so that the two persistence modules are dual and their interval
decompositions match. That correspondence is what a source would have to
supply; §2.5.4 and §6.13 establish the degreewise duality and Fact 2.14's
contravariance only, and Dey's cohomology-based persistence algorithm is
developed later, in the annotation method of §4.2.1 which Fact 2.14 explicitly
points forward to.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — the four steps, and which one needs $\mathbb{Z}$)

Ghrist §6.13 summarises the de Silva–Morozov–Vejdemo-Johansson construction in
four steps.

(a) State step 1, including the homotopy-theoretic input, and explain why it
makes cohomology the natural tool for coordinatising a point cloud.
(b) State step 2 and say why field coefficients are required, naming the result
Ghrist cites, and why $\mathbb{F}_p$ is preferred over $\mathbb{R}$ in practice.
(c) State step 3 and identify precisely the mathematical obstacle it removes.
(d) State step 4 and say what defect of the raw integral cocycle it repairs.
Then answer the design question: at which step does a barcode computation stop,
and at which step does a circular coordinate begin?

*(Ghrist §6.13, pp. 131–132)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Step 1 is $[X, \mathbb{S}^1] \cong H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$ — note the coefficients.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The tension in the whole construction is that step 2 wants a field and step 1
wants $\mathbb{Z}$; step 3 exists solely to get from one to the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
Ghrist prefers $\mathbb{F}_p$ for numerical reasons — to avoid roundoff errors.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) Step 1: for any space $X$, the group of basepoint-preserving homotopy
classes $[X, \mathbb{S}^1]$ of maps $X \to \mathbb{S}^1$ is isomorphic to
$H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$. A circular coordinate is exactly a map $X \to \mathbb{S}^1$
up to homotopy, so producing one is the same as producing an integral
$1$-dimensional cohomology class.
(b) Step 2: compute the persistent cohomology of a sequence of Vietoris–Rips
complexes. Field coefficients are required for the Structure Theorem
(Ghrist's Theorem 5.21); coefficients in a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$ are
preferred for numerical reasons, to avoid roundoff errors.
(c) Step 3: convert a persistent class $[\alpha_p] \in H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$ into
an integral class $[\alpha] \in H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$, using the long exact sequence
coming from the short exact sequence of coefficients
$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot p} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{F}_p \to 0$. The
obstacle removed is precisely the mismatch between step 1, which needs
$\mathbb{Z}$, and step 2, which needs a field.
(d) Step 4: the raw integer cocycle may be a poor coordinatisation, with all the
circular motion concentrated over a small subset of $X$; lifting to $\mathbb{R}$
coefficients and replacing $\alpha$ by a cohomologous harmonic cocycle
$\overline{\alpha}$ produces, by the local-averaging properties of the
Laplacian, a well-regulated coordinate function $\Theta : X \to \mathbb{S}^1$.
A barcode computation stops at the end of step 2 — it needs only the field
class. A circular coordinate begins at step 3 and is not available until the
integral lift has been obtained and step 4 performed.
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — the lift, and what has to be checked)

(a) Write out the long exact sequence Ghrist uses, and prove that if
$\ker(\cdot p : H^2(X;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^2(X;\mathbb{Z}))$ is zero then
$H^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$ is surjective. Name the exactness
step you use.
(b) Describe the elements of that kernel, and say why Ghrist regards them as
rare for $p > 2$ in spaces behind data sets. Is "rare" the same as "absent"?
(c) Suppose a practitioner computes a persistent $H^1$ barcode over
$\mathbb{F}_{11}$, takes the longest bar, and immediately smooths and integrates
its representative cocycle to produce circular coordinates, without performing
step 3. State exactly which claim in the pipeline is now unsupported, and
describe what could be observed in the output if the class did not in fact lift.
(d) A colleague argues that because persistent cohomology over $\mathbb{F}_p$
gives the same Betti numbers as persistent homology, "the coefficient field is
just an implementation detail". Rebut this using the distinction between the two
uses of cohomology in this unit.

*(Ghrist §6.13, step 3, pp. 131–132)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
Exactness at $H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$ says the image of the map from
$H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$ equals the kernel of $\delta$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Chase the sequence: if $\operatorname{im}\delta = \ker(\cdot p) = 0$ then
$\delta$ is the zero map, so everything in $H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$ is in the kernel
of $\delta$, hence in the image of the previous map.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
The kernel of $\cdot p$ on $H^2(X;\mathbb{Z})$ consists of the $p$-torsional
classes.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) The coefficient sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot p} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{F}_p \to 0$
gives a short exact sequence of cochain complexes and hence a long exact
sequence
$\cdots \to H^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p) \xrightarrow{\delta} H^2(X;\mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\cdot p} H^2(X;\mathbb{Z}) \to \cdots$.
By exactness at the third term, $\ker(\cdot p) = \operatorname{im}\delta$. If
$\ker(\cdot p) = 0$ then $\operatorname{im}\delta = 0$, so $\delta$ is the zero
map and $\ker\delta$ is all of $H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$. By exactness at
$H^1(X;\mathbb{F}_p)$, that kernel is the image of $H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$, so the
map is surjective and every $\mathbb{F}_p$ class lifts.
(b) The kernel consists of the $p$-torsional classes of $H^2(X;\mathbb{Z})$.
Ghrist notes these "would seem to be rare occurrences" in organic spaces behind
data sets for $p > 2$. Rare is emphatically not absent: it is a heuristic
expectation about the spaces one meets, not a theorem, so the hypothesis
$\ker(\cdot p) = 0$ is an assumption to be checked or at least declared, not one
that holds automatically.
(c) The unsupported claim is step 1's: that the object being smoothed and
integrated corresponds to a homotopy class of maps $X \to \mathbb{S}^1$. That
correspondence is with $H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$, and an $\mathbb{F}_p$ class that does
not lift is not an integral class at all. What could be observed is a coordinate
that fails to close up consistently — integrating the smoothed cocycle around
some loop returns a value differing from its start by something that is not a
whole number of turns, so the resulting $\Theta$ is not well defined as a map to
$\mathbb{S}^1$ even though every local computation succeeded.
(d) The two uses are different. As a *barcode* computation, cohomology over
$\mathbb{F}_p$ is defensible on the grounds of (Problem 3) degreewise duality
over a field, and there the choice of $p$ genuinely is largely an
implementation matter — subject to the caveat that torsion invisible to one
field may be visible to another. As a *coordinatisation*, the coefficient ring
is the entire content: $[X,\mathbb{S}^1] \cong H^1(X;\mathbb{Z})$ holds over
$\mathbb{Z}$ and over nothing else, which is why step 3 exists. Collapsing the
two uses is exactly the error that lets an unlifted class be smoothed into a
coordinate that does not exist.
</details>
