## Problem 1 (easy — the boundary map, by hand)

(a) Write out $\partial_n[v_0, \dots, v_n]$ for $n = 1, 2, 3$ from the formula
$\partial_n(\sigma) = \sum_i (-1)^i \sigma|[v_0, \dots, \hat v_i, \dots, v_n]$,
and check each against the picture: the signs should make the faces coherently
oriented.
(b) Prove Lemma 2.1, that $\partial_{n-1}\partial_n = 0$. Expand the double sum
into the $j < i$ and $j > i$ parts, and show that swapping the names $i$ and
$j$ in the second turns it into the negative of the first. Be explicit about
where the extra $-1$ in the exponent $(-1)^{j-1}$ comes from.
(c) Dexter states the same lemma and writes, in place of a proof, "This just
involves expanding the definition and working through the mess." Compare his
setup with Hatcher's: Dexter works with *oriented* simplices in a simplicial
complex, identifying $(a_0, \dots, a_n)$ with its even permutations, while
Hatcher works with Δ-complexes whose simplices come with an ordering built in.
Say in two sentences what Dexter's orientation convention is doing that
Hatcher's ordering does for free.
(d) State what $\partial_n \partial_{n+1} = 0$ gives you, in the form Hatcher
puts it on p. 106: an inclusion between two subgroups of $C_n$. Then define
$H_n$ and name its elements.

*(Hatcher §2.1, pp. 105–106; Dexter §7.1, Defn (Oriented n-simplex), Defn
(Boundary homomorphisms) and the Lemma)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(b) When you delete $v_i$ first and then $v_j$ with $j > i$, the index of
$v_j$ in the shortened list has dropped by one.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The whole of homology rests on this one computation, and it is a
bookkeeping identity, not a geometric insight. Do it once carefully and you
never have to do it again — Hatcher explicitly reuses the same proof verbatim
for singular simplices on p. 108.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $\partial[v_0,v_1] = [v_1] - [v_0]$;
$\partial[v_0,v_1,v_2] = [v_1,v_2] - [v_0,v_2] + [v_0,v_1]$;
$\partial[v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3] = [v_1,v_2,v_3] - [v_0,v_2,v_3] + [v_0,v_1,v_3] - [v_0,v_1,v_2]$.
(d) $\partial_n\partial_{n+1} = 0$ says exactly $\operatorname{Im}\partial_{n+1} \subseteq \operatorname{Ker}\partial_n$,
which is what makes the quotient $H_n = \operatorname{Ker}\partial_n / \operatorname{Im}\partial_{n+1}$
a group at all. Elements of $\operatorname{Ker}\partial_n$ are *cycles*, of
$\operatorname{Im}\partial_{n+1}$ are *boundaries*, of $H_n$ are *homology
classes*; two cycles are *homologous* when their difference is a boundary.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(b) $\partial_{n-1}\partial_n(\sigma) = \sum_{j<i}(-1)^i(-1)^j\,\sigma|[v_0,\dots,\hat v_j,\dots,\hat v_i,\dots,v_n]
+ \sum_{j>i}(-1)^i(-1)^{j-1}\,\sigma|[v_0,\dots,\hat v_i,\dots,\hat v_j,\dots,v_n]$.
⟨your step: in the second sum interchange the names of $i$ and $j$. The two
summations then run over the same index set and have the same simplices; compare
the signs and conclude.⟩
</details>

## Problem 2 (easy/medium — compute)

Work each of these from a Δ-complex structure, writing down $\Delta_n$, the
matrices of the boundary maps, and then kernels and images.

(a) $S^1$ with one vertex and one edge (Example 2.2).
(b) The torus $T$ with one vertex, three edges $a, b, c$ and two 2-simplices
$U, L$ (Example 2.3). Check that $\{a, b, a+b-c\}$ really is a basis for
$\Delta_1(T)$ before using it.
(c) $\mathbb{R}P^2$ with two vertices, three edges and two 2-simplices
(Example 2.4). This is the first computation that produces torsion; identify
exactly which step does so.
(d) $S^n$, built from two copies of $\Delta^n$ with boundaries identified by
the identity (Example 2.5). Compute $H_n^\Delta(S^n)$, and say why Hatcher
remarks that the other groups "would be more difficult" for this structure.
(e) Compare (b) and (c) with the fundamental groups you computed in at1-05:
$\pi_1(T) \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ and
$\pi_1(\mathbb{R}P^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$. What is the pattern, and what would
you conjecture about $H_1$ in general?

*(Hatcher §2.1, Examples 2.2, 2.3, 2.4 and 2.5, pp. 106–107)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) The image of $\partial_2$ is an index-two subgroup of $\ker \partial_1$.
Index two in a rank-two free abelian group is where the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ comes
from.
(e) You are rediscovering Hatcher's Theorem 2A.1. Conjecture it; do not claim
to have proved it.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Set each computation out as a table: chain groups across the top, boundary
matrices between them. Then homology is two linear-algebra questions per
column. Doing it this way makes the point that tda1-02's boundary-matrix
reduction is not a new idea but this idea, mechanised.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) $\partial_1 = 0$ since the torus has one vertex, so
$H_0^\Delta(T) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Both 2-simplices have
$\partial_2 U = a + b - c = \partial_2 L$, so
$\operatorname{Im}\partial_2 = \langle a+b-c \rangle$ and
$H_1^\Delta(T) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ on $[a]$ and $[b]$. There are
no 3-simplices, so $H_2^\Delta(T) = \ker \partial_2$, infinite cyclic on
$U - L$ because $\partial(pU + qL) = (p+q)(a+b-c)$ vanishes only when $p = -q$.
(c) $\partial_2 U = -a+b+c$ and $\partial_2 L = a-b+c$, so $\partial_2$ is
injective and $H_2^\Delta = 0$. ⟨your step: take $c$ and $a-b+c$ as a basis of
$\ker \partial_1$, and $a-b+c$ and $2c$ as a basis of
$\operatorname{Im}\partial_2$; the factor of 2 is the whole answer.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) One vertex $v$, one edge $e$, nothing above. $\Delta_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ on
$v$, $\Delta_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ on $e$, $\Delta_n = 0$ for $n \geq 2$. The only
boundary map that could be non-zero is $\partial_1$, and
$\partial_1 e = v - v = 0$. ⟨your step: with every boundary map zero, read off
the homology directly, and state the general principle Hatcher draws from this
on p. 106.⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — singular homology gets off the ground)

(a) Prove Proposition 2.7: $H_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for non-empty
path-connected $X$. Define $\varepsilon(\sum_i n_i\sigma_i) = \sum_i n_i$, show
$\operatorname{Im}\partial_1 \subseteq \ker\varepsilon$, and then do the harder
inclusion by choosing paths from a basepoint.
(b) Prove Proposition 2.8: a point has $H_n = 0$ for $n > 0$. Write out the
chain complex and identify the pattern of alternating maps.
(c) Define reduced homology $\tilde H_n$ via the augmented chain complex, and
prove $H_0(X) \cong \tilde H_0(X) \oplus \mathbb{Z}$. Explain why $X$ must be
required non-empty.
(d) Hatcher observes on p. 108 that two properties trivial for $H_n^\Delta$ are
"not at all clear" for $H_n$: finite generation, and vanishing above the
dimension. Say why each is obvious simplicially and obscure singularly, and
name the theorem that eventually recovers them.

*(Hatcher §2.1: singular simplices and $C_n(X)$, p. 108; Propositions 2.6, 2.7
and 2.8 with their proofs, pp. 109–110; reduced homology, p. 110)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) For the hard inclusion: if $\sum n_i = 0$, join each point $\sigma_i$ to a
basepoint by a path $\tau_i$ and compute $\partial(\sum_i n_i \tau_i)$.
(c) With $X$ empty the augmented complex would have homology in degree $-1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
These are the first proofs in the subject where the object is enormous — the
set of singular $n$-simplices in a space is usually uncountable — and the proof
is nevertheless three lines. That contrast is the point of the section. Notice
in each case that nothing is done with the whole of $C_n(X)$; one only ever
manipulates a finite formal sum.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) Simplicially, $\Delta_n(X)$ is free on the finitely many $n$-simplices and
is literally zero above the dimension of $X$, so both properties are immediate
from the definition. Singularly, $C_n(X)$ is free on all continuous maps
$\Delta^n \to X$, and is non-zero in every degree for every non-empty $X$,
since the constant maps alone are already there. How much bigger it gets
depends on the space, and not monotonically in any naive sense: for
$X = [0,1]$ there are uncountably many singular $n$-simplices in every degree,
whereas for a discrete space on $m$ points there are exactly $m$, because
$\Delta^n$ is connected and so every map out of it is constant. The property
that matters here is the one that holds in all cases: $C_n(X) \neq 0$ for
every $n \geq 0$. ⟨your step: name the theorem that closes the
gap, and say what it lets you do in practice.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) $H_0(X) = C_0(X)/\operatorname{Im}\partial_1$ because $\partial_0 = 0$.
Define $\varepsilon : C_0(X) \to \mathbb{Z}$ by
$\varepsilon(\sum_i n_i \sigma_i) = \sum_i n_i$; it is surjective as soon as $X$
is non-empty. For $\operatorname{Im}\partial_1 \subseteq \ker \varepsilon$: a
singular 1-simplex has $\partial_1 \sigma = \sigma|[v_1] - \sigma|[v_0]$, so
$\varepsilon\partial_1\sigma = 1 - 1 = 0$. ⟨your step: the reverse inclusion.
Given $\sum_i n_i \sigma_i$ with $\sum_i n_i = 0$, the $\sigma_i$ are just
points; choose a path $\tau_i$ from a fixed $x_0$ to $\sigma_i$, regard it as a
singular 1-simplex, and compute $\partial(\sum_i n_i \tau_i)$, using
$\sum_i n_i = 0$ at the last step.⟩
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium/hard — functoriality and homotopy invariance)

(a) Given $f : X \to Y$, define $f_\sharp : C_n(X) \to C_n(Y)$ and verify
$f_\sharp \partial = \partial f_\sharp$. Draw the commutative diagram and say
what commutativity means here.
(b) Prove Proposition 2.9 from (a): a chain map induces homomorphisms on
homology. Then verify the two formal properties $(fg)_* = f_* g_*$ and
$\mathbb{1}_* = \mathbb{1}$.
(c) Deduce Corollary 2.11 from Theorem 2.10 and (b): a homotopy equivalence
induces isomorphisms on all $H_n$. Then deduce that a contractible space has
$\tilde H_n = 0$ for all $n$.
(d) Corollary 2.11 answers the question Hatcher raises on p. 107 — whether
$H_n^\Delta$ depends on the chosen Δ-complex structure — but only once
Theorem 2.27 is in hand. Explain the logical route carefully: which theorem
supplies which link, and why Corollary 2.11 alone is not enough.
(e) Give the one-sentence reason Theorem 2.10 is the hard theorem of the
section while Proposition 2.9 is a formality.

*(Hatcher §2.1: chain maps and the commutative diagram, p. 111; Proposition 2.9
and the two formal properties, p. 111; Theorem 2.10 and Corollary 2.11, p. 111;
Proposition 2.12 on chain homotopy, p. 113; Theorem 2.27, p. 128)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(c) A homotopy equivalence $f$ has $g$ with $fg \simeq \mathbb{1}$ and
$gf \simeq \mathbb{1}$; apply Theorem 2.10 to both composites.
(d) $H^\Delta$ and $H$ are different functors of different inputs. You need a
bridge between them before homotopy invariance of one says anything about the
other.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Part (d) is the real exercise. Everything else in this problem is a diagram
chase. Write the route as a chain of three arrows and check that each is
justified by a named result.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(d) The route is: $H_n^\Delta(X) \cong H_n(X)$ by Theorem 2.27, for each
Δ-complex structure; $H_n$ is defined without reference to any structure and is
a homotopy invariant by Corollary 2.11; so two structures on homeomorphic — or
merely homotopy equivalent — spaces give simplicial groups isomorphic to the
same singular group, hence to each other. ⟨your step: say why Corollary 2.11
alone fails. What is Corollary 2.11 a statement about, and is $H_n^\Delta$ even
defined for a general space?⟩
(e) Proposition 2.9 needs only that $f_\sharp$ commutes with $\partial$, which
is one line of algebra. Theorem 2.10 has to convert a *continuous* homotopy
into an *algebraic* one — a chain homotopy, by way of a prism operator — and
that construction is where the topology enters.
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(a) For a singular $n$-simplex $\sigma : \Delta^n \to X$ set
$f_\sharp(\sigma) = f\sigma$, and extend linearly. Then
$f_\sharp \partial(\sigma) = f_\sharp\left(\sum_i (-1)^i \sigma|[v_0,\dots,\hat v_i,\dots,v_n]\right)
= \sum_i (-1)^i\, f\sigma|[v_0,\dots,\hat v_i,\dots,v_n] = \partial f_\sharp(\sigma)$,
where the middle equality is just the definition of $f_\sharp$ applied
term by term. ⟨your step: draw the ladder diagram and state what its
commutativity asserts.⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — what the two definitions cost, and what they buy)

(a) Hatcher gives a way of seeing singular homology as a special case of
simplicial homology: the *singular complex* $S(X)$, the Δ-complex with one
$n$-simplex for each singular $n$-simplex of $X$. Explain why
$H_n^\Delta(S(X))$ is identical to $H_n(X)$, and then say why this observation,
though correct, is not a computational tool.
(b) State Theorem 2.27. Hatcher's proof uses the long exact sequence of a pair
and excision, neither of which is developed in this unit. Without proving it,
say precisely what the theorem buys that neither definition has on its own —
list one property that simplicial homology has and singular does not, and one
the other way round.
(c) Hatcher notes on p. 107 that a simplicial complex structure on the torus
needs at least 14 triangles, 21 edges and 7 vertices, against the 2, 3, 1 of
the Δ-complex used in Example 2.3. Compute the Euler characteristic
$V - E + F$ both ways and check they agree. What does the agreement suggest,
and what would you need to prove it in general?
(d) Hatcher describes on p. 109 a geometric reading of cycles: a singular
$n$-cycle gives a map $K_\xi \to X$ from a Δ-complex that is a manifold away
from its $(n-3)$-skeleton, so that $H_1$ classes are collections of oriented
loops and $H_2$ classes are maps of closed oriented surfaces. Read his
paragraph and state exactly how far the manifold picture goes, and where it
stops. Why does he mention bordism?
(e) You now have two invariants for the same spaces. For the torus and
$\mathbb{R}P^2$, tabulate $\pi_1$ from at1-05 against $H_1$ from Problem 2.
State what $H_1$ has lost relative to $\pi_1$, and what it has gained. Then say
which of the two is computable by a machine and why.

*(Hatcher §2.1: the singular complex $S(X)$, p. 108; the geometric reading of
cycles and the remark on bordism, p. 109; the comparison of simplicial-complex
and Δ-complex sizes, p. 107; Theorem 2.27, p. 128. Part (e) is a reflection.)*

<details><summary>Nudge</summary>
(a) $S(X)$ has one simplex per continuous map $\Delta^n \to X$. Count them for
$X$ a single point, then for $X = [0,1]$.
(c) For the Δ-complex torus: $V = 1$, $E = 3$, $F = 2$.
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
(b) and (e) are the same question at two scales. Every definition in this
section is a trade between being computable and being defined for everything,
and Theorem 2.27 is the statement that you do not have to choose. Say what each
side of the trade actually is before saying that the theorem resolves it.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) By construction the Δ-complex $S(X)$ has exactly one $n$-simplex for each
singular $n$-simplex of $X$, and the face maps match the restrictions, so the
two chain complexes are the same complex written twice. It is not a tool
because $S(X)$ is, in Hatcher's phrase, an extremely large object compared with
$X$ — for $X$ a point it already has one simplex in every dimension, and for
$X = [0,1]$, a space that deformation retracts to a point, it has uncountably
many in every dimension.
(c) Δ-complex: $1 - 3 + 2 = 0$. Simplicial complex: $7 - 21 + 14 = 0$. They
agree. ⟨your step: say what invariance this suggests, and name what you would
need — a relation between the Euler characteristic and the homology groups,
which is at1-08's.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(e) Torus: $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$
— the same group, because $\pi_1$ was already abelian.
$\mathbb{R}P^2$: $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$, $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ — again
the same. ⟨your step: these two examples do not separate the invariants, so
find one that does. Take the figure eight, where at1-05 gives
$\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$; what do you expect $H_1$ to be, and what
has been lost? Then answer the computability question, using Problem 2's

</details>
