# at1-10 — The formal viewpoint: axioms and functoriality

**Module:** Algebraic Topology I · **Unit:** at1-10
**Sources:** Hatcher, *Algebraic Topology* — §2.3 "The Formal Viewpoint"
(pp. 160–165): the three axioms for a reduced homology theory on CW complexes
(p. 160); the unreduced form, the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms and the dimension
axiom, the equivalence of reduced and unreduced theories, coefficient groups,
and the uniqueness statement proved later as Theorem 4.59 (pp. 160–161);
the derivation of Mayer–Vietoris from the axioms alone (pp. 161–162);
categories, morphisms and composition, with the examples of spaces, groups,
sets, monoids, partially ordered sets, the homotopy category and chain
complexes (pp. 162–163); functors, covariance and contravariance, the list of
functors met in Chapter 2, natural transformations, and the classifying space
$B\mathcal{C}$ (pp. 163–165).

This is the closing unit of at1 and it deliberately proves almost nothing new.
Its work is to re-describe what the previous nine units built.

Interleaves at1-09, whose Mayer–Vietoris sequence is re-derived here from the
axioms with no chains at all, and at1-07, whose Theorem 2.10 turns out to say
something sharper than "homology is a functor".

Submit your written solutions via `/grade at1-10`.

## Problem 1 (easy — the axioms)

(a) State the three axioms for a reduced homology theory on CW complexes, in
full, including the naturality requirement inside axiom (2).
(b) Verify that singular reduced homology satisfies all three, naming for each
axiom the result from at1-07, at1-08 or at1-09 that supplies it.
(c) Hatcher says axiom (3) "may seem less substantial than the first two", and
that for *finite* wedge sums it can be deduced from the first two. Say why
axiom (3) is nevertheless not redundant.
(d) Describe how the unreduced axioms differ: the relative groups, the split of
axiom (2) into two parts, and what replaces the wedge sum in axiom (3).
(e) The Eilenberg–Steenrod list included a *dimension axiom*, that
$h_n(\mathrm{pt}) = 0$ for $n \neq 0$. Give Hatcher's argument that it is a
normalisation rather than a substantive constraint, and then name the theory he
cites for which it genuinely fails.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(c) Read the sentence after the one you are tempted to stop at. The word
"finite" is load-bearing.
(e) The normalisation argument is a one-line reindexing; the theory is named in
the same paragraph.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) The deduction works for finite wedge sums only. For infinite ones the first
two axioms do not imply the third, and Hatcher points to an example in the
Exercises. So axiom (3) is independent, and the appearance of being a
technicality is exactly the trap. ⟨your step: say why an axiom that is
redundant on all finite complexes is still worth stating.⟩
(e) Set $h_n(X, A) = H_{n+k}(X, A)$ for a fixed non-zero $k$. This is a
homology theory in which the dimension axiom fails, obtained by shifting
degrees and nothing else — so failure of the axiom carries no information by
itself. ⟨your step: name the theory Hatcher says was found shortly afterwards
in which the coefficients really are non-zero in infinitely many degrees.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
The interesting question in this problem is not whether singular homology
satisfies the axioms — it does, and you have already proved every case. It is
which of the axioms could be dropped and which could not. (c) and (e) are the
two places Hatcher answers that, in opposite directions.
</details>

## Problem 2 (medium — Mayer–Vietoris without chains)

(a) Reproduce Hatcher's derivation of Mayer–Vietoris from the axioms. Take
$X = A \cup B$ a CW complex with $A$, $B$ subcomplexes, and write the
commutative ladder induced by $(B, A \cap B) \hookrightarrow (X, A)$.
(b) Why are the vertical maps between the relative groups isomorphisms? The
reason is one equation between quotient spaces; give it and say which axiom
turns it into an isomorphism of groups.
(c) State the purely algebraic fact that finishes the argument — that a ladder
of exact sequences with every third vertical map an isomorphism yields a long
exact sequence in the remaining terms — and say where Hatcher leaves its proof.
(d) Compare this derivation with at1-09's. That one built a short exact
sequence of *chain complexes* and used Proposition 2.21 and barycentric
subdivision. This one uses no chains at all. Say what each derivation needs
that the other does not, and what is gained by having both.
(e) Conclude: does *every* homology theory have Mayer–Vietoris sequences?
State the answer with Hatcher's hypothesis attached, and say why the hypothesis
is there.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) $B/(A \cap B)$ and $X/A$ are the same space.
(e) Hatcher's sentence contains the phrase "at least for CW complexes".
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) Because $B/(A \cap B) = X/A$: collapsing $A \cap B$ inside $B$ and
collapsing $A$ inside $X$ produce the same quotient. Axiom (2) expresses the
relative groups in terms of the quotient, so equal quotients give isomorphic
relative groups.
(d) at1-09's derivation needs the singular chain complex and Proposition 2.21,
so it applies to arbitrary $A$, $B$ whose interiors cover, but it is available
only for a theory built from chains. This one needs only the axioms, so it
applies to *every* homology theory, but only to CW pairs. ⟨your step: say which
of the two you would use for a homology theory you had been handed as a black
box satisfying the axioms.⟩
</details>

## Problem 3 (medium — categories)

(a) Give Hatcher's definition of a category: the three pieces of data and the
three equations. Then check the definition against two of his examples in
detail — the category of topological spaces and continuous maps, and the
category of abelian groups and homomorphisms.
(b) Show that a group $G$ is the same thing as a category with a single object
in which every morphism is invertible, and that dropping invertibility gives
exactly a monoid. Be explicit about which axiom of a group corresponds to which
part of the definition of a category.
(c) Show that a partially ordered set $(X, \leq)$ is a category with objects
the elements of $X$ and at most one morphism between any two objects. Identify
what reflexivity, transitivity and antisymmetry each contribute.
(d) Describe the homotopy category. Its objects are spaces and its morphisms
are homotopy *classes* of maps; say what has to be checked for the composition
to be well defined, and check it.
(e) Give an example of a category in which the morphisms are not functions at
all, and one in which the objects are not sets.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(c) There are three conditions on a partial order and three things to account
for in a category; the correspondence is not quite one-to-one, and saying so is
part of the answer.
(d) $f_0g_0 \simeq f_1g_1$ whenever $f_0 \simeq f_1$ and $g_0 \simeq g_1$.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(c) Reflexivity $x \leq x$ supplies the identity morphism $\mathbb{1}_x$.
Transitivity supplies composition
$\mathrm{Mor}(x,y) \times \mathrm{Mor}(y,z) \to \mathrm{Mor}(x,z)$.
Antisymmetry — that $x \leq y$ and $y \leq x$ imply $x = y$ — is not needed to
have a category at all, and it is *not* what makes the morphism from $x$ to $y$
unique: that comes from the construction, since $\mathrm{Mor}(x,y)$ is a
one-element set when $x \leq y$ and empty otherwise. What antisymmetry adds
concerns the two directions together. For distinct $x$ and $y$, at most one of
$\mathrm{Mor}(x,y)$ and $\mathrm{Mor}(y,x)$ can be non-empty, so no two
distinct objects are isomorphic. Hatcher compresses this to "there is at most
one morphism between any two objects", which is right only if "between" is read
as unordered. ⟨your step: what kind of category do you get from a preorder,
where antisymmetry is dropped?⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
Part (c) is the one to spend time on, because a poset-as-category is the shape
a filtration has, and Problem 5 needs it. Everything else here is definition
chasing.
</details>

## Problem 4 (medium — functors and natural transformations)

(a) Define a functor. Then verify that $H_n$ is one, from spaces and continuous
maps to abelian groups and homomorphisms, naming the two properties of induced
maps that are exactly the two functor conditions.
(b) Define contravariant, and give Hatcher's example of the dual vector space
functor. Say which chapter of Hatcher takes up the contravariant version of
homology and what it is called.
(c) Hatcher lists the singular homology functor as a *composite*: the singular
chain complex functor followed by the algebraic homology functor. Describe both
factors, on objects and on morphisms. Then describe the further factorisation
of the first one through the category of Δ-complexes and Δ-maps.
(d) Define a natural transformation, draw the commuting square, and verify that
the change-of-coefficient homomorphisms $H_n(X; G_1) \to H_n(X; G_2)$ induced
by a homomorphism $G_1 \to G_2$ form one. You proved the required commutativity
in at1-09; say where.
(e) Theorem 2.10 from at1-07 says homotopic maps induce the same homomorphism.
Restate that as a statement about functors and the homotopy category of
Problem 3(d), and say what it adds to the bare claim that $H_n$ is a functor on
spaces.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(d) It is the preliminary observation in the proof of Lemma 2.49.
(e) A functor that sends homotopic maps to the same homomorphism does not
merely act on spaces; it fails to distinguish maps that the homotopy category
has already identified.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(a) $F(\mathbb{1}) = \mathbb{1}$ and $F(f \circ g) = F(f) \circ F(g)$ are
precisely $\mathbb{1}_* = \mathbb{1}$ and $(fg)_* = f_*g_*$, the two properties
established for induced maps in at1-07.
(e) Theorem 2.10 says $H_n$ is constant on homotopy classes, so it factors
through the homotopy category: there is a functor $\mathrm{hTop} \to
\mathrm{Ab}$ whose composite with the quotient functor
$\mathrm{Top} \to \mathrm{hTop}$ is $H_n$. ⟨your step: say what this factoring
immediately gives you about homotopy equivalences, and check it against
Corollary 2.11.⟩
</details>

## Problem 5 (hard — what functoriality does and does not deliver)

(a) Write out, as a diagram of categories and functors, the claim "$H_n$ is a
functor $\mathrm{Top} \to \mathrm{Ab}$", and add the factorisation through
$\mathrm{hTop}$ from Problem 4(e).
(b) Let $(P, \leq)$ be a partially ordered set, regarded as a category by
Problem 3(c). Say what a functor $P \to \mathrm{Top}$ consists of, concretely:
what data, and what conditions. Check that a nested family of subspaces
$X_p \subseteq X_q$ for $p \leq q$, with the inclusions, is exactly such a
functor.
(c) Now compose. Show that if $\mathcal{X} : P \to \mathrm{Top}$ is a functor
and $H_n : \mathrm{Top} \to \mathrm{Ab}$ is the homology functor, then
$H_n \circ \mathcal{X} : P \to \mathrm{Ab}$ is a functor, and describe it
concretely: a group for each $p$, and a homomorphism $H_n(X_p) \to H_n(X_q)$
whenever $p \leq q$, compatible under composition.
(d) The unit's mission strip says that homology being a functor "is what makes
persistence a functor too". Using (b) and (c), say precisely what is right
about that and what is loose about it. In particular: of the two functors whose
composite you formed in (c), which one did this module construct, and which one
did it not? Is the composite a functor *because* $H_n$ is, or for a reason that
needs both factors equally?
(e) Finally, the honest limits. Hatcher says that homology theories are in
general *not* determined by their coefficient groups, but that singular
homology is — a statement he proves only in Theorem 4.59, and only under the
dimension axiom. Say what work that theorem is doing, why the hook's slogan
"forget the construction, homology is pinned down by a handful of axioms" is
therefore an overstatement of what this unit has proved, and what would have to
be added to make it true.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>
(b) A functor out of a poset assigns an object to each element and a morphism
to each relation $p \leq q$, with the composition condition making the
triangles commute.
(d) Count the arrows. A composite needs both of them.
(e) Theorem 4.59 is in Chapter 4 and is not in this module's resources.
</details>
<details><summary>Partial</summary>
(b) A functor $P \to \mathrm{Top}$ assigns a space $X_p$ to each $p \in P$ and
a continuous map $X_p \to X_q$ to each relation $p \leq q$, such that the map
attached to $p \leq p$ is the identity and the map attached to $p \leq r$ is
the composite of those for $p \leq q$ and $q \leq r$. A nested family with
inclusions satisfies both conditions trivially, since composites of inclusions
are inclusions.
(c) Functors compose: $H_n(\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{1})) = \mathbb{1}$ and
$H_n(\mathcal{X}(f \circ g)) = H_n(\mathcal{X}(f)) \circ H_n(\mathcal{X}(g))$,
both immediate. ⟨your step: write down what the resulting object is, in words,
without using the word "persistence" — a group for every index and a map for
every comparison.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Worked start</summary>
(d) What is right: the composite $H_n \circ \mathcal{X}$ *is* a functor
$P \to \mathrm{Ab}$, and that is genuinely what a persistence module is. So the
strip is pointing at something real, and (c) proves it.

What is loose: the word "makes". A composite of two functors requires both
factors. This module built exactly one of them — $H_n : \mathrm{Top} \to
\mathrm{Ab}$ — and constructed neither the poset $P$ from a filtration
parameter nor the functor $\mathcal{X} : P \to \mathrm{Top}$. Hatcher supplies
the poset-as-category (p. 163) and the composition rule for functors (p. 163),
which is why (c) can be proved here at all; he supplies no filtration and never
uses the word. ⟨your step: complete the answer by saying which of the two
factors is tda1's, and then state what *this* unit is entitled to claim about
persistence — which is a statement about a composition, not about persistence.⟩
</details>
<details><summary>Strategy</summary>
This unit proves very little and re-describes a great deal, and (d) is where
the difference matters. The temptation is to treat "homology is a functor" as
though it explained persistence. It does not; it supplies one of two arrows.
Getting that right is worth more than any computation in the problem set,
because the same slip — taking a necessary ingredient for a sufficient reason —
is available at every module boundary in this degree.
</details>
