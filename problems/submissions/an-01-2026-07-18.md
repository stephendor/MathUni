## Problem 1 — Irrationality of (\sqrt 3)

Suppose, for contradiction, that (\sqrt 3) is rational. Then there exist integers (p,q), with (q\neq 0), such that  
$$[  
\sqrt 3=\frac pq,  
] $$ 
where (p) and (q) have no common factor.

Squaring both sides gives  
$$[  
3=\frac{p^2}{q^2},  
]  
a$$nd hence  
$$[  
p^2=3q^2.  
]
$$
Therefore $(3\mid p^2)$. We claim that this implies $(3\mid p)$. Indeed, every integer is congruent to (0), (1), or (2) modulo (3). If (3\nmid p), then  
$$[  
p\equiv 1\pmod 3  
\quad\text{or}\quad  
p\equiv 2\pmod 3,  
]  $$
so  

This contradicts $(3\mid p^2$). Hence $(3\mid p)$.

Write  
$$[  
p=3k  
]  $$
for some integer (k). Substituting this into $(p^2=3q^2)$ gives  
$$[  
9k^2=3q^2,  
] $$ 
so  
$$[  
q^2=3k^2.  
]$$

Thus $(3\mid q^2)$, and the same argument shows that $(3\mid q)$. Therefore (p) and (q) are both divisible by (3), contradicting the assumption that they have no common factor.

Hence  
$$[  
\boxed{\sqrt 3\notin\mathbb Q}.  
]$$

### Why the argument fails for (\sqrt 4)

The verbatim argument would require the implication  
$$[  
4\mid p^2\implies 4\mid p,  
]  $$
	but this is false: for example, $(p=2)$gives $(4\mid p^2)$, although $(4\nmid p)$. The argument works for (3) because (3) is prime; (4) is composite.

Even if we correctly infer only that (p) is even, writing (p=2k) gives  
$$[  
4k^2=4q^2,  
]  $$
and hence $(k^2=q^2)$, which does not imply that (q) is even and produces no contradiction.

## Problem 2 — Suprema and infima

### (a)

Let  
$$
A=\frac{n}{n+1}:n\in\mathbb N$$
We can write  
$$[  
\frac{n}{n+1}=1-\frac{1}{n+1}.  
]$$

The terms are all less than (1), but they approach (1) as $(n\to\infty)$. Thus  
$$[  
\sup A=1.  
]$$

The smallest value occurs at (n=1):  
$$[  
\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac12.  
]$$

Therefore  
$$[  
\boxed{\inf A=\frac12,\qquad \sup A=1}.  
]
$$
The infimum is attained, whereas the supremum is not.

### (b)

Let  
$$[  
B=\frac{(-1)^n}{n}:n\in\mathbb N.  
]$$

The first few terms are  
$$[  
-1,\quad \frac12,\quad -\frac13,\quad \frac14,\quad -\frac15,\ldots  
]$$

The positive terms occur when (n) is even:  
$$[  
\frac12,\frac14,\frac16,\ldots  
]  $$
so the largest positive term is $(1/2)$.

The negative terms occur when (n) is odd:  
$$[  
-1,-\frac13,-\frac15,\ldots  
]  $$
so the smallest term is $(-1)$.

Therefore  
$$[  
\boxed{\inf B=-1,\qquad \sup B=\frac12}.  
]
$$
Both the infimum and supremum are attained.

### (c)

Let  
$$[  
C=\frac1n-\frac1m:n,m\in\mathbb N.  
]$$

Since  
$$[  
0<\frac1n\leq 1  
\quad\text{and}\quad  
0<\frac1m\leq 1,  
]  $$
we have  
$$[  
-1<\frac1n-\frac1m<1.  
]$$

Thus $(1)$ is an upper bound and $(-1)$ is a lower bound.

Taking $(n=1)$, we obtain  
$$[  
1-\frac1m\longrightarrow 1  
]  $$
as $(m\to\infty)$. Hence values of $(C)$can be made arbitrarily close to $(1)$ from below, so  
$$[  
\sup C=1.  
]$$

Similarly, taking $(m=1)$, we obtain  
$$[  
\frac1n-1\longrightarrow -1  
]  $$
as $(n\to\infty)$. Hence values of $(C)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $(-1)$ from above, so  
$$[  
\inf C=-1.  
]$$

Therefore  
$$[  
\boxed{\inf C=-1,\qquad \sup C=1}.  
]
$$
Neither value is attained.

## Problem 3 — An upper bound belonging to the set

Suppose $(a)$ is an upper bound for $A$and $(a\in A)$.

Because $(a)$ is an upper bound for $(A)$,  
$$[  
x\leq a  
]  $$
for every $(x\in A).$

Since $(\sup A)$ is the least upper bound of $(A)$, and $(a)$ is an upper bound, we have  
$$[  
\sup A\leq a.  
]$$

On the other hand, $(\sup A)$ is itself an upper bound for $(A)$. Because $(a\in A)$, it follows that  
$$[  
a\leq \sup A.  
]$$

Combining the two inequalities,  
$$[  
a\leq\sup A\leq a.  
]
$$
Therefore  
$$[  
\boxed{a=\sup A}.  
]$$

Equivalently, an upper bound that belongs to the set is the maximum of the set, and every maximum is the supremum.

## Problem 4 — Greatest lower bound from completeness

Let $(A\subseteq\mathbb R)$ be nonempty and bounded below, and define  
$$[  
B={b\in\mathbb R:b\text{ is a lower bound for }A}.  
]$$

Because $(A)$ is bounded below, it has at least one lower bound. Hence  
$$[  
B\neq\varnothing.  
]$$

Choose some $(x_0\in A)$. Every $(b\in B)$ is a lower bound for (A), so  
$$[  
b\leq x_0.  
]$$

Therefore $(x_0)$ is an upper bound for $(B)$. Thus $(B)$ is nonempty and bounded above, so the Axiom of Completeness guarantees that  
$$[  
c=\sup B  
]  $$
exists.

We now show that $(c=\inf A)$.

Let $(x\in A)$. Because every $(b\in B)$ is a lower bound for $(A$),  
$$[  
b\leq x  
]  $$
for every $(b\in B)$.

Thus $(x)$ is an upper bound for $(B)$. Since $(c=\sup B)$ is the least upper bound of $(B)$,  
$$[  
c\leq x.  
]$$

This holds for every $(x\in A)$. Therefore $(c)$ is a lower bound for $(A)$, and hence  
$$[  
c\in B.  
]$$

Moreover, because $(c=\sup B)$,  
$$[  
b\leq c  
]  $$
for every $(b\in B)$. Thus $(c)$ is greater than or equal to every lower bound of $(A)$.

Consequently, $(c)$ is the greatest lower bound of $(A)$. Hence  
$$[  
\boxed{\sup B=\inf A}.  
]$$

## Problem 5 — Ruling out $(\alpha^2>2)$

Let  
$$[  
T={t\in\mathbb R:t^2<2}  
]  $$
and let  
$$[  
\alpha=\sup T.  
]$$

Suppose, for contradiction, that  
$$[  
\alpha^2>2.  
]$$

Because $(1^2<2)$, we have $(1\in T)$, and therefore  
$$[  
\alpha\geq 1>0.  
]$$

Define  
$$[  
h=\frac{\alpha^2-2}{4\alpha}.  
]$$

Because $(\alpha^2>2)$ and $(\alpha>0)$, we have  
$$[  
h>0.  
]$$

Also,  
$$[  
\alpha^2-2<\alpha^2,  
]  $$
so  
$$[  
h<\frac{\alpha^2}{4\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{4}.  
]$$

In particular,  
$$[  
0<h<\alpha,  
]  $$
and hence  
$$[
\beta=\alpha-h  
] $$
  
satisfies  
$$[  
0<\beta<\alpha.  
]$$

We now calculate $(\beta^2)$:  
$$[  
\begin{aligned}  
\beta^2  
&=(\alpha-h)^2\  
&=\alpha^2-2\alpha h+h^2.  
\end{aligned}  
]
$$
By the definition of (h),  
$$ 
2\alpha h=

2\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha^2-2}{4\alpha}\right)=

\frac{\alpha^2-2}{2}.  
$$

Therefore  
$$[  
\begin{aligned}  
\beta^2  
&=\alpha^2-\frac{\alpha^2-2}{2}+h^2\  
&=\frac{\alpha^2+2}{2}+h^2.  
\end{aligned}  
]$$

Since $(\alpha^2>2)$,  
$$[  
\frac{\alpha^2+2}{2}>2.  
]$$

Thus  
$$[  
\beta^2>2.  
]$$

We claim that $(\beta)$ is an upper bound for $(T)$. Suppose $(t\in T)$. If $(t>\beta)$, then, because $(\beta>0)$,  
$$[  
t>\beta>0.  
]$$

Squaring preserves the strict inequality for positive numbers, so  
$$[  
t^2>\beta^2>2.  
]$$

But £ means that  
$$[  
t^2<2,  
]  $$
which is a contradiction. Hence no $(t\in T)$ can satisfy $(t>\beta)$, and therefore  
$$[  
t\leq\beta  
]  $$
for every $(t\in T)$.

Thus $(\beta)$ is an upper bound for $(T)$. However,  
$$[  
\beta<\alpha,  
]  $$
contradicting the fact that $(\alpha=\sup T)$ is the least upper bound of $(T)$.

Therefore the assumption $(\alpha^2>2)$ is impossible:  
$$[  
\boxed{\alpha^2\not>2}.  
]$$

Combined with the corresponding argument ruling out $(\alpha^2<2)$, this gives  
$$[  
\boxed{\alpha^2=2}.  
]$$

## Problem 6 — Infimum of $({1/n:n\in\mathbb N})$

Let  
$$[  
S=\frac1n:n\in\mathbb N.  
]$$

We will prove that  
$$[  
\inf S=0.  
]$$

First, for every $(n\in\mathbb N)$,  
$$[  
\frac1n>0.  
]$$

Therefore  
$$[  
0\leq\frac1n  
]  $$
for every $(n\in\mathbb N)$, so $(0)$ is a lower bound for $(S)$.

It remains to show that no positive number can be a lower bound.

Let $(\varepsilon>0)$. By the Archimedean Property, there exists $(n\in\mathbb N$) such that  
$$[  
n>\frac1\varepsilon.  
]$$

Because both sides are positive, taking reciprocals reverses the inequality:  
$$[  
\frac1n<\varepsilon.  
]$$

Thus $(\varepsilon)$ is not a lower bound for $(S)$, because $(S$) contains an element $(1/n)$ that is smaller than $(\varepsilon)$.

Consequently, every positive number fails to be a lower bound. Since (0) is a lower bound, it is the greatest lower bound.

Therefore  
$$[  
\boxed{\inf\frac1n:n\in\mathbb N=0}.  
]$$

Note to self: A useful proof pattern in Problems 5 and 6 is to convert a qualitative claim into an explicit example: construct a smaller upper bound in Problem 5, and construct an element below any proposed positive lower bound in Problem 6.
